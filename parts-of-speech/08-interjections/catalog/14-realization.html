Realization, understanding, and requests for repetition#

The noises Americans make when a fact finally lands — and the ones they make when it doesn't.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

These are the cognitive interjections: instead of reporting an emotion, they report the state of your understanding. They cluster around two opposite moments — the click of comprehension (aha, oh, got it, no wonder) and the blank of missing it (huh?, come again?, sorry?) — with a small group of stalling words (wait, hold on, hmm) sitting in between while you catch up. Intonation does almost all the work in this category: huh, oh, sorry, excuse me, and pardon all mean one thing on a falling pitch and something completely different on a rising one. That is why so many entries here are written with a question mark — in this group a question mark marks rising pitch, not a literal question, and a period marks the fact settling into place.

Realization and discovery#

ah#

Pronunciation: /ɑ/ · AH Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: ahh, ahhh

A drawn-out open vowel marking the moment a piece of information lands. Lengthening it (ahh, ahhh) makes the realization feel slower and more complete, while a short clipped ah is closer to polite acknowledgment.

  1. "Ah — so you two already know each other."
  2. Ahhh, that's why the door wouldn't lock. The frame is swollen.
  3. "Ah, I see what you did. You sorted by last name."
  4. He read the note twice, said "Ah," and put it in his pocket.
  5. "Ah, right, the Thompson account. I remember now."
  6. "The pilot light went out." "Ahh. That explains the cold shower."

See also: aha, oh, I see, oh right

aha#

Pronunciation: /ɑˈhɑ/ · ah-HAH Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: a-ha, ah-ha, ahah

The classic sound of discovery — the penny dropping, usually with a note of triumph in it. It works both for solving a problem and for catching someone out.

  1. "Aha! I found the typo — line forty-two."
  2. She flipped the page and went, "Aha," under her breath.
  3. "Aha, so you're the one who's been taking my parking spot."
  4. Aha — the wire was never connected in the first place.
  5. Somewhere in the third chapter I had an aha moment about the whole plot.
  6. "Aha!" he said, pulling his keys out of the couch cushions.

See also: ah, bingo, eureka, that's it

bingo#

Pronunciation: /ˈbɪŋɡoʊ/ · BING-oh Type: secondary · Register: informal

Announces that something is exactly right or has just been found, borrowed from the game where it means your card is complete. It marks a hit: the right answer, the right guess, or the missing piece.

  1. "Is it the alternator?" "Bingo."
  2. Bingo — third drawer down, right where you said.
  3. "So the charge posted twice?" "Bingo. That's the whole problem."
  4. He tried the last password on the list and — bingo — he was in.
  5. "Bingo!" she said, when the two spreadsheets finally matched.

See also: aha, ding ding ding, that's it, there it is

called it#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɔld ɪt/ · KAWLD-it Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: called it!, I called it, totally called it

Claims credit for a prediction the moment it comes true. It is I knew it with a scoreboard attached — the point is not only that you now understand what happened but that you said it would — so it nearly always carries a little gloating.

  1. "They're getting back together." "Called it."
  2. "I called it — I said that hire wouldn't last six months."
  3. He watched the kick sail wide and muttered, "Called it."
  4. "The venue backed out two weeks before the wedding." "Called it. I told her to get it in writing."
  5. "Totally called it," she texted, with a screenshot of the message she'd sent in March.

See also: I knew it, figures, no wonder, of course

ding ding ding#

Pronunciation: /ˌdɪŋ dɪŋ ˈdɪŋ/ · ding-ding-DING Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal Variants: ding-ding-ding, ding ding ding ding

Imitates a game-show bell to announce that someone has just landed on the right answer. It is playful and a little theatrical, and it almost always follows someone else's correct guess.

  1. "Because he never actually read the contract?" "Ding ding ding."
  2. Ding ding ding — we have a winner.
  3. "Let me guess: the wifi was unplugged." "Ding ding ding ding."
  4. "She's not mad about the money, she's mad you didn't tell her." "Ding ding ding. Exactly."
  5. I said "the printer," and my coworker went "Ding ding ding" from across the room.

See also: bingo, that's it, there it is

duh#

Pronunciation: /dʌ/ · DUH Type: primary · Register: very informal (openly mocking, and insulting, when aimed at another person) Variants: duhh, doy (dated)

Marks something as blindingly obvious. Aimed at yourself — usually as oh, duh — it is harmless, the sound of realizing you missed the easy answer. Aimed at another person it is not merely dismissive: it calls them stupid, it carries a playground flavor that sounds worse coming from an adult, and it has no place at work or with anyone you are not close to.

  1. "Oh, duh. The file was in my downloads the whole time."
  2. "You have to turn it on first." "Duh. I know."
  3. Duh — I was looking at last year's calendar.
  4. "Is the store closed on Mondays?" "Duh, it says so right on the door."
  5. I spent an hour debugging before the duh moment: I never saved the file.

See also: of course, no wonder, oh right, figures

eureka#

Pronunciation: /jʊˈrikə/ · yoo-REE-kuh Type: primary · Register: neutral (in practice almost always jocular)

The cry of a major discovery, borrowed from Archimedes' Greek for "I have found it." Americans rarely use it straight — it usually carries a wink, either mocking the size of the discovery or celebrating a genuinely hard-won solution.

  1. "Eureka!" he shouted, holding up the missing sock.
  2. After three weeks of dead ends, the lab finally had its eureka moment.
  3. Eureka — if we run the two servers in parallel, the whole problem goes away.
  4. "Did you figure out the seating chart?" "Eureka. Grandma goes at the end."
  5. She wrote EUREKA in the margin next to the equation that finally worked.

See also: aha, bingo, that's it

huh (falling tone)#

Pronunciation: /hʌ/ · HUH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: hunh, huhn

Said with a flat or falling pitch, huh registers that you have just taken in something mildly surprising or newly understood. It is low-key — the sound of filing a fact away rather than reacting to it — and rising pitch turns it into a request to repeat instead.

  1. "Huh. I didn't know she used to live in Denver."
  2. He opened the box, looked inside, and said, "Huh."
  3. "It turns out the fee is waived if you just ask." "Huh. Good to know."
  4. Huh — the two signatures actually do match.
  5. "Huh," she said, in a tone that meant she would think about it later.

See also: huh? (rising tone), hmm, oh, ah

I knew it#

Pronunciation: /aɪ ˈnu ɪt/ · eye-NOO-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: knew it, I knew it!

Marks the moment a suspicion is confirmed. It carries a note of vindication and is often said half to yourself.

  1. "I knew it. The charge was for the extended warranty."
  2. I knew it — you're the one who ate the last piece of cake.
  3. "The test came back positive for the allergy." "I knew it. Every time she eats there she gets sick."
  4. "Knew it," he muttered, when the delay went up on the board.
  5. She read the last page and said, "I knew it! He was the brother all along."

See also: called it, figures, no wonder, that explains it, of course

oh#

Pronunciation: /oʊ/ · OH Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: ohh, ohhh, ohhhh

The workhorse of new information: oh marks the instant you learn something you didn't know. Lengthening it stretches the realization out, so a long ohhh means understanding arriving slowly and completely.

  1. "Oh, the meeting moved to three."
  2. "Ohhh, you meant the other Kevin. That makes a lot more sense."
  3. "Oh. I thought you already left."
  4. "It's her birthday tomorrow." "Oh! I should get a card."
  5. Oh — that's why there's no hot water. They shut it off for the repairs.
  6. "Ohhhh," she said slowly, as the diagram finally made sense.

See also: ah, oh-ho, oh right, oh yeah, huh (falling tone)

oh-ho#

Pronunciation: /oʊˈhoʊ/ · oh-HOH Type: primary · Register: informal (dated — cartoonish, and mostly heard from older speakers) Variants: oho, oh ho, oh-ho-ho

A knowing, slightly theatrical realization: you have caught on to something, often something the other person half hoped you wouldn't notice. It has a teasing edge and sounds a bit old-fashioned or cartoonish.

  1. "Oh-ho, so that's why you wanted to leave early."
  2. "Oho! Look who finally decided to show up."
  3. Oh-ho — somebody's been in the cookie jar.
  4. "He asked about you twice." "Oh-ho. Did he now."
  5. "Oh-ho-ho," my grandfather said. "Now I see what you're up to."

See also: aha, oh, I knew it

oh right#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈraɪt/ · oh-RITE Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: oh, right, oh right right right

Signals that you have just remembered or re-understood something you already knew. It is the sound of a fact coming back rather than arriving for the first time.

  1. "Oh right, you're allergic to shellfish. Let's go somewhere else."
  2. "The office is closed Monday." "Oh, right. The holiday."
  3. Oh right right right — I promised I'd pick her up at six.
  4. "Oh right, I still owe you twenty bucks."
  5. She started to ask where the key was, then said "Oh, right" and checked under the mat.

See also: oh, ah yes, oh wait, oh yeah, right

oh wait#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈweɪt/ · oh-WAYT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: oh, wait, oh wait!, oh wait wait wait

Marks a thought that arrives one beat too late — you were already talking, or already moving, when the correction landed. It is the standard American way to walk back your own sentence in real time. In writing it also sets up sarcasm, where the "realization" is something the writer knew perfectly well all along.

  1. "It's on the third floor — oh wait, they moved. Fourth."
  2. "Oh wait, I think I left my badge in the car."
  3. "Did you send it to Dana?" "Yep. Oh wait. I sent it to the other Dana."
  4. Oh wait — this isn't my coffee.
  5. "So nobody has a key?" "Oh, wait, the neighbor does."

See also: wait, oh, oh right, hold on

oh yeah#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈjɛə/ · oh-YEH Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: oh yeah!, oh yea (nonstandard)

Marks a forgotten fact coming back to you. It is the most casual of the "now I remember" markers, and with a drawn-out yeah it can also mean "now I understand what you were getting at."

  1. "Oh yeah, I saw that email this morning."
  2. "You met him at the wedding." "Oh yeah! The guy with the guitar."
  3. Oh yeah — the trash goes out tonight.
  4. "Oh yeah, I forgot you don't drink coffee."
  5. "It's the same street, it just changes names at the bridge." "Oh yeah. Now I get it."

See also: oh right, ah yes, right, I get it

that's it#

Pronunciation: /ˈðæts ɪt/ · THATS-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: that is it, that's it!

Confirms that something is exactly the thing being looked for or described — the right word, the right answer, the right item. Said to someone else it means "yes, you've got it"; said to yourself it marks the missing piece surfacing.

  1. "Was it Casablanca?" "That's it. That's the one."
  2. That's it! The word I wanted was "reciprocal."
  3. "Turn it clockwise, then push." "That's it, you've got it."
  4. She kept tasting the sauce and finally said, "That's it — nutmeg."
  5. "That's it, that's the whole trick. There's nothing else to it."

See also: there it is, bingo, aha, got it

there it is#

Pronunciation: /ˌðɛr ɪt ˈɪz/ · thair-it-IZ Type: phrase · Register: neutral

Marks the thing you were hunting for finally turning up, or a truth finally surfacing after some circling. With a resigned tone it means "and there's the catch I was waiting for."

  1. "There it is — the receipt was folded inside the manual."
  2. Scroll down one more screen. There it is.
  3. "So the promotion comes with no raise." "There it is."
  4. He waited for the fine print, and — there it is — a two-year contract.
  5. "There it is," the mechanic said, pointing at a cracked hose.

See also: that's it, bingo, ding ding ding

Registering and confirming understanding#

ah yes#

Pronunciation: /ˌɑ ˈjɛs/ · ah-YES Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: ah, yes

A slightly formal, deliberate "now I remember" — the sound of a fact being retrieved rather than discovered. In casual speech it often carries a touch of mock ceremony.

  1. "Ah yes, the Peterson file. I have it right here."
  2. "Ah, yes — you asked about this last week. Let me pull it up."
  3. "Ah yes, the famous shortcut that takes twenty minutes longer."
  4. She glanced at his name tag. "Ah yes, we spoke on the phone."
  5. "Ah, yes," he said, finally placing the face.

See also: ah, oh right, of course, I see

figures#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɪɡjɚz/ · FIG-yerz Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: that figures, it figures

Says that a new piece of information fits a pattern you already expected — usually an annoying one. It is realization with a shrug: of course this would happen.

  1. "The one day I forget my umbrella." "Figures."
  2. "He showed up an hour late." "That figures."
  3. Figures — the sale ended yesterday.
  4. "They gave the corner office to Dan." "It figures. He plays golf with the boss."
  5. "Figures the elevator would break on the day I'm carrying boxes."

See also: of course, naturally, no wonder, I knew it

got it#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡɑt ɪt/ · GOT-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: got it!, gotit

Confirms that you have understood an instruction or explanation and are ready to act on it. It is short, businesslike, and safe in almost any setting, including at work.

  1. "Second door on the left, then ask for Maria." "Got it."
  2. "Got it — I'll have the draft to you by Friday."
  3. "Don't press send until I've reviewed it." "Got it."
  4. She explained the shortcut twice before I said, "Okay, got it."
  5. "Got it, thanks. That clears things up."

See also: gotcha, I get it, I see, I'm with you, makes sense, right

gotcha#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡɑtʃə/ · GOTCH-uh Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: gotchya, gotcha!

A relaxed spoken contraction of "got you," used to say "I understand." It is friendly but casual, so it can sound too breezy in formal writing or with senior colleagues. A second, unrelated sense catches someone out: Gotcha!

  1. "The report goes to legal first, then to me." "Gotcha."
  2. "Gotcha — so it's not a bug, it's the setting."
  3. "I'm not saying no, I'm saying not this quarter." "Gotcha."
  4. "Gotcha, I'll call you after lunch."
  5. He jumped out from behind the door. "Gotcha!"

See also: got it, I see, I get it, makes sense

I get it#

Pronunciation: /aɪ ˈɡɛt ɪt/ · eye-GET-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: I get it now, now I get it

States that an explanation has finally landed — the point has gone from confusing to clear. With a flat, tired delivery it means something else entirely: "stop explaining, I understood you the first time."

  1. "I get it now — the second number is the year, not the day."
  2. "Do you see why that won't work?" "I get it."
  3. "Oh, I get it. It's a pun."
  4. "I get it, I get it. You don't have to keep saying it."
  5. She walked me through the diagram until I said, "Okay, I get it."

See also: got it, I see, makes sense, gotcha

I see#

Pronunciation: /aɪ ˈsi/ · eye-SEE Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: ah, I see, oh, I see

A calm, slightly formal acknowledgment that you now understand. Depending on tone it ranges from genuine comprehension to a cool, noncommittal "noted" that signals you are not thrilled.

  1. "I see. So the deposit isn't refundable."
  2. "Ah, I see — the two accounts have the same name."
  3. "He decided not to come." "I see."
  4. "I see what you're saying, but the numbers don't support it."
  5. The doctor looked at my chart and said "I see," which told me nothing.

See also: got it, I get it, ah, oh, makes sense

I'm with you#

Pronunciation: /aɪm ˈwɪð ju/ · eyem-WITH-yoo Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: I'm with you so far, with you, I'm following you

Tells the speaker you are still tracking the explanation and they should keep going. Unlike got it, it does not claim the whole thing has landed — it marks how far you have gotten, which is why so far is attached to it so often.

  1. "I'm with you so far. Keep going."
  2. "Is any of this landing?" "I'm with you."
  3. "I'm with you right up to the part where a second invoice shows up."
  4. He nodded through the first two slides — "With you" — and then stopped nodding.
  5. "I'm following you, but I don't see why that moves the deadline."

See also: got it, I get it, I see, makes sense, you lost me

makes sense#

Pronunciation: /ˌmeɪks ˈsɛns/ · makes-SENSE Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: that makes sense, makes sense to me

Accepts an explanation as reasonable and coherent. It confirms understanding and mild agreement at once, which makes it one of the safest responses in American workplace English.

  1. "We pushed the launch so it wouldn't collide with the holiday." "Makes sense."
  2. "That makes sense — no wonder the file was so big."
  3. "Makes sense. I'd probably do the same thing."
  4. "They charge more for the smaller size because of the packaging." "Huh. Makes sense, actually."
  5. "Makes sense to me, but let's run it by Dana."

See also: got it, I see, that tracks, of course, right

naturally#

Pronunciation: /ˈnætʃɚəli/ · NATCH-er-uh-lee Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: naturally!

Marks something as entirely expected, the obvious consequence of everything that came before. Said dryly — which is most of the time — it is a close cousin of figures, with a sigh built into it.

  1. "And naturally, the printer jammed five minutes before the deadline."
  2. "Did they charge you a fee?" "Naturally."
  3. "Naturally he blamed the intern."
  4. "The flight was delayed?" "Naturally. It's Chicago in January."
  5. "Naturally," she said, as the only open lane closed just as she reached it.

See also: of course, figures, no wonder

no wonder#

Pronunciation: /noʊ ˈwʌndɚ/ · no-WUN-der Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: no wonder!, small wonder

Announces that something puzzling has just been explained: the new fact accounts for what had you confused. It usually attaches directly to the thing it explains.

  1. "No wonder the room was freezing — the window was open all night."
  2. "He hasn't slept since Tuesday." "No wonder he looks like that."
  3. No wonder I couldn't find it. I was searching the wrong folder.
  4. "The recipe calls for a full cup of salt?" "No wonder it was inedible."
  5. "No wonder," she said, once she saw the flat tire.

See also: that explains it, figures, of course, ah

of course#

Pronunciation: /əv ˈkɔrs/ · uhv-KORSS Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: 'course, of course!

In this use, the sound of something obvious clicking into place — you should have seen it, and now you do. Aimed at a frustration rather than a discovery, it turns sarcastic.

  1. "Of course! The password is her birthday."
  2. "It's the same building, just a different entrance." "Of course. I feel silly."
  3. "Of course — you have to unlock it before it will charge."
  4. "Of course the coffee shop is closed the one morning I actually need it."
  5. "'Course," he said, snapping his fingers. "The battery's in backwards."

See also: naturally, figures, duh, right, no wonder

right#

Pronunciation: /raɪt/ · RITE Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: right, right, right right right

A quick marker that a fact has been received and slotted into place — halfway between "I understand" and "I remember now." Doubled or tripled, it hurries the speaker along: I'm with you, keep going.

  1. "The train leaves from track nine, not track one." "Right."
  2. "Right, right — she's the one who does the scheduling."
  3. "You have to log out first." "Right, I always forget that."
  4. "Right," he said, nodding. "So we start with the survey."
  5. "It's a two-hour drive without traffic." "Right right right. We should leave at six."

See also: oh right, got it, I see, makes sense

say no more#

Pronunciation: /ˌseɪ noʊ ˈmɔr/ · say-no-MOR Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: say no more!, no need to say more

Cuts an explanation short because you have already understood — and, usually, already agreed. It is warm and a little jokey, and the implication is that the rest of the explanation would be wasted breath.

  1. "I'd come, but I've got both kids this weekend—" "Say no more."
  2. "Say no more, I'll have it on your desk by noon."
  3. "It's the place with the green awning, right next to the—" "Say no more. I know it."
  4. "He wants to circle back and align on the roadmap." "Say no more."
  5. She held up the empty coffee pot. "Say no more," he said, and reached for his keys.

See also: got it, gotcha, I get it, makes sense

that explains it#

Pronunciation: /ˌðæt ɪkˈspleɪnz ɪt/ · that-ik-SPLAYNZ-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: well, that explains it, that explains a lot

Says that a new fact resolves something you had been wondering about. It is no wonder in sentence form, and it usually comes with audible relief at no longer being confused.

  1. "She's been out sick all week." "That explains it. I thought she was ignoring my emails."
  2. "Well, that explains it — the clock was an hour behind."
  3. "They repaved the whole street yesterday." "That explains a lot."
  4. "That explains it. I was reading the wrong column the entire time."
  5. "He grew up in New Orleans." "That explains the accent."

See also: no wonder, figures, makes sense, that tracks, oh

that tracks#

Pronunciation: /ˌðæt ˈtræks/ · that-TRAKS Type: phrase · Register: informal (recent — widespread since the late 2010s) Variants: tracks, that checks out, checks out

Says that a new fact fits everything you already knew about the person or the situation. It is a younger, drier relative of that makes sense, and it usually lands somewhere between agreement and a raised eyebrow.

  1. "He quit and moved to Portland." "That tracks."
  2. "Tracks," she said. "He's been threatening to leave since March."
  3. "The recipe is from a guy who hates cilantro." "Well, that checks out."
  4. "Apparently the outage started when somebody unplugged the wrong thing." "Yeah, that tracks."
  5. "That tracks — she doesn't answer email on Fridays either."

See also: makes sense, figures, naturally, of course

Pausing to process#

hang on#

Pronunciation: /ˌhæŋ ˈɑn/ · hang-ON Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hang on a second, hang on a sec, hang on a minute

Stops the conversation while you catch up, check something, or notice that a detail doesn't add up. It is the friendliest member of the wait family and is also the standard way to ask someone to hold on the phone.

  1. "Hang on — you said the deadline was the fifteenth?"
  2. "Hang on a sec, I've got another call."
  3. Hang on. If he left at noon, he should have been here by now.
  4. "Hang on, let me find a pen."
  5. "Hang on a minute, that's not what the invoice says."

See also: hold on, hold up, wait, wait a minute

hmm#

Pronunciation: /m̩ː/ · HMM — not really a word: a sustained nasal hum made with the lips shut. There is no vowel in it, and the h in the spelling is not pronounced. Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: hm, hmmm, hmmmm

A closed-mouth hum meaning you are thinking it over — weighing, doubting, or not quite convinced. The longer the hum, the more genuine the deliberation; a short hm usually signals mild skepticism instead.

  1. "Hmm. That price seems high for a used one."
  2. "What do you think of the blue?" "Hmmm. Let me see it in better light."
  3. "Hm, I don't remember agreeing to that."
  4. She looked at the map, said "Hmm," and turned it sideways.
  5. "Hmm — if we move the meeting to Thursday, that actually works."

See also: huh (falling tone), wait, hm?, ah

hold on#

Pronunciation: /ˌhoʊld ˈɑn/ · hold-ON Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: hold on a second, hold on a sec, hold on now

Asks the other person to stop so you can think, check, or object. It is a beat firmer than hang on and is the usual way to flag that what you just heard doesn't square with what you knew.

  1. "Hold on — did you say she already signed it?"
  2. "Hold on a second, I want to write this down."
  3. "Hold on. Let me back up and start over."
  4. "So we're paying for both?" "Hold on now, nobody said that."
  5. She was halfway out the door when she stopped. "Hold on. I think I left the stove on."

See also: hang on, hold up, wait, hold the phone, back up

hold the phone#

Pronunciation: /ˌhoʊld ðə ˈfoʊn/ · hold-thuh-FOHN Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hold the phone!

A playful, emphatic "stop everything," used when what you just heard is surprising enough to need a second look. It is deliberately a little corny and belongs in casual conversation.

  1. "Hold the phone — you got the job?"
  2. "Hold the phone. Since when do you cook?"
  3. He put down his fork. "Hold the phone. That's the same guy from the commercial."
  4. "They're giving everyone Friday off." "Hold the phone. Everyone?"
  5. "Hold the phone! You two are related?"

See also: hold on, hold up, wait, wait a minute

hold up#

Pronunciation: /ˌhoʊld ˈʌp/ · hold-UP Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hold up now

A casual, spoken "wait a second," used to pause someone mid-sentence when you need to catch up or push back. It is everywhere in relaxed American speech, especially among younger speakers, and it is unremarkable between colleagues — it is only a formal presentation or a first meeting with a client where it would sound too loose.

  1. "Hold up — you drove all the way there and they were closed?"
  2. "Hold up, let me tie my shoe."
  3. "Hold up now, that's not how I heard it."
  4. "Hold up. Say that part about the refund again."
  5. "And then she quit." "Hold up, she quit?"

See also: hold on, hang on, wait, wait, what?

wait#

Pronunciation: /weɪt/ · WAYT Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: wait wait wait, waaait

Stops the flow of conversation the instant something stops making sense — or the instant something clicks. As a realization marker it introduces the thought that has just arrived.

  1. "Wait. You're telling me this now?"
  2. "Wait wait wait — go back to the part about the money."
  3. "Wait, I know that song."
  4. "Wait, if the store closes at six, we'll never make it."
  5. He stopped in the doorway. "Wait. Did I lock the car?"

See also: wait a minute, oh wait, hold on, hang on, wait, what?, hold up

wait a minute#

Pronunciation: /ˌweɪt ə ˈmɪnɪt/ · wayt-uh-MIN-it Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: wait a second, wait a sec, waitaminute

A longer, more emphatic wait, used when something doesn't add up or when a suspicion suddenly takes shape. It usually introduces the objection or the discovery that follows it.

  1. "Wait a minute — this receipt is from last March."
  2. "Wait a second, weren't you supposed to be in Denver?"
  3. "Wait a minute. I thought you said he was an only child."
  4. "Wait a sec, I think I've been on this website before."
  5. "Wait a minute," she said slowly. "You planned this."

See also: wait, hold on, hang on, hold the phone

Requests for repetition and clarification#

back up#

Pronunciation: /ˌbæk ˈʌp/ · back-UP Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: back up a second, back it up

Asks the speaker to return to an earlier point and go over it again — not to repeat the last word, but to re-tell the part you lost. It is casual and equally at home in conversation and in meetings.

  1. "Back up — who told you that?"
  2. "Back up a second. You lost me at the part about the lease."
  3. "Okay, back up. Start from when you got to the airport."
  4. She stopped him mid-story. "Back it up — I missed the part about the dog."
  5. "Back up. Two hundred dollars for what?"

See also: hold on, run that by me again, you lost me, one more time, say again

beg your pardon#

Pronunciation: /ˌbɛɡ jɚ ˈpɑrdən/ · beg-yer-PAR-dun Type: phrase · Register: formal Variants: I beg your pardon, beg pardon

A formal, old-fashioned request to have something repeated. Said with a falling tone instead of a rising one, the same phrase becomes an expression of offense — "how dare you" — so intonation does all the work here.

  1. "I beg your pardon? I didn't catch the last name."
  2. "Beg your pardon — could you repeat the room number?"
  3. "I beg your pardon?" the elderly gentleman said, leaning closer to hear.
  4. "You people are all the same." "I beg your pardon."
  5. "Beg pardon, ma'am, did you say aisle nine?"

See also: pardon?, pardon me?, excuse me?, come again

come again#

Pronunciation: /ˌkʌm əˈɡɛn/ · kum-uh-GEN Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: come again?

A casual "say that again," usually because what you heard was surprising or hard to believe rather than simply inaudible. It is friendly and a little folksy.

  1. "Come again? I don't think I heard that right."
  2. "He's moving to Alaska." "Come again?"
  3. "Come again — how much did they want for it?"
  4. "Come again?" the cashier said over the noise of the blender.
  5. "I said I'll pay for dinner." "Come again? Say that into the microphone."

See also: say again, say what, run that by me again, what?

eh?#

Pronunciation: /eɪ/ or /ɛ/ · AY or EH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: ay?, eh

A short, rising grunt asking someone to repeat themselves, heard most often from older speakers or from someone who genuinely didn't hear. Americans also use eh as a tag seeking agreement, and they associate that tag use strongly with Canadians.

  1. "Eh? Speak up, I've got the water running."
  2. "Grandpa, your phone is ringing." "Eh?"
  3. "Eh? What was that last part?"
  4. He cupped a hand behind his ear. "Eh?"
  5. "Not bad for a first try, eh?"

See also: huh? (rising tone), hm?, what?, pardon?

excuse me?#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈskjuz mi/ · ik-SKYOOZ-mee Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: 'scuse me?, excuse me!

With rising intonation, a polite request to have something repeated. With a flat or sharp delivery it means the opposite of polite — it challenges what was just said — so intonation is the entire difference between courtesy and confrontation.

  1. "Excuse me? I couldn't hear you over the music."
  2. "Excuse me, did you say gate twelve or gate twenty?"
  3. "Excuse me?! You did what with my car?"
  4. "That'll be sixty dollars." "Excuse me? The sign said forty."
  5. "'Scuse me? I missed your name."

See also: pardon me?, sorry?, beg your pardon, come again

hm?#

Pronunciation: /m̩/ · HM — the same lips-shut nasal hum as hmm, but short and on rising pitch. No vowel, no consonant release. Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: hmm?, mm?, m?

A closed-mouth hum on a rising tone, meaning "what?" or "did you say something?" It is the sound you make when you were only half listening, and it is soft enough to be inoffensive.

  1. "Hm? Sorry, I was reading."
  2. "Can you grab the door?" "Hm? Oh — yeah, sure."
  3. "Hmm? What did you say your name was?"
  4. She looked up from her phone. "Hm?"
  5. "I asked if you wanted the last slice." "Mm? Oh, no thanks."

See also: hmm, huh? (rising tone), what?, sorry?

how's that?#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊz ˈðæt/ · howz-THAT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: how's that again?, how was that?

A mild request to repeat or restate something, common in casual American speech and often used when the content rather than the volume was the problem. It has a second, unrelated use asking whether something is satisfactory.

  1. "How's that? I couldn't hear you with the mixer going."
  2. "How's that again? The third what?"
  3. "It's forty dollars a month, plus tax." "How's that?"
  4. "The tickets are cheaper if we drive." "How's that? Parking alone is thirty dollars."
  5. He adjusted the mirror for her. "How's that?"

See also: come again, say again, what was that?, pardon?

huh? (rising tone)#

Pronunciation: /hʌ/ · HUH Type: primary · Register: very informal (blunt — fine with friends and family, rude with a stranger, a customer, or a boss) Variants: hunh?, huh

The most basic American request to have something repeated: a short grunt with rising pitch. It is extremely common in speech but blunt, so it can sound rude to a stranger, a customer, or a boss — sorry? or excuse me? is the safer choice there.

  1. "Huh? I didn't catch that."
  2. "Your keys are on the counter." "Huh?"
  3. "Huh? Say it again, the fan's on."
  4. "Huh?" he said, pulling out one earbud.
  5. "I think it's your turn." "Huh? Oh — sorry."

See also: huh (falling tone), what?, hm?, sorry?, eh?

let me get this straight#

Pronunciation: /ˌlɛt mi ɡɛt ðɪs ˈstreɪt/ · let-mee-get-this-STRAYT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: so let me get this straight, let me get this right, let me make sure I've got this

Announces that you are about to repeat back what you just heard, because you want it confirmed before you react. It is a request for clarification in form only — nearly every time, the recap is a runway for an objection.

  1. "Let me get this straight — you paid extra to check a bag you didn't bring?"
  2. "So let me get this straight, the refund goes back to a card that expired in April?"
  3. "Let me get this straight. She knew the whole time and said nothing."
  4. "Let me make sure I've got this: deposit first, then the inspection, then the keys."
  5. He set down his pen. "Let me get this straight. You want all of it by Friday."

See also: run that by me again, back up, wait a minute, hold on

one more time#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌn mɔr ˈtaɪm/ · wun-mor-TIME Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: one more time?, just one more time

Asks for a repetition, usually of something you are trying to get exactly right — a name, a number, a set of directions. It implies you have already heard it and want it once more to be sure.

  1. "One more time — is it 'ei' or 'ie'?"
  2. "One more time, please. Area code first."
  3. "Third left after the light." "One more time? I want to write it down."
  4. "Can you play the chorus one more time? I almost have it."
  5. "Just one more time and I promise I've got it."

See also: say again, run that by me again, come again, back up

pardon?#

Pronunciation: /ˈpɑrdən/ · PAR-dun Type: secondary · Register: formal Variants: pardon me?, beg pardon?

A short, polite request to have something repeated. It is far more common in British English; Americans hear it as noticeably formal or old-fashioned, and it turns up mostly from older speakers or in careful service-industry politeness.

  1. "Pardon? I didn't quite hear you."
  2. "Pardon? Was that a seven or an eleven?"
  3. "The doctor will see you at four." "Pardon?"
  4. "Pardon, could you say the street name again?"
  5. She leaned in. "Pardon? It's very loud in here."

See also: pardon me?, beg your pardon, sorry?, excuse me?

pardon me?#

Pronunciation: /ˈpɑrdən mi/ · PAR-dun-mee Type: phrase · Register: formal Variants: pardon me!

A polite request for repetition, a step more emphatic than plain pardon? Like excuse me?, it doubles as an apology for a small offense and, with a hard tone, as an objection.

  1. "Pardon me? I don't think I heard the total."
  2. "Pardon me, did you say Wednesday or Thursday?"
  3. "Pardon me? Could you repeat the question?"
  4. "He said you'd handle it." "Pardon me? Nobody asked me."
  5. "Pardon me," she said to the clerk, "was that gate B or gate D?"

See also: pardon?, excuse me?, beg your pardon, sorry?

run that by me again#

Pronunciation: /ˌrʌn ðæt baɪ mi əˈɡɛn/ · run-that-by-me-uh-GEN Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: run that by me one more time, run that past me again

Asks for a full re-explanation rather than a repeated word: you followed the sounds but not the sense. It is common in American workplaces and often carries mild skepticism about what you just heard.

  1. "Run that by me again — we're paying them to take it?"
  2. "Slow down and run that by me one more time."
  3. "Run that past me again. Who approved the change?"
  4. "Run that by me again, because it sounded like you said Tuesday."
  5. "Okay, run that by me again and I'll take notes this time."

See also: back up, let me get this straight, say again, one more time, come again

say again#

Pronunciation: /ˌseɪ əˈɡɛn/ · say-uh-GEN Type: phrase · Register: neutral (standard radio and military procedure); informal and brisk in ordinary speech Variants: say that again, say again?

A clipped request to repeat, borrowed from radio and military usage where it is the standard call. In everyday speech the fuller say that again is more common; the bare say again sounds brisk and slightly official.

  1. "Say again? You're breaking up."
  2. "Say that again — I want to make sure I heard you right."
  3. "Bravo Two, say again, over."
  4. "Say again, I was on the other line."
  5. "He's retiring in June." "Say that again?"

See also: come again, one more time, run that by me again, what was that?

say what#

Pronunciation: /ˌseɪ ˈwʌt/ · say-WUT Type: phrase · Register: very informal (dated — it has an unmistakable 1980s ring) Variants: say whaaat, say what now, say what?!

A slangy, disbelieving "what did you just say," used when the content is startling rather than inaudible. It came into general American slang from African American English and peaked in the 1980s, so younger speakers now use it half-ironically. It is playful and casual, and it would land badly in a formal setting.

  1. "Say what? He proposed already?"
  2. "They cancelled the whole season." "Say what?!"
  3. "Say what now? You want me to drive to Ohio tonight?"
  4. "Say whaaat. No way she said that."
  5. "I put in my two weeks." "Say what? Since when?"

See also: come again, what?, wait, what?, huh? (rising tone)

sorry?#

Pronunciation: /ˈsɑri/ or /ˈsɔri/ · SAH-ree or SOR-ee Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: I'm sorry?, sorry, what?

With rising intonation, a polite request to have something repeated — the most common courteous option in American English. The same word on a falling tone is an apology, and a sharp, clipped sorry? registers disbelief or objection.

  1. "Sorry? I didn't hear the last part."
  2. "Sorry, what? There was a truck going by."
  3. "The total comes to ninety." "I'm sorry? Ninety?"
  4. "Sorry? Could you spell that for me?"
  5. "Sorry, what did you say your name was?"

See also: excuse me?, pardon?, huh? (rising tone), what?

wait, what?#

Pronunciation: /ˌweɪt ˈwʌt/ · wayt-WUT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: wait what, waitwhat, wait — what?

Combines a stop signal with a request to repeat, used when what was just said doesn't compute. It is one of the most common American reactions to genuinely surprising news, in speech and online alike.

  1. "Wait, what? You're moving to Tokyo?"
  2. "The wedding's off." "Wait — what? Since when?"
  3. "Wait, what? I thought the test was Friday."
  4. She read the text twice. "Wait what."
  5. "So then his brother bought the company." "Wait, what? Back up."

See also: wait, say what, what?, hold up, back up

what?#

Pronunciation: /wʌt/ or /wɑt/ · WUT Type: secondary · Register: informal (curt — reads as impatient or plainly rude to a stranger, a customer, or a boss) Variants: whut, wut, what?!

The blunt request to repeat something. It is very common but abrupt, and it is blunter than it looks on the page: to a stranger, a customer, or a boss it reads as impatient at best and rude at worst, and on a sharp falling tone it stops being a question and becomes a challenge. Sorry? is the safe substitute in all of those situations.

  1. "What? I can't hear you from in here."
  2. "Marcus." "What?" "Come look at this."
  3. "Your car's being towed." "What?!"
  4. "What, you didn't get my message?"
  5. He shouted "What?" over the sound of the mower.

See also: huh? (rising tone), sorry?, say what, wait, what?, what do you mean?, come again

what do you mean?#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt də jə ˈmin/ · wut-duh-yuh-MEEN (said carefully: /ˌwʌt du ju ˈmin/) Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: what do you mean by that?, whaddya mean, how do you mean?

Asks for the sense of something rather than a repeat of the sound: you heard every word and they still didn't add up. Said flat and hard it stops being a request and becomes a challenge, and the longer what do you mean by that? is almost always that second thing.

  1. "What do you mean, it's not covered? I've had the plan for six years."
  2. "What do you mean? You already talked to her?"
  3. "It's complicated." "How do you mean?"
  4. "Whaddya mean you're not going? You bought the ticket."
  5. "She's a lot." "What do you mean by that?"

See also: run that by me again, come again, you lost me, back up

what was that?#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt wəz ˈðæt/ · wut-wuz-THAT Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: what was that again?, what'd you say?

Asks for a repetition of something you didn't catch, usually because of noise or distraction. It is softer than a bare what? and works comfortably in most settings.

  1. "What was that? The train was going by."
  2. "What was that again? The last four digits?"
  3. "What was that? You cut out for a second."
  4. "I said dinner's ready." "What was that?"
  5. "What'd you say? My headphones were in."

See also: say again, how's that?, sorry?, one more time

you lost me#

Pronunciation: /ju ˈlɔst mi/ · yoo-LAWST-mee Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: you've lost me, I'm lost, you lost me at ___

Admits that you stopped following partway through, and usually names the spot where it happened. It puts the fault on the explanation rather than on the listener, which makes it a gentler interruption than a bare what? — though delivered dryly, you lost me at ___ is a polite way to call something nonsense.

  1. "You lost me at 'reverse amortization.'"
  2. "Okay, you lost me. Can you start over?"
  3. "Is this making sense so far?" "Honestly, you lost me about two slides ago."
  4. "I'm lost. Which one is the blue wire?"
  5. He tried the analogy a second time and I still had to say, "You've lost me."

See also: back up, run that by me again, I'm with you, what do you mean?
