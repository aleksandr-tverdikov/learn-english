# Agreement, affirmation, and backchanneling

> The words Americans use to say *yes*, to signal that they agree with what was just said, and to prove out loud that they are still listening.

This is the largest and most everyday family of interjections in American English, and it splits into three jobs that shade into one another. Some of these words **answer** a question (*yes, yep, sure, absolutely*); some **endorse** a statement someone else made (*exactly, totally, word, true*); and some are **backchannels** — the small noises and receipts a listener drops into the gaps (*uh-huh, mm-hmm, oh, really*) that mean nothing more than "I'm here, keep going." The last group is the one learners most often skip, and silence where an American expects a backchannel reads as boredom, disagreement, or a bad phone connection.

Two quirks run through the whole category. First, **register spread is enormous**: *indeed* and *word* mean nearly the same thing and could not be less interchangeable. Second, several of the most common items are not really spellings of words at all. *Mm*, *mm-hmm*, and *hm* are hums made with the lips closed; *uh-huh* and *aha* are open-mouthed grunts. None of them have a fixed vowel to spell, their meaning lives almost entirely in the pitch contour, and a flat *mm-hmm* can mean the opposite of an enthusiastic one. Punctuation is light: these words take a comma when they lead into a sentence (*Yeah, I saw that*) and a period or exclamation point when they stand alone.

## Plain yes and its everyday variants

### aight

**Pronunciation:** /aɪt/ &middot; *ITE* — also two syllables, /əˈaɪt/ &middot; *uh-ITE*, with a small break between the two vowels
**Русский:** лады, ага — очень разг., афроам. англ.; стяжённое alright
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *a'ight*, *ight*, *awight*

A clipped spoken form of *alright*, strongly associated with African American English and with casual urban speech generally. It works as agreement, as an acknowledgment, and as a check-in question (*You aight?*), and it sounds badly out of place in any professional setting.

1. "You want me to grab you a plate?" "*Aight*, thanks."
2. *Aight*, I'm heading out — text me when you land.
3. "We're meeting at seven instead of six." "*Aight*, cool."
4. "*Aight*, *aight*, I hear you. I'll stop bringing it up."
5. He just nodded and said "*a'ight*" and went back to his game.

**See also:** [alright](#alright), [okay](#okay), [bet](#bet)

### alright

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈraɪt/ &middot; *awl-RITE*
**Русский:** ладно, хорошо — разг. согласие, а в начале реплики — переход «так, дальше»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *all right* (the traditional two-word spelling, still preferred in edited prose)

A general-purpose yes that also works as a transition. As agreement it is warmer than *fine* and less committed than *absolutely*; at the front of a turn it often just means "I'm moving us along now."

1. "Can you be there by nine?" "*Alright*, nine works."
2. *Alright*, everybody, let's get started.
3. "I'll pay you back Friday." "*All right*, but don't forget again."
4. *Alright*, fair point — I hadn't thought about the shipping cost.
5. "*Alright*!" he said, clapping his hands. "Who's driving?"

**See also:** [aight](#aight), [okay](#okay), [fine](#fine), [sounds good](#sounds-good)

### okay

**Pronunciation:** /oʊˈkeɪ/ &middot; *oh-KAY* — often reduced to /əˈkeɪ/ &middot; *uh-KAY* or clipped to /keɪ/ &middot; *KAY*
**Русский:** окей, ладно — самое универсальное согласие, уместно где угодно
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *OK*, *O.K.*, *ok*, *'kay*, *k* (text only), *kk* (text only), *okey-dokey* (jokey)

The most widely usable agreement token in American English: it fits a boardroom, a text message, and a hospital form. It accepts, it acknowledges, and — with falling intonation and a pause — it signals a shift to the next thing. In texting, a bare *k* often reads as cold or annoyed, so people write *ok* or *kk* instead.

1. "Meeting's been pushed to Thursday." "*Okay*, I'll update the calendar."
2. *Okay*, so where were we?
3. "Don't touch anything until I get back." "*Okay*, *okay*."
4. She texted back a single "*ok*" and I spent an hour wondering what I'd done.
5. "*Okey-dokey*," my grandfather said, "let's see what's under the hood."

**See also:** [alright](#alright), [sure](#sure), [got it](#got-it), [fine](#fine)

### sure

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʊr/ &middot; *SHOOR*
**Русский:** конечно, ага — лёгкое «да» на просьбу; протянутое suuure — саркастичное недоверие
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *sure, sure* (impatient), *suuure* (sarcastic, in writing)

An easy, low-effort yes, most natural as an answer to a request or an offer. Tone does all the work: a bright *sure* is genuinely willing, a flat one is grudging, and a drawn-out *suuure* means the speaker does not believe a word of it.

1. "Could you cover my shift Saturday?" "*Sure*, no problem."
2. "Want some coffee?" "*Sure*, thanks."
3. "Is it okay if I bring my roommate?" "*Sure*, the more the merrier."
4. "I was going to call you." "*Suuure* you were."
5. "*Sure*, *sure*, whatever you say," he muttered, clearly done arguing.

**See also:** [sure thing](#sure-thing), [okay](#okay), [of course](#of-course), [you bet](#you-bet)

### sure thing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃʊr ˈθɪŋ/ &middot; *shoor-THING*
**Русский:** не вопрос, без проблем — разг. бодрое «да» и ответ на «спасибо»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A friendly, upbeat yes to a request, and the standard cheerful American answer to *thank you*. It is the sort of thing waiters, coworkers, and neighbors say; it would be too breezy in a formal reply to a client.

1. "Can you send me that file when you get a chance?" "*Sure thing*."
2. "Thanks for holding the elevator." "*Sure thing*."
3. "Mind if I borrow your charger?" "*Sure thing*, it's in my bag."
4. "*Sure thing*, boss — I'll have it on your desk by noon."
5. "*Sure thing*," the guy behind the counter said, and had it boxed up before I finished the sentence.

**See also:** [sure](#sure), [you got it](#you-got-it), [you bet](#you-bet), [will do](#will-do)

### yeah

**Pronunciation:** /jɛə/ &middot; *YEH* — also /jæ/ &middot; *YAA*
**Русский:** ага, да — обычное разговорное «да»; удвоенное — нетерпеливое «да-да-да»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yeh*, *yah*, *ya*, *yeahhh*, *yeah yeah yeah*

The default spoken *yes* in American English — so ordinary that a full *yes* in casual talk can sound stiff or pointed. **1.** Plain agreement or affirmation. **2.** Doubled or tripled (*yeah, yeah, yeah*), it turns impatient: "I've heard this already, move on."

1. "Are you coming to the thing tonight?" "*Yeah*, I'll be there around eight."
2. *Yeah*, that's exactly what I was thinking.
3. "You've seen this movie, right?" "*Yeah*, like four times."
4. "*Yeah*, *yeah*, *yeah* — I know, I should have checked the oil."
5. "Did it hurt?" "*Yeah*, more than I expected, honestly."

**See also:** [yes](#yes), [yep](#yep), [oh yeah](#oh-yeah), [hell yeah](#hell-yeah)

### yep

**Pronunciation:** /jɛp/ &middot; *YEP* — the *yup* spelling is /jʌp/ &middot; *YUP*
**Русский:** ага, угу — короткое чёткое «да», вопрос закрыт
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yup*, *yep yep*, *yuppers* (jokey)

A crisp, closed-off yes, ending in a popped *p*. It confirms efficiently and often signals that the speaker considers the matter settled — which can read as brisk or slightly cold if the other person wanted more.

1. "Is this the line for tickets?" "*Yep*."
2. "So you already sent it?" "*Yup*, this morning."
3. "*Yep*, that's the one," she said, pointing at the blue folder.
4. "You're sure you locked the door?" "*Yep*. Checked twice."
5. "Sounds like a long drive." "*Yep*. Nine hours, no stops."

**See also:** [yeah](#yeah), [yes](#yes), [uh-huh](#uh-huh)

### yes

**Pronunciation:** /jɛs/ &middot; *YES*
**Русский:** да — полное, весомое утверждение: официально, точно или с нажимом
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *yesss* (drawn out, enthusiastic), *yes sir* / *yes ma'am* (deferential or Southern), *yaas* (playful, internet)

The full, unreduced affirmative. In American speech it carries weight: people use it for formal answers, for emphasis, and when a casual *yeah* would sound too loose — under oath, in an interview, when correcting a misunderstanding, or when they really mean it.

1. "Do you swear to tell the truth?" "*Yes*, I do."
2. "*Yes*, I'm absolutely certain. I saw the email myself."
3. "Would you like the receipt?" "*Yes*, please."
4. "Are you saying we're over budget?" "*Yes*. By about nine thousand."
5. "*Yes* sir, I'll take care of it first thing."

**See also:** [yeah](#yeah), [yep](#yep), [certainly](#certainly), [absolutely](#absolutely)

### you bet

**Pronunciation:** /juˈbɛt/ &middot; *yoo-BET* — *you betcha* is /juˈbɛtʃə/ &middot; *yoo-BECH-uh*
**Русский:** ещё бы, а то как же — разг. сердечное «да», также ответ на «спасибо»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *you betcha*, *betcha*, *you bet your life*, *you bet I am*

A hearty American yes with a Midwestern and Western flavor. It answers a question with enthusiasm and, like *sure thing*, doubles as a warm reply to *thank you*. *You betcha* is folksier still and can sound like a deliberate impression of the Upper Midwest.

1. "Will you be at the game Sunday?" "*You bet*."
2. "Thanks for all your help today." "*You bet*, anytime."
3. "Is the fishing any good up there?" "*You betcha*. Best walleye in the state."
4. "Can you have it done by Friday?" "*You bet* — Thursday, even."
5. "You think she'll win?" "*You bet* she will."

**See also:** [sure thing](#sure-thing), [absolutely](#absolutely), [you got it](#you-got-it)

## Emphatic and enthusiastic agreement

### 100 percent

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhʌndrəd pɚˈsɛnt/ &middot; *uh-HUN-dred per-SENT*
**Русский:** на все сто, стопудово — полное согласие без единой оговорки
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *a hundred percent*, *one hundred percent*, *hundo p* (jokey)

Total, unqualified agreement — the modern replacement for *absolutely* among younger speakers. It says not just "yes" but "I have no reservations whatsoever."

1. "We should just cancel and reschedule." "*One hundred percent*."
2. *A hundred percent* — that place has gone downhill since they changed owners.
3. "Do you think I should take the job?" "*A hundred percent*. Don't overthink it."
4. "It's not really about the money, is it?" "*One hundred percent* not."
5. "*A hundred percent* agree with everything you just said."

**See also:** [absolutely](#absolutely), [totally](#totally), [no doubt](#no-doubt), [facts](#facts)

### absolutely

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæbsəˈlutli/ &middot; *ab-suh-LOOT-lee*
**Русский:** безусловно, совершенно верно — эмфатическое «да», уместно в любом стиле
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *absolutely not* (the emphatic negative), *abso-freaking-lutely* (very informal)

Emphatic yes with no hedging. It works at every level of formality, which makes it one of the most useful upgrades from a plain *yes* — warm in conversation, professional in an email, forceful in a meeting.

1. "Is that number final?" "*Absolutely*. We double-checked it."
2. "Would you recommend her?" "*Absolutely* — she's the best hire we made last year."
3. "Can I quote you on that?" "*Absolutely*, go ahead."
4. "*Absolutely*," the doctor said. "That's a completely normal reaction to the medication."
5. "You'd do it again?" "*Absolutely*. In a heartbeat."

**See also:** [definitely](#definitely), [certainly](#certainly), [100 percent](#100-percent), [of course](#of-course)

### ain't that the truth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌeɪnt ðæt ðə ˈtruθ/ &middot; *aynt-that-thuh-TROOTH*
**Русский:** вот уж правда, что верно, то верно — разг. согласие с горькой истиной
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ain't that the truth!*, *ain't it the truth*, *isn't that the truth*

Warm, folksy agreement with a weary or hard-won observation. The nonstandard *ain't* is doing deliberate work here: Americans who never otherwise say *ain't* say this, because the whole point is to sound plainspoken. It answers complaints and rueful truths, never questions.

1. "Everything costs twice what it did five years ago." "*Ain't that the truth*."
2. "*Ain't that the truth*," my aunt said, and went back to her crossword.
3. "Nobody's job is safe anymore." "*Ain't that the truth*. Ask me how I know."
4. "You can't fix a bad manager with a better spreadsheet." "*Ain't it the truth*."
5. "Kids grow up while you're busy looking at your phone." "*Ain't that the truth*, unfortunately."

**See also:** [amen](#amen), [true](#true), [tell me about it](#tell-me-about-it), [you can say that again](#you-can-say-that-again)

### amen

**Pronunciation:** /eɪˈmɛn/ &middot; *ay-MEN* — also /ɑˈmɛn/ &middot; *ah-MEN*
**Русский:** аминь; вот именно — из молитвы; вне церкви — горячее согласие
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *amen to that*, *amen, brother*, *amen, sister*, *can I get an amen*

Borrowed from Hebrew by way of church liturgy, where it closes a prayer with "so be it." Outside of worship it is fervent agreement with something someone just said, usually a complaint or a hard truth, and it carries a faint pulpit echo that speakers use half-jokingly.

1. "Whoever designed this parking lot should be ashamed." "*Amen*."
2. "*Amen to that*," she said, raising her glass.
3. "All I want is one weekend with nothing on the calendar." "*Amen*, brother."
4. The congregation answered *amen* and the organ started up.
5. "Kids should be outside, not on screens all day." "*Amen*. Can I get an *amen*?"

**See also:** [hear hear](#hear-hear), [preach](#preach), [word](#word), [true](#true)

### bet

**Pronunciation:** /bɛt/ &middot; *BET*
**Русский:** замётано, договорились — молодёжн. сленг из афроам. англ.
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *bet!*, *aight bet*, *bet bet*

Current American youth slang, from African American English, meaning "agreed," "understood," or "it's a deal." It answers a plan with confirmation and is common in texts among people under about thirty-five; from a manager to a client it would land as strange.

1. "Meet me at the courts at six." "*Bet*."
2. "I'll Venmo you tonight." "*Bet*, thanks."
3. "You're not gonna finish that whole thing." "*Bet*." *(here: a challenge — "watch me")*
4. "Party's moved to Danny's place." "*Bet*, see you there."
5. "*Bet*," he texted back, and that was the whole conversation.

**See also:** [say less](#say-less), [aight](#aight), [deal](#deal), [word](#word)

### bingo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɪŋɡoʊ/ &middot; *BING-oh*
**Русский:** в точку!, бинго! — разг. «ты назвал именно то, что нужно»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *bingo!*, *and bingo*, *bingo was his name-o* (jokey)

From the game, where it announces a winning card. As agreement it means "you have hit the exact answer," and it is usually said when the other person names the very thing the speaker was fishing for. It is upbeat and a little theatrical — right for a whiteboard, wrong for a condolence call.

1. "So the charge only shows up after the free trial ends?" "*Bingo*."
2. "*Bingo* — that's the file I've been hunting for all morning."
3. "Wait. Is it because both accounts use the same email?" "*Bingo*."
4. "*Bingo*!" the teacher said. "That's exactly the word I was after."
5. "You think he's stalling because he never ordered the parts?" "*Bingo*. Give the man a prize."

**See also:** [exactly](#exactly), [precisely](#precisely), [there you go](#there-you-go), [right](#right)

### certainly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɝtnli/ &middot; *SUR-tun-lee*
**Русский:** конечно, разумеется — офиц.-вежливое «да», в дружеской речи звучит чопорно
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *most certainly*, *certainly not* (the negative)

A polished, slightly formal yes. It is the standard register of hotel desks, customer service, and professional correspondence; between friends it can sound stilted or faintly sarcastic.

1. "Could you send someone up with extra towels?" "*Certainly*, ma'am."
2. "*Certainly*. I'll have the revised contract to you this afternoon."
3. "May I ask a follow-up question?" "*Certainly*."
4. "Would that be a problem for your team?" "*Certainly* not."
5. "So you're the expert on all this now?" "*Certainly*," he said, in a voice that meant the opposite.

**See also:** [of course](#of-course), [absolutely](#absolutely), [indeed](#indeed), [yes](#yes)

### damn right

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdæm ˈraɪt/ &middot; *dam-RITE*
**Русский:** чёрт возьми, да; а то! — мягк. бран., вызывающее согласие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal, mildly profane
**Variants:** *damn straight*, *damn skippy* (folksy), *you're damn right*, *darn right* / *dang right* (the clean substitutes)

Forceful agreement with a note of defiance in it — not merely "yes" but "yes, and I dare you to argue." *Damn* sits at the mild end of American profanity, but it is profanity: unremarkable among friends or in a bar, and conspicuous in a meeting, a classroom, or anywhere somebody's kids are listening. *Darn right* and *dang right* carry the same punch at no risk.

1. "You're going to ask for the raise, aren't you?" "*Damn right* I am."
2. "*Damn straight*. I'm not paying forty dollars to park."
3. "That was a foul." "*Damn right* it was."
4. "You'd have done the same for me." "*Darn right* I would."
5. "So you told him no?" "*Damn right* I did, and I'd tell him again tomorrow."

**See also:** [hell yeah](#hell-yeah), [absolutely](#absolutely), [you know it](#you-know-it), [you bet](#you-bet)

### definitely

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɛfənətli/ &middot; *DEF-uh-nit-lee*
**Русский:** определённо, точно — уверенное согласие без пафоса
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *defo* (rare in the U.S., mostly British), *definately* (a very common misspelling — avoid it)

Confident agreement, with a shade more "I'm sure about this" than "I'm enthusiastic about this." It is the everyday middle ground between a plain *yes* and a booming *absolutely*.

1. "Should we book ahead?" "*Definitely*. It fills up on weekends."
2. "*Definitely* the best burger in town."
3. "Are you in for Saturday?" "*Definitely*, put me down for two."
4. "You think that's a typo?" "*Definitely*. That can't be the real price."
5. "Am I making that up, or was he there too?" "*Definitely* there. He drove."

**See also:** [absolutely](#absolutely), [for sure](#for-sure), [no doubt](#no-doubt), [totally](#totally)

### exactly

**Pronunciation:** /ɪɡˈzæktli/ &middot; *ig-ZAKT-lee*
**Русский:** вот именно, точно — поддержка чужой мысли: «ты попал в точку»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *exactly!*, *that's exactly it*, *exactly right*

The standard way to endorse someone else's point rather than answer their question. It means "you have put your finger on it," and it is the single most useful agreement token in a discussion or an argument where you want to align yourself with the last speaker. It is also an ordinary adverb, so watch the boundary: *Exactly how much are we talking about?* is a real question, while a standalone *Exactly.* is agreement.

1. "So the problem isn't the code, it's the data." "*Exactly*."
2. "*Exactly* — that's the part nobody wants to talk about."
3. "You're saying we'd have to redo the whole thing?" "*Exactly* right."
4. "She wasn't angry, she was embarrassed." "*Exactly*. That's what I've been trying to say."
5. "*Exactly*!" three people said at once, and the meeting finally moved on.

**See also:** [precisely](#precisely), [right](#right), [true](#true), [I know, right](#i-know-right)

### facts

**Pronunciation:** /fækts/ &middot; *FAKTS*
**Русский:** факт, чистая правда — очень разг., молодёжное и афроам. согласие
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *facts!*, *straight facts*, *no lies detected* (related slang)

Internet-era and African American English agreement meaning "what you just said is simply true." It is a full turn on its own and is extremely common in comments, group chats, and casual speech among younger Americans.

1. "Nobody's checking email at eleven at night." "*Facts*."
2. "*Facts* — that album has not aged well."
3. "Rent in this city is completely out of control." "*Facts*."
4. She replied to his post with one word: "*facts*."
5. "You can't out-train a bad diet." "*Facts*. Learned that the hard way."

**See also:** [true](#true), [word](#word), [real talk](#real-talk), [100 percent](#100-percent)

### for real

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ˈril/ &middot; *fer-REEL*
**Русский:** правда, реально — разг. подтверждение; с вопр. интонацией — «серьёзно?»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *for real though*, *for real for real* / *frfr* (intensified), *fr* (text), *is that for real?*

**1.** As a reply it endorses what was just said as the honest truth, close to *facts*. **2.** With rising intonation, *for real?* is genuine surprise — the casual cousin of *really?*. The doubled *for real for real*, written *frfr* in texts, insists that the speaker is not exaggerating at all.

1. "Nobody in this office knows how the printer works." "*For real*."
2. "He drove eleven hours straight without stopping." "*For real*?"
3. "*For real*, that is the last time I let him plan a trip."
4. "I'm putting in my notice at the end of the month." "*For real*? Since when?"
5. "*Frfr*," she texted, "I have not slept in two days."

**See also:** [facts](#facts), [real talk](#real-talk), [really](#really), [true](#true)

### for sure

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ˈʃʊr/ &middot; *fer-SHOOR*
**Русский:** точно, наверняка — разг. уверенное согласие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *fo sho* (very informal, African American English), *fer sure* (Valley-girl parody), *that's for sure*

Relaxed, confident agreement — a little more emphatic than *sure* and a little more casual than *definitely*. It also tags onto the end of a statement to underline it (*It's cold out, for sure*).

1. "Are you going to the reunion?" "*For sure*. Wouldn't miss it."
2. "It's cheaper to fly out of the other airport, *for sure*."
3. "You'll let me know either way?" "*For sure*."
4. "*Fo sho*," he laughed. "I'll be there at eight."
5. "That was the loudest concert I've ever been to." "*For sure*. My ears are still ringing."

**See also:** [sure](#sure), [definitely](#definitely), [no doubt](#no-doubt), [totally](#totally)

### hear hear

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɪr ˈhɪr/ &middot; *HEER-HEER*
**Русский:** браво!, верно, верно! — книжн., публичное одобрение на собрании или тосте
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *hear, hear* (with a comma), *here here* (a common misspelling — the word is *hear*)

A shout of public agreement inherited from British parliamentary practice, short for "hear him, hear him." Americans use it at toasts, at meetings, and in comment threads, often with a knowing formality; said in a small private conversation it sounds like a joke.

1. "To another twenty-five years of marriage." "*Hear, hear*!"
2. "*Hear, hear*," someone called from the back of the room.
3. The board members murmured *hear, hear* as she finished her statement.
4. "Anyone who fixes this parking situation gets my vote." "*Hear, hear*."
5. Half the replies under the article were some version of "*hear, hear*."

**See also:** [amen](#amen), [agreed](#agreed), [indeed](#indeed)

### hell yeah

**Pronunciation:** /hɛl ˈjɛə/ &middot; *hel-YEH*
**Русский:** чёрт возьми, да!; ещё как! — мягк. бран., восторженное согласие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal, mildly profane
**Variants:** *hell yes*, *hell to the yes*, *heck yeah* (the polite substitute), *aw hell yeah*

Loud, delighted agreement. It expresses excitement as much as assent. *Hell* is at the mild end of American profanity, but it is still profanity: harmless among friends, noticeable in a classroom, a client meeting, or a room with somebody's grandmother in it. *Heck yeah* carries the same energy with none of the risk.

1. "Want to go see them play Friday?" "*Hell yeah* I do."
2. "*Hell yeah*!" the whole bar yelled when the shot went in.
3. "Free food in the break room." "*Heck yeah*."
4. "Are you proud of her?" "*Hell yes*. She worked two years for this."
5. "They finally fixed the elevator." "*Heck yeah* they did. Only took eight months."

**See also:** [yeah](#yeah), [damn right](#damn-right), [right on](#right-on), [you bet](#you-bet)

### I'll say

**Pronunciation:** /aɪl ˈseɪ/ &middot; *ile-SAY*
**Русский:** ещё бы!, не то слово — согласие с преуменьшением, слегка старомодное
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I'll say!*, *I'll say so*, *you said a mouthful* (dated relative)

Emphatic agreement with an understatement: "you're right, and if anything you're putting it mildly." It has a mid-century flavor and comes more readily from older Americans, but it has never gone away, and it is nearly always said with a short laugh.

1. "That was a long meeting." "*I'll say*. Two and a half hours."
2. "Looks like it rained a little." "*I'll say*! There's an inch of water in the basement."
3. "She seems pretty happy about the news." "*I'll say* she is."
4. "*I'll say*," my father muttered. "Cheapest car he ever bought and the best one."
5. "The hill's a little steep at the end." "*I'll say*. I had to walk the bike up."

**See also:** [you can say that again](#you-can-say-that-again), [tell me about it](#tell-me-about-it), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [amen](#amen)

### indeed

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈdid/ &middot; *in-DEED*
**Русский:** действительно, в самом деле — книжн.; в разговоре звучит нарочито учёно
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *indeed it is*, *indeed I did*, *very much indeed*

A dignified yes that confirms and slightly elevates. In American English it is noticeably bookish; used in ordinary chat it reads as either erudite or deliberately arch, which is exactly why people use it for comic effect.

1. "This is the original 1912 edition?" "*Indeed* it is."
2. "*Indeed*," the professor said, "that is precisely the difficulty."
3. "You must be exhausted." "*Indeed*. It's been a very long week."
4. "So you two have met." "*Indeed* we have."
5. "That's a bold claim." "*Indeed* it is — and I intend to defend it."

**See also:** [certainly](#certainly), [precisely](#precisely), [agreed](#agreed), [true](#true)

### naturally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnætʃɚəli/ &middot; *NATCH-er-uh-lee*
**Русский:** естественно, а как же — часто с оттенком «ну конечно, чего ещё ждать»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *naturally!*, *but naturally* (arch), *why, naturally*

Yes to something the speaker treats as following automatically from everything else. It is close to *of course* but drier, and in American use it very often carries a resigned "and of *course* that's what happened" about some predictable annoyance.

1. "Did they charge you for the second bag?" "*Naturally*."
2. "*Naturally*, the one morning I forget an umbrella, it pours."
3. "Will you be at the wedding?" "*Naturally*. She's my sister."
4. "And the hardware store was closed when you finally got there." "*Naturally*."
5. "*Naturally* we'll cover the shipping," the manager said. "That was our mistake."

**See also:** [of course](#of-course), [certainly](#certainly), [indeed](#indeed), [absolutely](#absolutely)

### no doubt

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈdaʊt/ &middot; *noh-DOWT*
**Русский:** без сомнения, спору нет — разг. согласие как с бесспорным
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *no doubt about it*, *without a doubt*, *nd* (rare, text)

Agreement that treats the point as beyond argument. As a standalone reply it is casual and confident; the longer *without a doubt* is the version that fits formal speech and writing.

1. "That was the best game of the season." "*No doubt*."
2. "*No doubt* about it — he's the fastest kid on the team."
3. "You think traffic's gonna be bad?" "*No doubt*. It's a Friday before a holiday."
4. "*Without a doubt* the most talented person in the department."
5. "She earned that promotion." "*No doubt*. Nobody's arguing."

**See also:** [for sure](#for-sure), [definitely](#definitely), [totally](#totally), [true](#true)

### of course

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈkɔrs/ &middot; *uhv-KORSS*
**Русский:** конечно, разумеется — «да» как само собой разумеющееся
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *'course*, *of course not* (the negative), *but of course* (arch)

Yes to something the speaker treats as obvious or automatic. **1.** A warm, willing yes to a request. **2.** Confirmation of something the listener should already have known — which, with the wrong tone, sounds condescending.

1. "Could you hold the door?" "*Of course*."
2. "Do you remember me?" "*Of course* I do! You cut my hair for six years."
3. "Is the museum closed on Mondays?" "*Of course* — like every museum in the country."
4. "*'Course* I'll help you move. What time?"
5. "You didn't have to bring anything." "*Of course* I did. My mother raised me right."

**See also:** [certainly](#certainly), [absolutely](#absolutely), [definitely](#definitely), [sure](#sure)

### preach

**Pronunciation:** /pritʃ/ &middot; *PREECH*
**Русский:** золотые слова!, говори, говори! — очень разг., из афроам. церковной традиции
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *preach it*, *preach, sister*, *you better preach*

Enthusiastic agreement borrowed from Black church call-and-response: "keep saying what you're saying, because it's true." It endorses an opinion, usually a bold or frustrated one, and it belongs strictly to casual speech.

1. "People need to learn how to merge." "*Preach*."
2. "*Preach*! Somebody finally said it."
3. "If they want us in the office, they can pay for parking." "*Preach*, sister."
4. She read the post out loud and her roommate just said, "*Preach*."
5. "Nobody needs eleven streaming subscriptions." "*Preach it*."

**See also:** [amen](#amen), [facts](#facts), [word](#word), [tell me about it](#tell-me-about-it)

### precisely

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈsaɪsli/ &middot; *pri-SICE-lee*
**Русский:** именно так, совершенно верно — книжн. двойник exactly
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *precisely so*, *that's precisely it*

The formal twin of *exactly*: it confirms that the other person has captured your meaning without error. It shows up in analytical and professional talk and can sound clipped or superior in casual company.

1. "So the risk isn't the storm itself, it's the power loss." "*Precisely*."
2. "*Precisely* the point I was trying to make."
3. "You're suggesting the sample was contaminated?" "*Precisely*."
4. "*Precisely* so," the attorney said. "Nothing more, nothing less."
5. "*Precisely* what I have been telling this committee for two years," she said, and sat back down.

**See also:** [exactly](#exactly), [indeed](#indeed), [right](#right), [certainly](#certainly)

### right on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌraɪt ˈɑn/ &middot; *rite-AWN*
**Русский:** в самую точку!, здорово! — разг. одобрение с духом контркультуры 60–70-х
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *right on, man*, *right onnn*

Approving agreement with a distinctly 1960s-and-70s counterculture flavor that has never fully gone away. It says "I'm with you and I like what you just said," and older speakers use it straight while younger ones often use it with a wink.

1. "I quit the job and I've never been happier." "*Right on*."
2. "*Right on*, man. Good for you."
3. "We're walking there instead of driving." "*Right on*. It's a nice night."
4. "She's going back to school at fifty-two." "*Right on*."
5. "*Right on*!" my uncle said, giving the guitarist a thumbs-up.

**See also:** [word](#word), [amen](#amen), [preach](#preach), [totally](#totally)

### say less

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *say-LESS*
**Русский:** понял, больше ни слова — молодёжн. сленг: «уговорил, хватит объяснять»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *say less fam*, *say no more* (the older, milder idiom)

Recent slang, from African American English, meaning "I'm already convinced — you can stop explaining." It agrees and closes the topic in one move. It is at least as common in speech as in texting among speakers under about thirty, and it is out of place in any professional exchange.

1. "There's free pizza in the conference room." "*Say less*."
2. "I can get us in without paying cover." "*Say less*, I'm on my way."
3. "It's a two-hour drive, but the beach is empty in October." "*Say less*."
4. "*Say less* — I'll bring the speaker."
5. "You don't have to explain the whole plot, I'll watch it." "*Say less*."

**See also:** [bet](#bet), [I hear you](#i-hear-you), [got it](#got-it), [word](#word)

### totally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtoʊtəli/ &middot; *TOH-tuh-lee* — casually /ˈtoʊɾəli/, with a flapped *t*
**Русский:** полностью согласен, ещё как — разг. дружелюбное «мы на одной волне»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *totes* (jokey, dated), *totally agree*, *oh totally*

Whole-hearted agreement with a friendly, unbuttoned feel. It confirms both facts and feelings, and it is one of the most common ways American speakers signal "same page as you" in relaxed conversation.

1. "That commute would kill me." "*Totally*."
2. "*Totally* — I felt the exact same way when I first moved here."
3. "Do you think I overreacted?" "*Totally* not. Anyone would have said something."
4. "It's worth the extra money, don't you think?" "*Oh, totally*."
5. "*Totally* get it. No apology necessary."

**See also:** [absolutely](#absolutely), [100 percent](#100-percent), [for sure](#for-sure), [same](#same)

### true

**Pronunciation:** /tru/ &middot; *TROO*
**Русский:** верно, а ведь правда — разг. уступка: «тут ты прав»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *so true*, *true true*, *facts* (near-synonym), *tru* (text spelling)

A one-word concession that the other person is right, often about something the speaker hadn't considered. Doubled — *true, true* — it becomes a mild, thinking-it-over agreement rather than a strong endorsement.

1. "You'd have to leave at five to beat the traffic." "*True*."
2. "*True*, but that only works if the store's still open."
3. "It's cheaper to just cook at home." "*True*, *true*."
4. "Nobody actually reads the terms and conditions." "*So true*."
5. "*True*. I hadn't thought about the deposit."

**See also:** [facts](#facts), [fair enough](#fair-enough), [right](#right), [word](#word)

### word

**Pronunciation:** /wɝd/ &middot; *WURD*
**Русский:** точно, правда твоя — очень разг., из афроам. англ.
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *word up*, *word to that*, *werd*

Agreement and acknowledgment from African American English, widely adopted in casual speech: "that's the truth" or, with rising pitch, "really?" Its heyday was the late 1980s and 1990s but it is still very much in use.

1. "That show got way better in season two." "*Word*."
2. "I'm taking Friday off." "*Word*? Nice."
3. "*Word up* — that's exactly what I've been saying."
4. "Traffic on the bridge was insane this morning." "*Word*. Took me an hour."
5. He nodded once and said, "*Word*," and they bumped fists.

**See also:** [facts](#facts), [true](#true), [right on](#right-on), [bet](#bet)

### you can say that again

**Pronunciation:** /ju kən ˈseɪ ðæt əˈɡɛn/ &middot; *yoo-kuhn-SAY-that-uh-GEN*
**Русский:** и не говори!, вот уж точно — согласие с жалобой, а не просьба повторить
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *you said it*, *you can say that again!*, *don't have to tell me twice*

Wholehearted agreement with a statement, almost always a complaint. Like *tell me about it*, it means the opposite of what the words say — nobody actually wants it repeated. *You said it* is the shorter and more common spoken form; the full phrase sounds a touch more emphatic.

1. "That was the worst flight of my life." "*You can say that again*."
2. "This week has felt about a month long." "*You said it*."
3. "*You can say that again* — I've been saying it for a year and nobody listened."
4. "Parking downtown is a nightmare." "*You said it*, brother."
5. "Somebody should have caught that before it went out the door." "*You can say that again*."

### you know it

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈnoʊ ɪt/ &middot; *yoo-NOH-it*
**Русский:** а то!, ещё спрашиваешь — разг. самоуверенное «да»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *you know it!*, *ya know it*, *you already know*

A confident, faintly cocky yes: "obviously — you didn't even need to ask." It answers questions about the speaker's own plans, tastes, or abilities, and with a light tone it reads as friendly rather than boastful.

1. "You going to the tailgate Saturday?" "*You know it*."
2. "Bet you're glad that's finally over." "*You know it*."
3. "Are you still doing the five a.m. workout thing?" "*You know it*. Every day."
4. "*You already know*," he said when I asked whether he'd saved me a seat.
5. "You're rooting for the underdog, aren't you?" "*You know it*."

## Agreeing with a statement, a plan, or a deal

### agreed

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡrid/ &middot; *uh-GREED*
**Русский:** согласен, принято — деловое подтверждение на встрече или в переписке
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *agreed!*, *fully agreed*, *couldn't agree more*

A compact, businesslike way to sign on to a statement or a proposal. It is at home in meetings, email threads, and negotiations, where it functions almost like a vote.

1. "We should push the launch to April." "*Agreed*."
2. "*Agreed* — let's take the whole thing back to the drawing board."
3. "This can't happen again." "*Agreed*. I've already changed the process."
4. "*Agreed* on all three points. I'll write it up."
5. "Two weeks is too tight." "*Agreed*, but the client won't move."

**See also:** [deal](#deal), [hear hear](#hear-hear), [sounds good](#sounds-good), [fair enough](#fair-enough)

### can't argue with that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkænt ˈɑrɡju wɪð ˌðæt/ &middot; *kant-AR-gyoo-with-THAT*
**Русский:** тут не поспоришь — признание правоты собеседника по существу
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *can't argue*, *no argument here*, *hard to argue with that*

Agreement that concedes the point on its merits, usually after the speaker has briefly considered resisting. It is a graceful way to lose a small disagreement without making a production of it, and it fits a conference room and a kitchen table equally well.

1. "It's cheaper, it's faster, and we already own the license." "*Can't argue with that*."
2. "*No argument here*. Let's go with the second option."
3. "If we leave now we beat the whole rush." "*Hard to argue with that*."
4. "Free is free." "*Can't argue*."
5. "You look like a man who needs a day off." "*Can't argue with that*," he said, laughing.

**See also:** [fair enough](#fair-enough), [granted](#granted), [point taken](#point-taken), [true](#true)

### cool

**Pronunciation:** /kul/ &middot; *KOOL*
**Русский:** окей, норм — разг. спокойное принятие плана, без восторга
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *cool cool*, *cool cool cool* (jokey, usually anxious), *that's cool*, *cool beans* (dated, jokey)

Low-key approval of a plan or acceptance of a piece of news — one of the most-used acknowledgment words in casual American English. It signals "no objection" rather than enthusiasm, and learners should know that a bare *cool* in a text reads flat or faintly annoyed, the same way a bare *k* does.

1. "I'll just meet you there at seven." "*Cool*."
2. "Dress code is whatever you want." "*Cool*, I'll wear jeans then."
3. "*Cool* — I'll let everybody know."
4. "Turns out we don't need the permit after all." "*Oh, cool*."
5. "*Cool cool cool*," he said, which meant he was quietly panicking.

**See also:** [okay](#okay), [alright](#alright), [sounds good](#sounds-good), [fine](#fine)

### deal

**Pronunciation:** /dil/ &middot; *DEEL*
**Русский:** идёт!, по рукам! — согласие на сделку или уговор
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *it's a deal*, *deal!*, *you've got a deal*, *deal or no deal* (set phrase)

Acceptance of a bargain or a trade, spoken at the moment the terms are settled. It implies a two-sided arrangement, so it answers an offer rather than a statement of fact.

1. "I'll cook if you do the dishes." "*Deal*."
2. "You get the front seat there, I get it back." "*Deal*."
3. "Fifty bucks and you throw in the case." "*Deal*."
4. "*Deal*," she said, and they shook on it.
5. "One episode, then bed." "*Deal*, but a long one."

**See also:** [done](#done), [agreed](#agreed), [bet](#bet), [fine](#fine)

### ditto

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪtoʊ/ &middot; *DIT-oh*
**Русский:** и я тоже, аналогично — разг. «то же самое можно сказать и обо мне»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ditto that*, *same here* (near-synonym)

From the bookkeeping mark meaning "the same again." As an interjection it means "what he said goes for me too," so it works only when the previous statement could be repeated word for word by the speaker.

1. "I'm exhausted." "*Ditto*."
2. "I thought the ending was a letdown." "*Ditto*. Felt rushed."
3. "Nice to finally meet you." "*Ditto*!"
4. "*Ditto* on the coffee — make mine black."
5. "I've got nothing on my calendar tomorrow." "*Ditto*, weirdly."

**See also:** [same](#same), [true](#true), [agreed](#agreed), [I know, right](#i-know-right)

### done

**Pronunciation:** /dʌn/ &middot; *DUN*
**Русский:** договорились, считай сделано — мгновенное согласие без торга
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *done deal*, *and done*, *consider it done*

Instant acceptance: the speaker treats the matter as already settled by saying so. It is punchier than *deal* and often signals that no negotiation is needed at all.

1. "Would you be willing to swap weekends?" "*Done*."
2. "All I need is your signature by Friday." "*Done*. I'll sign it now."
3. "Can you take my name off the list?" "*Consider it done*."
4. "Just promise you won't tell my mother." "*Done*."
5. "*Done* and *done*," he said, crossing both items off the whiteboard.

**See also:** [deal](#deal), [will do](#will-do), [you got it](#you-got-it), [agreed](#agreed)

### fair enough

**Pronunciation:** /fɛr ɪˈnʌf/ &middot; *fair-ih-NUF*
**Русский:** ладно, справедливо, резонно — уступка: «убедил, снимаю возражение»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *fair*, *fair point*, *that's fair*

Concession rather than enthusiasm: "I may not fully agree, but your reason is good enough and I'll drop it." It is one of the most useful phrases in an American disagreement because it ends the argument without a loser. The clipped *fair* is the younger, slangier version.

1. "I'd rather not drive at night." "*Fair enough*, I'll take the wheel."
2. "*Fair enough* — I hadn't considered the tax side of it."
3. "I just don't like horror movies." "*Fair*."
4. "We can't afford both this quarter." "*Fair enough*. Let's do the servers first."
5. "*That's fair*," he admitted. "I should have called."

**See also:** [true](#true), [touché](#touché), [I hear you](#i-hear-you), [fine](#fine)

### fine

**Pronunciation:** /faɪn/ &middot; *FINE*
**Русский:** ладно, хорошо — нейтральное согласие; отдельной репликой — неохотная сдача
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *fine, fine*, *fiiine* (drawn out, grudging), *that's fine*, *fine by me*

Acceptance with the enthusiasm drained out. **1.** Neutral approval of an arrangement (*Tuesday is fine*). **2.** Standing alone with falling intonation, it is famously reluctant — a surrender rather than an agreement, and Americans hear the difference instantly.

1. "Does Tuesday work for you?" "*Fine* by me."
2. "*Fine*, we'll do it your way." *(giving in)*
3. "I'll be about twenty minutes late." "*Fine*, no rush."
4. "*Fiiine*, you can have the last slice."
5. "Is this seat okay?" "*Fine*, thanks."

**See also:** [okay](#okay), [alright](#alright), [sounds good](#sounds-good), [fair enough](#fair-enough)

### granted

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd/ &middot; *GRAN-tid*
**Русский:** допустим, признаю — уступка в одном пункте, обычно перед «но»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *granted!*, *I'll grant you that*, *granted, but…*

A concession token: the speaker accepts one piece of the other side's case, usually on the way to pressing their own. Standing alone it is a clean admission. Leading a sentence — *Granted, it's expensive* — it is almost always setting up a *but*, and American listeners hear that *but* coming.

1. "It did come in under budget." "*Granted*."
2. "*Granted*, the first season is slow. Stay with it anyway."
3. "You've never actually driven one." "*Granted* — but I've read every review there is."
4. "*I'll grant you that* the timing was terrible."
5. "It's a lot of money." "*Granted*. It's also the last time we'll ever have to buy one."

**See also:** [fair enough](#fair-enough), [point taken](#point-taken), [true](#true), [touché](#touché)

### if you say so

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ju ˈseɪ ˌsoʊ/ &middot; *if-yoo-SAY-soh*
**Русский:** ну, если ты так говоришь — согласие лишь на словах, пассивно-агрессивное
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *if you say so…*, *sure, if you say so*, *whatever you say*

Agreement in form only. The speaker is dropping the argument without being convinced, and everyone in the room can hear it. This is one of the most reliably passive-aggressive phrases in American English — it is not a polite way to yield, and it should be used only when the sting is the point.

1. "It's not that far — twenty minutes, tops." "*If you say so*."
2. "*If you say so*," she said, in a tone that said she did not say so.
3. "He swears he's cutting back." "*If you say so*."
4. "This is the good kind of surprise, I promise." "*If you say so*."
5. "The new system is so much simpler." "*Sure, if you say so*."

**See also:** [I guess](#i-guess), [fine](#fine), [sure](#sure), [really](#really)

### I guess

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈɡɛs/ &middot; *eye-GESS*
**Русский:** наверное, пожалуй — вялое согласие, слышится как неохота
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I guess so*, *I suppose*, *yeah, I guess*, *I guess not* (the negative)

Hedged, half-hearted agreement: "all right, but I'm not enthusiastic and I could be wrong." Americans use it constantly to soften a yes. Learners should know how weak it sounds — *I guess* in answer to an invitation reads as reluctance, not modesty, and hosts hear it that way.

1. "Should we just walk from here?" "*I guess*. It's not that far."
2. "*I guess so*," he said. "I don't have a better idea."
3. "You're okay with the middle seat?" "*I guess*."
4. "So it's my turn to host this year." "*I guess* it is."
5. "That's the whole story?" "*I guess*, unless she left something out."

**See also:** [if you say so](#if-you-say-so), [fine](#fine), [sure](#sure), [alright](#alright)

### I hear you

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈhɪr ju/ &middot; *eye-HEER-yoo* — casually /aɪ ˈhɪr jə/ &middot; *eye-HEER-yuh*
**Русский:** понимаю тебя, слышу — сочувственное признание, но не обязательно согласие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I hear ya*, *I feel you*, *I hear that*

Sympathetic acknowledgment: "your complaint registers with me and I'm not arguing." Note the hedge built into it — *I hear you* often precedes a *but*, and by itself it can mean "I understand your position" without conceding it.

1. "This heat is unbearable." "*I hear you*. My AC's been running nonstop."
2. "*I hear ya*, man. Mondays are rough."
3. "I just want one weekend to myself." "*I hear you*, believe me."
4. "*I hear you*, but the deadline isn't moving."
5. "Nobody tells me anything around here." "*I hear that*."

### I know, right

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈnoʊ ˌraɪt/ &middot; *eye-NOH-rite*
**Русский:** вот и я о том же!, да не говори — очень разг. радостное согласие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *IKR* (text), *I know, right?*, *right?* (clipped)

Excited agreement with an opinion the speaker already held — "yes, and I'm glad someone else said it." The tag *right* has question intonation but is not a real question; nobody expects an answer.

1. "That trailer looked so bad." "*I know, right*?"
2. "*I know, right*? I thought I was the only one who noticed."
3. "Their prices have doubled." "*I know, right*? For the same sandwich."
4. She texted back "*ikr*" within about four seconds.
5. "It's way too cold in here." "*Right*? I've been saying that all week."

### I'm down

**Pronunciation:** /aɪm ˈdaʊn/ &middot; *ime-DOWN*
**Русский:** я за, я в деле — очень разг. согласие на затею (не про грусть)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *down*, *I'm down for it*, *I'm game*, *I'm in*, *down for whatever*

Willing agreement to a plan — "count me in." The *down* surprises learners, because *down* elsewhere means sad or unavailable; here it is entirely positive, and *I'm down to go* and *I'm down for that* both mean an enthusiastic yes. It answers proposals, never statements of fact.

1. "We're thinking tacos and then whatever's playing." "*I'm down*."
2. "Anybody want to drive up Saturday?" "*I'm down*, I've got nothing going on."
3. "Fair warning, it's a six a.m. start." "Still *down*."
4. "*I'm game*," she said, "as long as somebody else does the booking."
5. "Three days, no cell service, sleeping on the ground." "*I'm in*."

### point taken

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpɔɪnt ˈteɪkən/ &middot; *point-TAY-kuhn*

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *point taken!*, *fair point*, *your point is taken* (stiff)

An admission that the other person's objection landed and moved the speaker at least a little. It is the professional register's *touché* — standard in meetings, performance reviews, and arguments between adults who intend to keep working together afterward.

1. "You've made that same complaint in three different meetings now." "*Point taken*."
2. "*Point taken* — I'll stop rewriting your emails before they go out."
3. "Maybe don't send those at midnight." "*Point taken*."
4. "*Point taken*," the director said. "We'll build in the extra week."
5. "It's hard to be a team player from behind a closed door." "*Point taken*. Door's open."

### real talk

**Variants:** *real talk though*, *for real though*

From African American English. As a response it endorses what was just said as unvarnished truth; used at the front of a turn it announces that the speaker is about to drop the politeness and say what they actually think.

### right

A workhorse. **1.** Confirmation that a statement is correct. **2.** A backchannel dropped repeatedly into someone else's explanation to show you are following. **3.** At the head of a turn, a transition marker: "understood — now the next thing."

The standard American way to approve a plan without fuss. It is the closing move of countless emails and text threads, pleasant enough for a client and casual enough for a friend.

### tell me about it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtɛl mi əˈbaʊt ɪt/ &middot; *tel-mee-uh-BOWT-it*

**Variants:** *you're telling me*, *don't I know it*, *tell me about it!*

Emphatic agreement with a complaint, meaning the exact opposite of its literal words: "no need to explain — I've lived it." Learners sometimes take it as a real request for information, which produces a confusing conversation.

1. "This traffic is ridiculous." "*Tell me about it*. I've been sitting here forty minutes."

Approving confirmation that somebody has finally arrived at the right idea or the right attitude — "yes, now you've got it." Warm and encouraging when the tone is right, condescending when it is not. 1. "Okay, fine — I'll just ask them for the discount." "*There you go*."

## Acknowledging orders and information

### affirmative

**Pronunciation:** /əˈfɝmətɪv/ &middot; *uh-FUR-muh-tiv*
**Русский:** так точно, подтверждаю — воен. и радиообмен; в быту шутливо
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *that's affirmative*, *affirm* (aviation shorthand), *negative* (the opposite)

The official radio and military yes, chosen because it cannot be confused with *negative* over a noisy channel. Civilians use it either in genuinely procedural contexts — pilots, dispatchers, police — or jokingly, to sound like a robot or a soldier.

1. "Tower, confirm runway two-seven left." "*Affirmative*."
2. "Are you in position?" "*Affirmative*, we're a block out."
3. "*Affirmative*," the dispatcher replied. "Units are en route."
4. "Did you eat the last cookie?" "*Affirmative*," my brother said, deadpan.
5. "So we're agreed — nobody tells Mom until after the wedding." "*Affirmative*."

### aye

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ/ &middot; *EYE*
**Русский:** да, за — офиц. голос «за» на собрании; также мор. и «пиратское»

**Variants:** *aye, sir*, *the ayes have it* (set phrase)

A voting yes. In the United States it is the word used in legislatures, on boards, and at any meeting run on parliamentary procedure; outside of voting it survives mainly in nautical talk and in pirate imitations.

1. "All in favor?" "*Aye*."
2. "The motion carries — twelve *ayes*, three nays."
3. "*Aye*," said the senator from Ohio.
4. "All those in favor of ordering pizza instead of cooking?" "*Aye*!" said all four kids at once.

### aye aye

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪ ˈaɪ/ &middot; *eye-EYE*

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *aye-aye*, *aye aye, sir*, *aye aye, Captain*

The Navy's acknowledgment of an order, and it means something very specific: "I heard the order, I understand it, and I am carrying it out." Landlubbers use it playfully to accept an instruction, usually with a mock salute.

1. "Raise the anchor." "*Aye aye*, sir."
2. "*Aye aye*, Captain," the ensign answered.

### check

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɛk/ &middot; *CHEK*
**Русский:** есть, отмечено — при перекличке по списку, пункт за пунктом
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *check!*, *check and check*, *checkity-check* (jokey)

Item-by-item acknowledgment, taken straight from ticking boxes on a list: each *check* confirms that one more thing is present or done. It belongs to running through a list out loud — a packing list, a pre-flight, a recipe — and it does not work as an answer to an open question.

Radio language for "I received your message and understood it" — note that it confirms *reception*, not agreement. It has spread far beyond radios into ordinary workplace speech, where it sounds efficient and slightly clipped.

An acknowledgment that the information has been received and filed away. It is common in professional email and can be perfectly neutral — but standing alone in a reply it often reads as cool or faintly resistant, especially after criticism.

## Backchannels and continuers

These are the noises a listener makes to prove they are still there. Most of them carry no content at all — their entire meaning is in the pitch. Flat means "go on," rising means "really?", and falling means "hm, I'm not so sure." American speakers expect one every few seconds on the phone, and the silence of a listener who makes none reads as anger or a dropped call.

### ah

**Pronunciation:** /ɑ/ &middot; *AH*
**Русский:** а!, ага — до меня дошло, информация встала на место
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *ahh*, *ahhh*, *ah, I see*

A receipt token for a piece of information that clicks into place. Held longer (*ahhh*) it signals a fuller realization; clipped, it is a polite "I follow." Unlike *oh*, it suggests the news fits something the speaker already suspected.

1. "*Ah*, so that's why the door wouldn't lock."
2. "She's his sister, not his wife." "*Ah*. That explains dinner."
3. "*Ahhh*, now I get it. The two forms look almost identical."
4. "It's pronounced *EE*-lee, not *EYE*-lee." "*Ah*, sorry."
5. "*Ah*, yes — I remember reading about that."

**See also:** [oh](#oh), [aha](#aha), [I see](#i-see), [got it](#got-it)

### aha

**Pronunciation:** /ɑˈhɑ/ &middot; *ah-HAH* — an open-mouthed grunt on two beats, the second one stressed and sharply higher; the vowels are not fixed and some speakers say it closer to /əˈhɑ/
**Русский:** ага!, вот оно что! — возглас открытия, а не поддакивание
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ah-ha*, *ahaa*, *aha!*

The discovery token — the sound of a problem coming apart in the speaker's hands. It is not a backchannel and not agreement, and swapping it for *uh-huh* is one of the more noticeable learner errors: *uh-huh* means "go on," while *aha* announces that the speaker has just understood something, usually with a change of posture to match.

1. "*Aha* — the second copy was sitting in my drafts the whole time."
2. "*Aha*!" she said, fishing the missing screw out of the carpet.
3. "It only breaks when the file name has a space in it." "*Aha*."
4. "*Aha*, so *that's* why the two totals never matched."
5. He stared at the wiring diagram for a full minute before a quiet "*aha*" escaped him.

**See also:** [ah](#ah), [oh](#oh), [I see](#i-see), [uh-huh](#uh-huh)

### and?

**Pronunciation:** /ænd/ &middot; *AND* — usually reduced to /ən/ &middot; *UHN*
**Русский:** и?, и что дальше? — просьба продолжать; ровным тоном — «ну и что?»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *and then?*, *and so?*, *and…?*

A prompt for the rest of the story, with sharply rising intonation. **1.** Impatient curiosity — "don't stop there." **2.** Said flatly, it is dismissive: "so what?" The two are told apart entirely by tone, and the second one starts fights.

1. "So I finally called her back." "*And*?"
2. "*And*? What did the doctor say?"
3. "I told him exactly what I thought of the whole plan." "*And*?" "*And* he quit."
4. "You're going to be late." "*And*?" *(dismissive)*
5. "They offered me the job." "*And*?!" "*And* I took it!"

**See also:** [go on](#go-on), [really](#really), [oh yeah](#oh-yeah), [uh-huh](#uh-huh)

### go on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ ˈɑn/ &middot; *goh-AWN*
**Русский:** продолжай, дальше — прямое приглашение говорить дальше
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *go on…*, *do go on* (arch or sarcastic), *and go on from there*

An explicit invitation to keep talking, used when a speaker has paused to check whether they still have permission. It is common from therapists, interviewers, and parents, and drawn out (*ohhh, go on*) it becomes flirtatious or falsely modest.

1. "I don't want to bore you with the details." "*Go on*, I'm interested."
2. "*Go on* — what happened after she opened the letter?"
3. "So there were two problems with the design." "*Go on*."
4. "You're the best cook in this family." "*Oh, go on*." *(mock modesty)*
5. The detective nodded once. "*Go on*. Take your time."

**See also:** [and?](#and), [uh-huh](#uh-huh), [really](#really), [I see](#i-see)

### hm

**Pronunciation:** /hm̩/ &middot; *HMM* — not a spelled word but a hum through closed lips with a breathy onset; there is no vowel, and the pitch typically dips and comes back up
**Русский:** хм — мычание раздумья: «надо взвесить»; коротко и вниз — сомнение
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *hmm*, *hmmm*, *hmph* (a different sound — a short annoyed snort through the nose)

The thinking hum. Where *mm* says "I'm listening," *hm* says "I'm weighing this," and it buys the speaker a beat before answering. Long and level it is genuine consideration; short and falling it is skepticism. Americans reliably read a lone *hmm* in a text message as bad news coming.

1. "*Hm*. Let me think about that one."
2. "What if we moved the whole thing to Thursday?" "*Hmm*. That could actually work."
3. "*Hmm*," the mechanic said, which is never what you want to hear.
4. "He told me he'd already sent it." "*Hm*. That's not what my inbox says."
5. She wrote back a single "*hmm*" and I knew the answer was no.

**See also:** [mm](#mm), [huh](#huh), [oh](#oh), [really](#really)

### huh

**Pronunciation:** /hʌ/ &middot; *HUH* — often nasalized and reduced to /hə̃/
**Русский:** хм, надо же — с падением интерес; с подъёмом huh? — «что?, а?» (невежливо)
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *huh!*, *huh?*, *hunh*

**1.** With falling intonation, a small backchannel of interest: "that's news to me, and mildly interesting." **2.** With rising intonation it is not agreement at all but a request for repetition ("what?"), and a bare *huh?* to a stranger or a boss can sound rude — *sorry?* or *excuse me?* is safer.

1. "Turns out the building used to be a hat factory." "*Huh*. I had no idea."
2. "*Huh*, that's actually a pretty clever solution."
3. "They're closing the store in June." "*Huh*. Well, that stinks."
4. "*Huh*," he said, turning the box over in his hands.
5. "Your flight got moved to Terminal C." "*Huh*? Since when?"

**See also:** [oh](#oh), [really](#really), [wow](#wow), [ah](#ah)

### I see

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈsi/ &middot; *eye-SEE*
**Русский:** понятно, ясно — признание, что объяснение усвоено
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *I see…*, *oh, I see*, *ah, I see*

Acknowledgment that an explanation has been understood. It is a listening token with a slight formality to it — helpful on the phone and in professional talk — but delivered slowly and flatly it can imply disapproval or disbelief.

1. "The charge is for the second bag." "*I see*. That wasn't on the confirmation."
2. "*I see* — so nothing ships until the payment clears."
3. "He's been out sick all week." "*Ah, I see*."
4. "*I see*," the interviewer said, writing something down.
5. "We changed the process in January." "*I see*. That would explain the confusion."

**See also:** [ah](#ah), [got it](#got-it), [understood](#understood), [oh](#oh)

### is that right

**Pronunciation:** /ɪz ˈðæt ˌraɪt/ &middot; *iz-THAT-rite*
**Русский:** да ну?, вот как? — псевдовопрос-поддакивание; ровным тоном — скепсис
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *is that so*, *izzat right*, *is that a fact*

A backchannel that pretends to be a question. It invites the speaker to keep going and shows mild surprise; said flatly, especially with a Southern drawl, it can carry a cool skepticism close to "we'll see about that."

1. "He says he's going to run the whole marathon in under four hours." "*Is that right*?"
2. "*Is that right*? I never knew she grew up in Alaska."
3. "They're paying him twice what you make." "*Is that right*." *(flat, unimpressed)*
4. "The recipe's been in the family since 1890." "*Is that so*!"
5. "*Is that right*?" my grandfather said, which meant he did not believe a word of it.

**See also:** [really](#really), [you don't say](#you-dont-say), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [oh yeah](#oh-yeah)

### mm

**Pronunciation:** /m̩/ &middot; *MM* — a closed-mouth hum on a single note; there is no vowel to spell, so any respelling is an approximation
**Русский:** м-м, угу — минимальный звук «я слушаю», рот закрыт
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mmm*, *mmmm*, *mm?* (questioning)

The most minimal listening noise in English: lips closed, one hum, meaning little more than "I'm still here." A short flat *mm* is neutral attention; a long *mmm* over food is pleasure, and a falling *mm* with a lowered chin is doubt rather than agreement. Do not confuse it with *mm-mm* — two hums, the second one lower — which is the closed-mouth **no**.

1. "So I told him we'd think about it—" "*Mm*." "—and he seemed fine with that."
2. "*Mm*," she said, eyes still on her book.
3. "Are you listening to me?" "*Mm*. Keep going."
4. "*Mmm*, this soup is incredible."
5. "You really think that's a good idea?" "*Mmm*…" *(doubtful, trailing off)*

**See also:** [mm-hmm](#mm-hmm), [hm](#hm), [uh-huh](#uh-huh), [huh](#huh)

### mm-hmm

**Pronunciation:** /ˌm̩ˈhm̩/ &middot; *mm-HMM* — two hums through a closed mouth, the second one higher and stressed
**Русский:** угу, ага — «да» с закрытым ртом; в ответ на «спасибо» — «не за что»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mhm*, *mm-hm*, *m-hm*, *mmhmm*

The closed-mouth yes. It affirms and backchannels at once, and Americans use it constantly on the phone. Two warnings for learners: with a flat, descending contour it turns cold and can mean "I heard you and I disagree," and as an answer to *thank you* it is the standard Southern-flavored "you're welcome."

1. "You're coming Saturday, right?" "*Mm-hmm*."
2. "So I get off at exit 40—" "*Mm-hmm*." "—and you're the second driveway."
3. "Thanks so much for the ride." "*Mm-hmm*."
4. "*Mhm*," she texted back, which was somehow worse than nothing.
5. "Did you take your medicine?" "*Mm-hmm*," he said with his mouth full.

**See also:** [uh-huh](#uh-huh), [mm](#mm), [yeah](#yeah), [right](#right)

### no kidding

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈkɪdɪŋ/ &middot; *noh-KID-ing*
**Русский:** да ну?, серьёзно? — удивление; с падающей интонацией — сухое «да что ты»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *no kidding?*, *you're kidding*, *no joke*

**1.** With rising intonation, genuine surprise: "seriously?" **2.** With falling intonation, dry agreement with something obvious: "you don't say." The second one is gently sarcastic and very common in American conversation.

1. "She's been promoted twice this year." "*No kidding*? Good for her."
2. "It's freezing in this office." "*No kidding*. I'm wearing two sweaters."
3. "*No kidding* — I ran into my third-grade teacher at the airport."
4. "Turns out the cheap tires wear out faster." "*No kidding*." *(dry)*
5. "They gave us an extra day to finish." "*No kidding*? That's a first."

**See also:** [you don't say](#you-dont-say), [really](#really), [is that right](#is-that-right), [wow](#wow)

### oh

**Pronunciation:** /oʊ/ &middot; *OH*
**Русский:** а!, о! — реакция на новость: «этого я не знал»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *ohh*, *ohhh*, *oh!*, *oh…*

The basic receipt token of English: it marks the moment a speaker's state of knowledge changes. In this category it is the listener's way of saying "that's new to me," and a conversation in which one party never says *oh* sounds oddly flat to American ears.

1. "The meeting got moved to Thursday." "*Oh*. Okay, thanks."
2. "*Oh*, I didn't realize you two knew each other."
3. "She's not coming — she's got the flu." "*Oh*, that's too bad."
4. "*Ohhh*, you meant the *other* Main Street."
5. "*Oh*," he said quietly, and you could hear him rearranging his whole afternoon.

**See also:** [ah](#ah), [oh yeah](#oh-yeah), [I see](#i-see), [huh](#huh)

### oh yeah

**Pronunciation:** /oʊ ˈjɛə/ &middot; *oh-YEH*
**Русский:** а, точно!; ну да, ещё как — вспоминание или согласие; oh yeah? — вызов
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh yeah?*, *oh right*, *oh yes*

**1.** Sudden remembering: "that's right, I'd forgotten." **2.** Agreement with extra enthusiasm. **3.** With rising intonation, *oh yeah?* is a challenge — "is that so?" — and in an argument it is a step toward a fight.

1. "You said you'd call the plumber." "*Oh yeah*, I completely forgot."
2. "That place has amazing tacos." "*Oh yeah*, the best in town."
3. "*Oh yeah*? And who's going to stop me?" *(challenge)*
4. "*Oh yeah*, I remember him — tall guy, played bass."
5. "Do you like hiking?" "*Oh yeah*, we go almost every weekend."

**See also:** [yeah](#yeah), [oh](#oh), [really](#really), [is that right](#is-that-right)

### really

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɪli/ &middot; *RIL-ee* — also /ˈriəli/ &middot; *REE-uh-lee*
**Русский:** правда?, серьёзно? — интерес; ровным тоном — скепсис или ирония
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *really?*, *oh really*, *rly* (text), *reeeally* (drawn out, skeptical)

The workhorse backchannel of surprise and interest. **1.** Rising, it asks the speaker to confirm and keep going. **2.** Falling and flat, it is skeptical or unimpressed, and *oh, really* with a drawl is nearly always sarcastic.

1. "They eloped in Vegas last weekend." "*Really*? Nobody told me."
2. "*Really*. And how much did that cost you?" *(skeptical)*
3. "I've never seen the ocean." "*Really*? Not once?"
4. "He says he never checks his phone at dinner." "*Oh, really*."
5. "It only took twenty minutes." "*Really*! I budgeted an hour."

**See also:** [is that right](#is-that-right), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [seriously](#seriously), [for real](#for-real)

### seriously

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪriəsli/ &middot; *SEER-ee-uhs-lee*
**Русский:** серьёзно?, да ладно — удивление; ровным тоном — «вот именно, безобразие»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *seriously?*, *are you serious*, *srsly* (text), *for serious* (jokey)

**1.** Rising, it asks the speaker to confirm surprising news — *really?* with more disbelief in it. **2.** Falling and flat, it is emphatic agreement with a complaint: "yes, and it's ridiculous." **3.** Stressed hard and aimed at somebody, *seriously?* stops being a backchannel and becomes an accusation.

1. "They cancelled the whole program." "*Seriously*? After two years of this?"
2. "Who parks across two spaces?" "*Seriously*."
3. "*Seriously*," he said, staring at the empty pot. "Every single morning."
4. "She got the promotion." "*Seriously*? Good for her."
5. "*Srsly*," my sister texted, "do not let him pick the restaurant again."

**See also:** [really](#really), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [is that right](#is-that-right), [wow](#wow)

### uh-huh

**Pronunciation:** /ʌˈhʌ/ &middot; *uh-HUH* — nasalized in casual speech, closer to /ə̃ˈhə̃/
**Русский:** угу, ага — «да» с открытым ртом и знак «я слушаю»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *uh huh*, *unh-huh*, *uh-huh?*

The open-mouthed cousin of *mm-hmm*: an affirmative grunt, made with the jaw loose and the second syllable stressed and higher, that answers yes-or-no questions and keeps a conversation moving. It is not a synonym for *aha*, which announces a discovery rather than acknowledging one. A rapid, flat string of *uh-huhs* often signals impatience rather than attention.

1. "Did you remember to lock up?" "*Uh-huh*."
2. "So you take 95 north—" "*Uh-huh*." "—for about ten miles."
3. "*Uh-huh*, *uh-huh*, and then what?"
4. "Are you even listening?" "*Uh-huh*." "Then what did I just say?"
5. "You'll be there by seven?" "*Uh-huh*, maybe earlier."

**See also:** [mm-hmm](#mm-hmm), [aha](#aha), [yeah](#yeah), [mm](#mm)

### wow

**Pronunciation:** /waʊ/ &middot; *WOW*
**Русский:** ух ты, ничего себе — реакция на впечатляющую или скверную новость
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *woww*, *wowww*, *oh wow*

Usually filed under surprise, but as a backchannel it does a specific job: it tells the speaker their news deserves a reaction. It is the listener's way of scoring a story as impressive, shocking, or bad — and a flat, delayed *wow* is a well-known way of expressing disapproval.

1. "They drove the whole thing in one day — nineteen hours." "*Wow*."
2. "*Wow*, that's a lot of money for a used car."
3. "And then he just walked out in the middle of the meeting." "*Oh, wow*."
4. "*Wow*." She set down the letter. "I don't even know what to say."
5. "I told her the truth and she thanked me for it." "*Wow*, that's rare."

**See also:** [huh](#huh), [really](#really), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [oh](#oh)

### you don't say

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈdoʊnt ˌseɪ/ &middot; *yoo-DOHNT-say*
**Русский:** да что ты говоришь! — чаще ирон., о чём-то до боли очевидном
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *you don't say!*, *ya don't say*, *do tell* (related)

**1.** An old-fashioned expression of surprise that invites more of the story. **2.** In modern American use it is far more often deadpan sarcasm aimed at something painfully obvious, so learners should assume the ironic reading unless the speaker is clearly delighted.

1. "It turns out the cat had been in the garage the whole time." "*You don't say*!"
2. "Apparently it rains a lot in Seattle." "*You don't say*." *(sarcastic)*
3. "*You don't say*! Your mother and I went to that same hotel."
4. "The line is longer at lunchtime." "*You don't say*."
5. "He's forty and still lives with his parents." "*Ya don't say*."
