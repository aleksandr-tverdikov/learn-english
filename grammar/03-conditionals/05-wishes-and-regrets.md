# Wishes and regrets

> The unreal past outside the *if*-clause — *wish*, *if only*, *would rather*, *it's time*, *as if*, and the polite request frames that all run on the same machinery.

[← Conditionals](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

A [second conditional](01-the-four-types.md) puts a past tense into a clause about right now — *if I **knew**, I'd tell you* — and that past tense has nothing to do with past time. The trick is not confined to *if*. A dozen other structures borrow the same device, and once you see what the device is doing, a dozen separate "rules" collapse into one.

**A past form marks distance from fact, not distance in time.** One step back from the present (*know* → *knew*) says *this is not so now*. One step back from that (*knew* → *had known*) says *this was not so then, and nothing can be done about it*. The forms are the ordinary past and past perfect, spelled exactly as they always are. Only the job has changed.

| The step | The form | What it marks | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| one step back from the present | past simple — *knew*, *had*, *were* | not true now; or fixed and unwelcome in the future | *I wish I **knew**.* |
| one step back from the past | past perfect — *had known* | not true then, and past repair | *I wish I **had known**.* |
| the modal step | *would*, *could* | a change you want but cannot make yourself | *I wish it **would** stop.* |

The mechanism itself — tense as distance from fact — is worked out in [Real, unreal, and time](06-real-unreal-and-time.md). Every frame in this file picks one of those three steps and sticks to it. *Wish* takes all three. *If only* takes all three. *Would rather*, *it's time*, *as if*, *suppose*, and the polite frames take the first two. Nothing here takes a present tense where a past tense is called for, and that single fact is the source of nearly every error in the topic — ✗ *I wish I know*, ✗ *It's time we go*, ✗ *I'd rather you don't*.

**The unreal past is invisible to backshift.** A past form that is already doing non-past work cannot be pushed back again. Reporting *"I wish I knew"* gives *She said she **wished** she **knew*** — the real present *wish* moves, the unreal past *knew* does not. The mechanism is worked out in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md#what-backshift-never-touches) and the full inventory of reported wishes is in [Special cases](../01-reported-speech/08-special-cases.md#reporting-wish-and-if-only).

**The unreal past has no person agreement worth the name.** The one form that survives from the old subjunctive is *were*, used for all persons: *I were*, *he were*, *it were*. It appears after *wish*, after *if*, after *as if*, and after *suppose*, and it is the only place in modern English where the verb form itself announces that the clause is counterfactual.

## *wish* + past simple

### The form

> **subject + *wish* (+ *that*) + subject + past simple**

The *that* is grammatical and almost always dropped: *I wish (that) I knew.* Everything that follows this rule in the file assumes the bare version.

The past tense in the clause describes **now** — or a future already settled and not to your liking. *I wish I knew* means *I do not know, and I mind*.

> I **wish** I **knew** what she wanted.  
> She **wishes** she **had** more time.  
> We **wish** the apartment **were** bigger.  
> I **wish** my Spanish **was** good enough for this.

Negative in the *wish*-clause — this is the one learners reach for last and need most, because English flips the polarity of the Russian equivalent:

> I **wish** I **didn't have** to work Saturdays.  
> He **wishes** his neighbors **weren't** so loud.  
> I **wish** she **didn't know** about it.  
> They **wish** it **wasn't** so far from the train.

Questions, both about the wish and about its content:

> **Do** you ever **wish** you **lived** somewhere warmer?  
> **Don't** you **wish** you **had** a bigger kitchen?  
> **What do** you **wish** you **knew** more about?  
> **Doesn't** he **wish** he **spoke** the language?

Negated *wish* itself is rare and means something different — *I don't wish him any harm* is a formal use of *wish* as a transitive verb, not this frame at all. See [*wish* is also an ordinary verb](#wish-is-also-an-ordinary-verb).

### The meaning is always a contradiction

Read any *wish* + past sentence and you can state the fact it denies. That is the test, and it is worth running whenever you are unsure which tense the frame wants.

| The wish | The fact it asserts |
|---|---|
| *I wish I **knew** German.* | I do not know German. |
| *I wish I **didn't know**.* | I do know, and I would rather not. |
| *I wish she **were** here.* | She is not here. |
| *I wish I **had** a car.* | I do not have a car. |
| *I wish I **didn't have** a car.* | I have one, and it is a nuisance. |

Notice rows 2 and 6. English puts the negative on the **wished-for** situation, not on the actual one. Russian does the opposite — *жаль, что я знаю* states the fact and marks it as regrettable — and the mismatch produces the single most common wish error there is. It gets its own treatment in [What Russian speakers get wrong most](#what-russian-speakers-get-wrong-most).

### The future case

A past tense after *wish* can also point forward, provided the future in question is already fixed and you are not happy about it. You are not wishing for a change; you are registering a complaint about an arrangement.

> I **wish** tomorrow **were** Friday.  
> She **wishes** the meeting **weren't** at seven in the morning.  
> I **wish** I **was going** to the reunion, but I'll be in Denver.

If you want the future to *change*, this frame is the wrong one: ✗ *I wish it stops raining tomorrow* → *I **hope** it stops raining tomorrow* (the change is possible) or *I **wish** it **would** stop raining* (a complaint about now).

### The *were* rule, and the truth about *was*

After *wish*, the verb *be* has two first- and third-person singular forms available: subjunctive *were* and indicative *was*.

| | Form | Status |
|---|---|---|
| *I wish I **were** taller.* | subjunctive | correct everywhere; required in formal and edited writing |
| *I wish I **was** taller.* | indicative | standard in American speech; common in informal writing; still flagged by editors |
| *I wish she **were** here.* | subjunctive | correct everywhere |
| *I wish she **was** here.* | indicative | extremely common in speech |

The honest summary, since coursebooks usually give half of it: **write *were*, and do not be surprised by *was*.** American speakers say *I wish I was* constantly, and no native listener hears it as an error. But *were* is the form that is never wrong, it is what a copy editor will put back, and in one fixed expression it is effectively obligatory — *if I were you* is the advice formula, and ✗ *if I was you* reads as dialect.

Two more details on *were*. It is the only verb with a distinct unreal form; every other verb uses its plain past for both jobs (*I wish I knew* is the same *knew* as *I knew him in college*). And the plural is no help in telling the two apart, because *we were* and *they were* are spelled identically whichever mood they are in.

### The verbs that will not go into the past

*Wish* + past needs a state, not an event. You can wish you *knew*, *had*, *were*, *lived*, *spoke*, *understood* — all conditions that hold or do not hold right now. You cannot wish that a one-time action *happened*: ✗ *I wish the bus came on time tomorrow* → *I hope the bus comes on time tomorrow*; ✗ *I wish she called me* (meaning "I want her to call") → *I wish she **would** call me*.

A past simple with a dynamic verb after *wish* is not ungrammatical — it is just read as a **habit**, which is a state in disguise: *I wish she called more often* is a complaint about how seldom she calls, not a request for a call tonight.

## *wish* + past perfect

### The form

> **subject + *wish* + subject + *had* + past participle**

One more step back, for a regret about something that is finished. *I wish I had listened* means *I did not listen, and it is too late*.

> I **wish** I **had listened** to you.  
> She **wishes** she **had taken** the other job.  
> We **wish** we **had booked** earlier.  
> He **wishes** he **had never said** it.

Negatives sit on *had*, and again the polarity is the mirror image of the Russian:

> I **wish** I **hadn't said** anything.  
> She **wishes** she **hadn't spent** the money.  
> They **wish** they **hadn't bought** the house.

Questions:

> **Do** you ever **wish** you **had studied** something else?  
> **What do** you **wish** you **had done** differently?  
> **Don't** you **wish** you **had brought** a jacket?

### The contraction trap

*I'd* is the same two letters for *I had* and *I would*, and both are legal after *wish*. The word that follows settles it: a past participle means *had*, a bare infinitive means *would*.

| Written | Expands to | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *I wish I**'d** known.* | *I had known* | past regret |
| *I wish he**'d** call.* | *he would call* | complaint about now |
| *I wish he**'d** called.* | *he had called* | past regret |

The middle and last rows differ by one letter in writing and by one consonant in speech, and they mean different things. In careful speech Americans keep the *d* of *called* audible for exactly this reason.

### Past regret, not past tense

The commonest structural error here is a plain past where the frame wants the past perfect:

> ✗ *I wish I **didn't say** that.*  →  ✓ *I wish I **hadn't said** that.*

✗ *I wish I didn't say that* is not meaningless — it is a well-formed sentence of the previous frame, meaning *I regret that I do not say that, as a habit*, which is almost never what anyone intends. The rule is mechanical: **regret about a completed event takes *had* + participle**, no exceptions. Modals follow suit with a perfect infinitive — *I wish I **could have been** there*, *She wishes she **could have warned** him*, never ✗ *I wish I could be there yesterday*.

## *wish* + *would*

### The form

> **subject + *wish* + a different subject + *would* + bare infinitive**

This is the complaint frame. It does not describe a state that is untrue; it describes a **behavior you want changed**, and it carries irritation, impatience, or resignation. Where the other two frames look backward at the facts, this one looks forward at somebody who is not cooperating.

> I **wish** you **would** slow down.  
> I **wish** he **would** answer his phone.  
> She **wishes** her landlord **would** fix the heat.  
> I **wish** somebody **would** tell me what is going on.

Negative — *wouldn't* is where this frame does its heaviest work in real speech, because it is how Americans ask someone to stop doing something without issuing an order:

> I **wish** you **wouldn't** do that.  
> I **wish** she **wouldn't** interrupt.  
> I **wish** they **wouldn't** park across the driveway.  
> I **wish** he **wouldn't** talk to me like that.

Questions are uncommon with this frame but perfectly grammatical:

> **Don't** you **wish** he **would** just admit it?  
> **Do** you ever **wish** they **would** turn the music down?

### Two uses

**Somebody's behavior.** The subject is a person, the verb is something they choose to do, and the sentence is a complaint aimed past the listener rather than at them. *I wish you wouldn't smoke in here* is softer than *Don't smoke in here* and harder to argue with than *Could you not smoke in here?* — it states a preference and leaves the compliance to you.

**Something outside your control.** The subject is a thing, the weather, a machine, or a situation, and the sentence is an appeal to nobody in particular:

> I **wish** it **would** stop raining.  
> I **wish** this computer **would** load faster.  
> I **wish** the semester **would** hurry up and end.  
> I **wish** the noise **would** stop.

What the two share is that a change is imaginable. *Would* here is the *would* of willingness — the same one in *The door won't open* — so it needs something that could act differently. A permanent state cannot: ✗ *I wish he would be taller* → *I wish he **were** taller*; ✗ *I wish I would have more money* → *I wish I **had** more money*.

### You cannot normally wish *you* would

This is the rule that catches everyone. **The subject after *would* has to be different from the subject of *wish*.**

> ✗ *I wish I would get the job.*  
> ✓ *I **hope** I **get** the job.*  
> ✗ *I wish I would remember people's names.*  
> ✓ *I wish I **could** remember people's names.*

The logic is that *would* asks somebody else to change their behavior, and you cannot ask yourself as though you were a third party. What you want instead depends on the meaning:

| What you mean | The repair | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I want this to happen and it might | *hope* + present or *will* | *I **hope** I pass.* |
| I lack the ability | *wish* + *could* | *I wish I **could** sing.* |
| I lack the thing or the quality | *wish* + past | *I wish I **had** more patience.* |
| I regret a past act of mine | *wish* + past perfect | *I wish I **hadn't** quit.* |

There is one narrow exception, and it proves the rule. When you speak of your own habit as though it were beyond your control — as though the person doing it were not quite you — *I wish I would* does occur: *I wish I would stop biting my nails.* It is idiomatic, it is not common, and it always carries that note of self-estrangement. Learners are better off with *I wish I could stop*.

### *Wish* + *would* is not a polite request

The frame is aimed at the listener but does not address them. That makes it useful and also makes it edged: *I wish you'd call me back* is a reproach for not having called, not a request that you call. When you want an actual polite request, English has a set of frames built for the job, and they are in [The polite frames](#the-polite-frames).

## *wish* + *could*

*Could* after *wish* is the past form of *can*, doing exactly what *knew* does for *know*. It covers inability, unavailability, and refused permission, in the present:

> I **wish** I **could** help.  
> I **wish** we **could** stay longer.  
> She **wishes** she **could** afford it.  
> I **wish** I **could** tell you, but I signed an NDA.

Negative — *I wish I **couldn't** hear them through the wall*; *He wishes he **couldn't** remember any of it.* Question — *Don't you **wish** you **could** just walk out?* For the past, *could* takes a perfect infinitive: *I wish I **could have come** to the wedding*; *She wishes she **could have said** goodbye.*

*I wish I could help* is the standard soft refusal in spoken American English, and it works because the unreal past makes the inability sound like a fact of the universe rather than a decision. The contrast with *would* is worth holding on to:

| Sentence | What it says |
|---|---|
| *I wish I **could** come.* | I am unable. Circumstances, not choice. |
| *I wish you **would** come.* | You are able. You are choosing not to, and I mind. |
| *I wish he **could** call more often.* | Something stops him. |
| *I wish he **would** call more often.* | Nothing stops him; he just doesn't. |

## *wish* is also an ordinary verb

Three uses of *wish* have nothing to do with the unreal past, and they are set out here so the frame above is not over-applied.

**wish + two objects** is the formulaic one, for greetings and good luck: *I wish you luck*, *We wish you a safe trip*, *They wished us a merry Christmas*.

**wish + *to* + infinitive** is a formal substitute for *want* — *I wish to file a complaint*; *Passengers who wish to board early should proceed to gate 12*. It is not a wish in the ordinary sense at all: *I wish to speak to the manager* means you intend to, not that you regret anything, and ✗ *I wish to have more time* is the classic misuse — the intended meaning needs *I wish I had more time*.

**wish for** is wishing in the fairy-tale sense, usually ironic: *She wished for a pony every birthday*; *Be careful what you wish for.*

## *wish* vs. *hope*

This is the distinction Russian speakers get wrong more often than any other in the topic, because the two English verbs carve up a space that Russian divides along a different line.

### The rule

| | *hope* | *wish* |
|---|---|---|
| The situation is | still possible | contrary to fact |
| Tense after it | present, *will*, or past for a past event | unreal past, past perfect, or *would* |
| Attitude | optimism | regret |
| *I ___ you're right.* | **hope** — you may well be | |
| *I ___ you were right.* | | **wish** — you are not |

> I **hope** you**'re** right.  →  It might be true. I am rooting for it.  
> I **wish** you **were** right.  →  You are not right. I am sorry about that.

Those two sentences describe the same listener saying the same thing, and they take opposite positions on whether it is true. That is the whole distinction, and it is entirely carried by the verb plus the tense.

### The forms *hope* takes

*Hope* is an ordinary verb and takes ordinary tenses. In particular it takes the **present simple for the future**, which is where learners most often insert a *will* that native speakers leave out:

> I **hope** it **works**.  
> I **hope** they **get** here on time.  
> I **hope** you **feel** better soon.  
> I **hope** it **will work**. (also fine, slightly more formal or more emphatic)

*Hope* about the past is perfectly normal, which surprises people — you can hope about something already settled, as long as you do not know the outcome: *I **hope** I didn't wake you*; *I **hope** she got home all right*; *I **hope** they haven't left yet.*

Negative answers use *I hope not*, never ✗ *I don't hope so*: "Is it going to rain?" — "I **hope not**." *I hope so* is the positive counterpart, and both are fixed: ✗ *I hope it*, ✗ *I don't hope*.

### The side-by-side

| Sentence | Verb | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I ___ you're right.* | **hope** | It may be true. |
| *I ___ you were right.* | **wish** | You are not right. |
| *I ___ it doesn't rain tomorrow.* | **hope** | Tomorrow is open. |
| *I ___ it would stop raining.* | **wish** | It is raining now. |
| *I ___ I pass the exam.* | **hope** | Not taken yet, or not graded. |
| *I ___ I had passed the exam.* | **wish** | I failed. |

One error here is a habit rather than a grammar slip. ✗ *I wish you a quick answer*, in an email, is built on the legitimate *wish + two objects* pattern, but it lands strangely: English reserves that pattern for luck, health, and holidays, not for outcomes you are asking someone to produce. Write *I hope to hear from you soon.*

## *if only*

*If only* is the emphatic twin of *wish*. It takes the same three tenses, means the same things, and differs in two ways: it is **stronger**, and it can **stand alone** with no main clause at all. Its dictionary entry, with the *if only to* sense that is not a wish at all, is at [if only](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-only).

### All three time frames

| Tense | Time | Example |
|---|---|---|
| past simple | now or a fixed future | ***If only** I **knew** where she went.* |
| past perfect | a finished past | ***If only** I **had listened**.* |
| *would* | somebody else's behavior | ***If only** he **would** answer his phone.* |
| *could* | inability now | ***If only** we **could** afford it.* |

> **If only** I **had** more time.  
> **If only** she **were** here to see this.  
> **If only** they **hadn't** sold the place.  
> **If only** it **would** stop snowing.  
> **If only** I **could** take the day off.

Negatives behave exactly as after *wish*, with the negative on the wished-for situation:

> **If only** I **didn't have** to explain this again.  
> **If only** he **hadn't** opened his mouth.  
> **If only** you **wouldn't** argue with everything.

There is no question form. *If only* is an exclamation, and an exclamation cannot be asked.

### As a complete sentence

This is what *wish* cannot do. *If only* needs no main clause; the regret is the whole utterance, and it usually takes an exclamation point:

> **If only** I **had known**!  
> **If only** it **were** that simple.  
> "Maybe they'll give you the afternoon off." — "**If only**."

Bare *If only*, two words and a period, is a fixed reply meaning *I wish that were true*. In formal prose the clause left on its own is a sentence fragment, which is why editors convert it: *If only we had more data* becomes *We wish we had more data*. In speech and in fiction the fragment is the point.

A result clause can also be attached, in which case *if only* is simply an intensified *if*: *If only I **had known**, I **would have called**.* Compare the plain conditional — *If I had known, I would have called* — and the difference is entirely emotional: *if only* says the missing condition is the whole tragedy.

### *wish* against *if only*

| | *wish* | *if only* |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | neutral | strong, often bitter |
| Stands alone | no — ✗ *I wish.* is not a sentence in this sense | yes — *If only!* |
| Takes a result clause | no | yes |
| Register | any | speech, fiction, argument; rare in formal prose |
| Exclamation point | unusual | normal |

## *would rather*

*Would rather* states a preference, and it is the frame where English most abruptly demands a past tense for a present meaning — but only when the preference is about somebody else. The modal entry is at [would rather](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#would-rather).

### Same subject: bare infinitive

> **subject + *would rather* + bare infinitive (+ *than* + bare infinitive)**

No *to*, ever, on either side of *than*:

> I**'d rather** stay home.  
> She**'d rather** walk than wait for the bus.  
> We**'d rather** not talk about it.  
> They**'d rather** pay cash than use a card.

Negative: *not* goes after *rather*, and *I'd rather not* stands alone as a complete polite refusal.

> I**'d rather not** say.  
> He**'d rather not** drive at night.  
> "Do you want to come?" — "I**'d rather not**."

Question:

> **Would** you **rather** sit inside or out?  
> **Would** she **rather** we came another time?  
> **Wouldn't** you **rather** take the earlier flight?

For a preference about your own past, *would rather* takes a perfect infinitive:

> I**'d rather have heard** it from you.  
> She**'d rather have stayed**, but the babysitter had to leave.

### Different subject: past tense

> **subject + *would rather* + a different subject + past simple**

The verb goes into the past and the meaning is present or future. This is the construction, and it is where ✗ *I'd rather you don't* comes from.

> I**'d rather** you **didn't** mention it.  
> She**'d rather** we **came** on Sunday.  
> They**'d rather** nobody **knew** yet.  
> I**'d rather** he **paid** me in advance.

Negative:

> I**'d rather** you **didn't**.  
> We**'d rather** they **weren't** told until Monday.  
> He**'d rather** she **didn't** call the office.

*I'd rather you didn't*, standing alone, is one of the most useful sentences in polite American English: it declines a request without giving a reason and without saying no.

Question:

> **Would** you **rather** I **drove**?  
> **Would** he **rather** we **waited** outside?

For the past, the clause takes the past perfect:

> I**'d rather** you **hadn't** told her.  
> She**'d rather** he **hadn't** come at all.

| Frame | Subject | Time | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *would rather* + bare infinitive | same | now / future | *I'd rather **stay**.* |
| *would rather have* + participle | same | past | *I'd rather **have stayed**.* |
| *would rather* + clause, past simple | different | now / future | *I'd rather you **stayed**.* |
| *would rather* + clause, past perfect | different | past | *I'd rather you **had stayed**.* |

### The neighbors

**Would sooner** is an exact synonym, slightly more old-fashioned: *I'd sooner walk than ask him for a ride.* **Would just as soon** means "I have no objection, and mildly prefer": *I'd just as soon eat at home.* **Would prefer** is the one that takes *to*, and its two-subject version needs *it if*: *I'd prefer **to** walk*; *I'd prefer **it if** you didn't.*

> ✗ *I'd rather to stay.*  &middot;  ✗ *I'd rather you don't.*  &middot;  ✗ *I'd prefer stay.*  &middot;  ✗ *I'd prefer if you didn't.* (needs *it*)

## *it's (high) time*

### The form

> ***It's time* + subject + past simple**

The past tense again means now, and the sentence means *this should have happened already*. It is a nudge, and with *high* or *about* in front of it, a complaint.

> **It's time** we **left**.  
> **It's time** you **got** a real job.  
> **It's high time** somebody **said** something.  
> **It's about time** they **fixed** that light.

Negative and question forms exist but are rare; the frame is a pronouncement, not a proposition to be argued with:

> **It's time** we **didn't** have to worry about this every month.  
> **Isn't it time** you **called** your mother?

### *It's time* + infinitive: the neutral version

Without a subject, *it's time* takes an ordinary infinitive and carries no criticism at all:

> **It's time to** go.  
> **It's time for** us **to** go.  
> **It's time to** start thinking about next year.

So the frame comes in two shapes with different jobs:

| Form | Example | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| *it's time to* + infinitive | *It's time to leave.* | plain announcement |
| *it's time for* + object + *to* | *It's time for us to leave.* | plain, with the person named |
| *it's time* + past clause | *It's time we left.* | overdue; a nudge |
| *it's high time* + past clause | *It's high time we left.* | overdue and someone is at fault |
| *it's about time* + past clause | *It's about time you asked.* | impatient, often sarcastic |

*It's about time!* on its own is a stock complaint meaning *finally*: "The plumber's here." — "**It's about time.**"

*High time* and *about time* essentially require the past clause. ✗ *It's high time to go* is possible but stilted; nobody says it.

### The errors

> ✗ *It's time we **go**.*  →  ✓ *It's time we **went**.*  
> ✗ *It's high time you **learn** to drive.*  →  ✓ *It's high time you **learned** to drive.*  
> ✗ *It's time we **will leave**.*  →  ✓ *It's time we **left**.*

One wrinkle with *be*: *It's time I **was** going* is the everyday form and *It's time I **were** going* sounds archaic — the opposite of the preference after *wish*. *It's time we were leaving* is a common, gentle way of announcing your departure.

## *as if* and *as though*

### The form

> ***as if* / *as though* + clause**

The tense of that clause decides whether the comparison is imaginary or possibly real. This is the only frame in the file where both a present and a past tense are available and both are correct — they just mean different things.

| Tense | Reading | Example |
|---|---|---|
| present | it may well be true | *He acts as if he **owns** the place.* |
| past | it is not true | *He acts as if he **owned** the place.* |
| past perfect | it was not true, about the past | *He talked as if he **had been** there.* |

> She looks **as if** she **has** something to say. (maybe she does)  
> She looks **as if** she **had** all the time in the world. (she does not)  
> He sounded **as though** he **knew** what he was talking about.  
> They act **as if** nothing **happened**. / They act **as if** nothing **had happened**.

*Were* shows up here for the same reason it does after *wish*, and it is the clearest possible signal that the comparison is unreal:

> She talks **as if** she **were** an expert.  
> He looked at me **as though** I **were** a stranger.

Negative and question:

> It's not **as if** I **had** a choice.  
> It doesn't look **as though** they**'re** coming.  
> Does he always behave **as if** he **owned** the place?

*It's not as if…* is a fixed rhetorical frame meaning *let's be fair*: *It's not as if he were trying to hurt anyone.*

When the main clause is already past, the two readings still separate cleanly: *He looked as if he **had seen** a ghost* (he had not) against *He looked as if he **was** about to cry* (he may well have been).

### *like*

In American speech, *like* replaces *as if* freely and takes the indicative:

> He acts **like** he **owns** the place.  
> It looks **like** it**'s** going to rain.  
> She sounded **like** she**'d** been crying.

This is standard spoken American English and has been for two centuries. It is still treated as informal by editors, and in formal writing *as if* or *as though* is the safe choice; before a noun phrase — *He fought like a lion* — *like* is beyond dispute, because *as if* would be ungrammatical there. Finally, *As if!* on its own, stressed on *if*, is a dismissive exclamation meaning *certainly not*: "Did you apologize?" — "**As if.**"

## *suppose*, *imagine*, *what if*

These three launch a hypothetical without an *if*-clause, and they take the same tense contrast as *as if*: present for something that could really happen, past for something remote or imaginary. *Suppose* and *unless* as full conditional connectors are in [Alternatives to *if*](03-alternatives-to-if.md); their dictionary entries are at [what if](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#what-if), [supposing that](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#supposing-that), and [say](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#say).

| Tense | Distance | Example |
|---|---|---|
| present | a real possibility, often a worry | *What if it **rains**?* |
| past | remote, imaginary, or merely polite | *What if it **rained**?* |
| past perfect | a past that did not happen | *What if it **had rained**?* |

> **Suppose** you **lost** your passport — what would you do?  
> **Imagine** you **could** live anywhere in the world.  
> **What if** she **says** no? (she might)  
> **What if** she **said** no? (thought experiment)  
> **Imagine** we **hadn't** met.  
> **Suppose** nobody **had** noticed.

The present-tense version is doing real work, not being careless. *What if the flight is canceled?* is a practical worry about a flight you are actually taking; *What if the flight were canceled?* frames the same event as a hypothetical exercise. Both are correct English.

**No *would* in the clause.** This is the same restriction that keeps *would* out of an *if*-clause ([Common mistakes](07-common-mistakes.md)):

> ✗ *What if she **would** say no?*  →  ✓ *What if she **said** no?*  
> ✗ *Suppose he **would** find out.*  →  ✓ *Suppose he **found** out.*

*Suppose* and *supposing* also float a proposal, in which case they take the present and expect an answer: *Suppose we **take** the train instead?*, *Supposing we **split** the difference?* *Say* does the same job in casual speech, as a bare conjunction: *Say you won the lottery — what's the first thing you'd buy?*

## *had better*

### The form

> ***had better* / *'d better* + bare infinitive**

*Had better* is a warning with advice inside it. The full modal entry is at [had better](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#had-better).

Three things about the form, all of them counterintuitive:

- **The *had* is not a past tense.** *Had better* refers to now or the future. There is no present ✗ *I have better*, and no third-person ✗ *he has better*.
- **The infinitive is bare.** ✗ *You'd better **to** go.*
- **The negative is *had better not*.** *Not* goes after *better*, never before *had*: ✗ *You hadn't better go.*

> You**'d better** leave now if you want a seat.  
> We**'d better** call ahead.  
> He**'d better** have a good explanation.  
> You**'d better not** be late.  
> They**'d better not** find out from someone else.

Questions are formed by inverting *had*, and they are formal and uncommon — most Americans rephrase:

> **Hadn't** we **better** check the address?  
> **Shouldn't** we check the address? (what people actually say)

In fast speech the *'d* disappears entirely: *You better go*, *You better not tell him*. Write the *'d*; expect not to hear it.

### *had better* against *should*

| | *had better* | *should* |
|---|---|---|
| Force | urgent warning; something bad follows if ignored | advice, or the right thing to do |
| Time | this occasion, now or soon | any time, including general truths |
| Generality | never general | fine for general rules |
| Politeness | can sound like a threat from a superior | neutral |
| Past | none — ✗ *I had better have gone* as a regret | *should have* + participle |

> You**'d better** take an umbrella. (it is going to rain on you)  
> You **should** take an umbrella. (sensible idea)  
> You **should** see a dentist twice a year. — not ✗ *You'd better see a dentist twice a year*, which turns routine advice into a threat.  
> You **should have** told me. — there is no *had better* version of this; for past regret the frame is *I wish you had told me*.

The threat is real enough that *had better* is risky upward: *You'd better get back to me by Friday* reads as an ultimatum to a client. Downward and sideways it is ordinary — parents, coaches, and friends use it constantly. One perfect form does exist, and it is a deadline rather than a regret: *You'd better **have finished** by the time I get back* means the finishing must be complete at a future point.

## The polite frames

The unreal past is also how English is polite. A request put in a past tense is a request framed as hypothetical, and a hypothetical request is one the listener can decline without saying no to anything real. Nothing in this section is about past time; every past form in it is a politeness marker.

### *I'd appreciate it if…*

> ***I'd appreciate it if* + subject + past simple**

The ***it* is obligatory.** This is one of the most reliable non-native tells in American business email, and the fix is a single word:

> ✗ *I would appreciate if you could send the file.*  
> ✓ *I**'d appreciate it if** you **could** send the file.*  
> ✓ *I**'d appreciate it if** you **called** me back today.*  
> ✓ *We**'d appreciate it if** you **didn't** discuss this outside the team.*

The negative version — *I'd appreciate it if you wouldn't…* — is the polished way to ask somebody to stop, and it is noticeably colder than *I wish you wouldn't*: *I'd appreciate it if you wouldn't park in my spot.*

### *I'd be grateful if…*

> ***I'd be grateful if* + subject + *would* / *could* + bare infinitive**

More formal than *appreciate*, and standard in letters. Unlike a true conditional *if*-clause, this one **takes *would*** — the volitional *would* of willingness, not the future:

> I**'d be grateful if** you **would** confirm by Friday.  
> We**'d be grateful if** you **could** reconsider.  
> I**'d be very grateful if** someone **could** look into this.

### *If you wouldn't mind…*

*Mind* means "object to," so the negative is what makes it polite, and the complement is an `-ing` form or a clause, never an infinitive:

> ***If you wouldn't mind*** waiting here, I'll go get him.  
> Could you sign both copies, **if you wouldn't mind**?  
> **Would you mind** holding the door?  
> **Would you mind if** I **opened** a window?  
> **Do you mind if** I **open** a window? (same request, marginally less formal)

Two traps. ✗ *Would you mind to wait* is ungrammatical — the verb takes `-ing`. And the answer is inverted: *Would you mind moving your car?* — ***Not at all**, let me get my keys.* Saying *yes* to a *mind* question means you object.

### The rest of the family

| Frame | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *I was wondering if…* | *I **was wondering if** you **could** take a look.* | Past continuous for distance; the wondering is happening now. |
| *It would be great if…* | *It **would be** great **if** you **could** join us.* | The most neutral of the set. |
| *Would it be possible to…* | ***Would it be possible to** move the meeting?* | Removes the person entirely. |
| *I'd rather you didn't* | *"Can I smoke in here?" — "I**'d rather** you **didn't**."* | A refusal with no *no* in it. |

## Every frame and the tense it takes

| Frame | What follows | Time it refers to | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *wish* | past simple | now, or a fixed future | *I wish I **knew**.* |
| *wish* | past perfect | finished past | *I wish I **had listened**.* |
| *wish* | *would* + bare infinitive | now — someone else's behavior | *I wish you **would** listen.* |
| *wish* | *could* + bare infinitive | now — my inability | *I wish I **could** help.* |
| *wish* | *could have* + participle | past inability | *I wish I **could have helped**.* |
| *hope* | present simple or *will* | future, still open | *I hope it **works**.* |
| *hope* | past simple | past, outcome unknown | *I hope I **didn't** wake you.* |
| *if only* | past, past perfect, *would*, *could* | all three, plus standalone | *If only she **had told** me.* |
| *would rather* | bare infinitive | same subject, now or future | *I'd rather **stay**.* |
| *would rather* | *have* + participle | same subject, past | *I'd rather **have stayed**.* |
| *would rather* | past simple, or past perfect, clause | different subject, now or past | *I'd rather you **stayed**.* |
| *it's time* | *to* + infinitive | now — neutral | *It's time **to** go.* |
| *it's (high / about) time* | past simple clause | overdue, with a nudge | *It's high time we **left**.* |
| *as if* / *as though* | present | possibly true | *He acts as if he **owns** it.* |
| *as if* / *as though* | past simple | not true, now | *He acts as if he **owned** it.* |
| *as if* / *as though* | past perfect | not true, in the past | *He talked as if he **had been** there.* |
| *suppose* / *imagine* / *what if* | present | a real possibility | *What if it **rains**?* |
| *suppose* / *imagine* / *what if* | past, or past perfect | remote, or a past that did not happen | *What if it **rained**?* |
| *had better* | bare infinitive | now or future — a warning | *You'd better **leave**.* |
| *I'd appreciate it if* | past simple | now — polite request | *I'd appreciate it if you **called**.* |
| *I'd be grateful if* | *would* / *could* | now — polite request | *I'd be grateful if you **would** confirm.* |
| *would you mind* | `-ing`, or *if* + past | now — polite request | *Would you mind if I **sat** here?* |

## What Russian speakers get wrong most

Russian marks unreality **once**, with the particle *бы* plus a past-tense verb, and that single marking covers every time frame at once. English marks it **three** ways, and picking among them is not optional. Nearly every error in this file traces back to that mismatch.

1. **The three tenses that Russian collapses into one.** *Жаль, что я не знал* and *жаль, что я не знаю* differ only in context; English forces the choice. *I wish I **knew*** is about now; *I wish I **had known*** is about a past you cannot repair; *I wish you **would** tell me* is a complaint about behavior. Getting the time frame wrong is not a style problem — ✗ *I wish I didn't say that* is a sentence about your habits, not about last night.

2. **The negative goes on the opposite clause.** Russian states the fact and calls it regrettable: *жаль, что я не знаю немецкий* — "a pity that I do **not** know German." English states the wish and leaves the fact implied: *I wish I **did** know German* → *I wish I **knew** German*. The Russian negative must be **dropped**, and the English negative goes on whatever you are glad is not so. ✗ *I wish I don't know German* and ✗ *I wish I didn't know German* both mean the reverse of what is intended.

3. **✗ *I wish I would…*** — Russian *хотел бы* covers both *I'd like* and *I wish*, so *хотел бы получить эту работу* comes out as ✗ *I wish I would get the job*. English: *I **hope** I get the job* if it is possible, *I **wish** I **could*** if it is not.

4. **✗ *I wish you will…*** — *надеюсь* maps onto *hope*, not *wish*, and *hope* takes a present tense for the future: *I **hope** you get better soon*, never ✗ *I wish you will get better soon*.

5. **✗ *It's time we go.*** — *пора идти* has an infinitive, so the English past clause feels wrong. Both shapes exist: *It's time **to** go* with the infinitive, *It's time we **went*** with the clause. There is no ✗ *It's time we go* in between.

6. **✗ *I'd rather you don't.*** — *лучше бы ты не делал этого* is one *бы* + past; English splits it by subject. Same subject takes a bare infinitive (*I'd rather not go*), a different subject takes a past tense (*I'd rather you didn't go*), and the past takes the past perfect (*I'd rather you hadn't gone*).

7. ***Лучше бы* has three English translations, and they are not interchangeable.** *You'd better leave* is a warning about the future. *I'd rather you left* is a preference. *I wish you had left* is a regret. Russian uses *лучше бы* for all three.

8. **✗ *He acts as if he owns the place*** when you mean he does not. *Как будто* takes an indicative in Russian, so the unreal past after *as if* has to be added consciously: *as if he **owned** the place*.

9. **No *бы* in the *if*-half.** Russian puts *бы* in both clauses of a conditional; English never puts *would* in the subordinate clause of these frames. ✗ *What if she would say no*, ✗ *I wish it would be warmer* (→ *were*), ✗ *Suppose he would find out*.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I wish I **know** the answer.* | *I wish I **knew** the answer.* | *Wish* never takes a present tense. |
| ✗ *I wish I **didn't say** that.* | *I wish I **hadn't said** that.* | A regret about a finished event takes the past perfect. |
| ✗ *I wish I **would** get the job.* | *I **hope** I get the job.* | You cannot wish that you yourself *would* do something. |
| ✗ *I wish you **will** call.* | *I **hope** you call.* / *I wish you **would** call.* | *Will* cannot follow *wish* at all. |
| ✗ *I wish I **can** help.* | *I wish I **could** help.* | Every verb in the clause takes its past form, modals included. |
| ✗ *I wish he **would be** taller.* | *I wish he **were** taller.* | *Would* needs a changeable action, not a permanent state. |
| ✗ *I hope I **were** taller.* | *I **wish** I were taller.* | *Hope* is for what may be true; *wish* is for what is not. |
| ✗ *I **don't hope** so.* | *I **hope not**.* | *Hope* forms its negative answer with *not*, not with *don't*. |
| ✗ *I wish **to have** more time.* | *I wish I **had** more time.* | *Wish to* + infinitive is formal *want*, a different frame. |
| ✗ *It's time we **go**.* &middot; ✗ *It's high time **to** leave.* | *It's time we **went**.* &middot; *It's high time we **left**.* | The clause version takes the past; *high time* and *about time* want the clause. |
| ✗ *I'd rather you **don't**.* | *I'd rather you **didn't**.* | A different subject after *would rather* takes a past tense. |
| ✗ *I'd rather **to** stay.* &middot; ✗ *You'd better **to** go.* | *I'd rather **stay**.* &middot; *You'd better **go**.* | Both are semi-modals: bare infinitive, never *to*. |
| ✗ *You **hadn't better** go.* | *You'd better **not** go.* | *Not* follows *better*. |
| ✗ *What if she **would** say no?* | *What if she **said** no?* | No *would* in the hypothetical clause. |
| ✗ *I would appreciate **if** you called.* | *I'd appreciate **it if** you called.* | The *it* is not optional. |
| ✗ *Would you mind **to wait**?* | *Would you mind **waiting**?* | *Mind* takes an `-ing` form. |

## Quick reference

**Decide in three questions.**

1. **Is it still possible?** Yes → *hope* + present. No → read on.
2. **Is it finished?** Yes → *wish* / *if only* + past perfect. No → read on.
3. **Can somebody change it?** Yes, somebody else → *wish* + *would*. No, it is a state or my own inability → *wish* + past, or *wish* + *could*.

**The four sentences to hold on to.**

> I **hope** you**'re** right. (you may be)  
> I **wish** you **were** right. (you are not)  
> I **wish** I **had known**. (too late)  
> I **wish** you **would** stop. (you can, so stop)

**The frames, in one line each.** *wish* + past / past perfect / *would* / *could* &middot; *if only* the same, plus standalone &middot; *would rather* + bare infinitive, or + past clause for someone else &middot; *it's time* + past clause, or *to* + infinitive &middot; *as if* + past for unreal, present for possible &middot; *suppose* / *what if* + past for remote &middot; *had better* + bare infinitive &middot; *I'd appreciate it if* + past.

Fix or complete each one. Some are already correct.

1. I wish I ______ (know) what to do.
2. I wish I ______ (listen) to you last year.
3. I wish it ______ (stop) snowing.
4. I wish I ______ (can) come to the party.
5. ✗ I wish I would win the lottery.
6. ✗ I don't hope so.

8. ✗ It's time we go.
9. ✗ It's high time to fix that door.
10. ✗ I'd rather you don't tell him.
11. I'd rather you ______ (not / say) anything yesterday.

13. ✗ You'd better to call her.

15. ✗ Would you mind to close the window?
16. ✗ I wish I didn't send that email last night.

| # | Answer |
|---|---|
| 1 | I wish I **knew** what to do. |
| 2 | I wish I **had listened** to you last year. (Past regret — past perfect.) |
| 3 | I wish it **would** stop snowing. (A change you want; *wish it stopped* would be a complaint about a habit.) |
| 4 | I wish I **could** come to the party. |
| 5 | I **hope** I win the lottery. (You cannot wish that you yourself *would*.) |
| 6 | I **hope not**. |
| 7 | If only she **had told** me before she signed it. |
| 8 | It's time we **went**. (Or *It's time to go*.) |
| 9 | It's high time **we fixed** that door. (*High time* wants the clause.) |
| 10 | I'd rather you **didn't** tell him. |
| 11 | I'd rather you **hadn't said** anything yesterday. |
| 12 | What if she **refused**? (Or *refuses*, if the refusal is a live worry. Never *would refuse*.) |

| 14 | I'd appreciate **it** if you could confirm. |
