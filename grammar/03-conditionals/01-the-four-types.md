# The four types

> Zero, first, second and third — the form of each, what each one claims about reality, and every shape the two clauses are allowed to take.

[← Conditionals](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

A conditional sentence is two clauses locked together. One states a condition — the ***if*-clause** — and the other states what follows from it — the **result clause**. Either half can come first, and the sentence means the same thing in both orders:

> **If it rains, we'll stay home.**  
> **We'll stay home if it rains.**

The *if*-clause is **unasserted**. The speaker is not claiming it is true, only saying what follows if it is. *If the check cleared, we're fine* tells you nothing about whether the check cleared, while *Because the check cleared, we're fine* tells you it did. That single property is what makes the condition clause behave so strangely: it describes a situation the speaker is holding at arm's length, and English marks that distance with **tense**.

Here is the idea the whole topic rests on, stated once and then used everywhere below.

**In a conditional, the tense of the verb is not measuring time. It is measuring distance from reality.** Each step further back in tense buys one step further away from what is actually the case. *If it rains* is open. *If it rained* is unlikely. *If it had rained* is over and did not happen. Nothing in that sequence is about when.

That is the same **unreal past** that appears after *wish*, *if only*, *it's time*, and *would rather* — a past-tense form doing a job that has nothing to do with past time. It is treated from the other side in [When not to backshift](../01-reported-speech/03-no-backshift.md), where the point is that a form already doing unreal work cannot be shifted again.

## The four types in one table

| Type | *If*-clause | Result clause | What it claims | Time referred to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zero** | present simple | present simple | this is always how it works | general — no particular occasion |
| **First** | present simple | *will* + base form | this may well happen | future (occasionally present) |
| **Second** | past simple | *would* + base form | this is not the case, or is unlikely | present and future |
| **Third** | past perfect | *would have* + past participle | this did not happen, and cannot now | past |

Laid out as a ladder, with one rung of unreality bought per step:

```
        the if-clause            the result            the type
        ---------------------------------------------------------------
   0    present          +      present          →     zero     always true
   1    present          +      will             →     first    real, still open
   2    past             +      would            →     second   unreal now
   3    past perfect     +      would have       →     third    unreal, and settled
```

Read down the left column and you are reading the ordinary tense ladder — present, past, past perfect. Read down the right column and you are reading probability falling from certainty to impossibility. The two columns move together, and that coupling is the system.

The numbers are a teaching convenience, not a fact about English. Real sentences mix the halves — a type 3 condition with a type 2 result is ordinary and correct — and several conditional patterns have no number at all. This section covers the four standard types exhaustively. **Mixed types and the variations on each pattern are [section 2](02-mixed-and-variations.md); the conjunctions other than *if* are [section 3](03-alternatives-to-if.md); inversion without *if* — *Had I known…* — is [section 4](04-inversion-and-formal.md); and the *wish* family is [section 5](05-wishes-and-regrets.md).**

## The comma rule

One rule, all four types, no exceptions worth the name.

**Comma when the *if*-clause comes first. No comma when it comes second.**

> **If** you need anything**,** let me know.  
> Let me know **if** you need anything.

> **If** I'd known**,** I would have called.  
> I would have called **if** I'd known.

The comma is not marking the condition. It is marking the end of an introductory element, which is why it disappears the moment the clause stops being introductory. The same rule governs *when*, *because*, *although*, and every other fronted subordinate clause in English.

Two footnotes, neither of which changes the rule:

- A trailing *if*-clause that is an afterthought, a contrast, or very long can take a comma: *We'll cancel the whole trip, if it comes to that.* This is a choice about pacing, not about grammar, and the default remains no comma.
- **Never put a comma before *if* when it introduces a noun clause**: *I don't know if he's coming*, not ✗ *I don't know, if he's coming*. That *if* means *whether* and is not a conditional at all. It is a [complementizer](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md).

Which order to choose is a matter of emphasis. Fronting the condition sets the scene and gives the condition weight; ending with it makes the result the main event and the condition a qualification. Both are equally standard, and long stretches of writing alternate between them.

## Zero conditional

### The form

| Slot | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if*-clause | present simple | *If you heat water to 212 degrees,* |
| result clause | present simple | *it boils.* |

Both halves in the present, both halves asserted as real. The *if*-clause can also be present continuous or present perfect; the result can be an imperative. Everything else stays put.

### What it means

The zero conditional states a **reliable link between two real situations**. It does not point at an occasion. It says: whenever the first is true, the second is true. That is why *if* here can almost always be replaced by *when* or *whenever* with no change of meaning.

**Time reference: none in particular.** The present tense in both halves is a genuine present — the timeless present of general statements, the same one in *water boils at 212 degrees* and *the store opens at nine*. Nothing is unreal, nothing is shifted, and both clauses could stand alone as true sentences about the world.

### General truths and scientific facts

> **If** you heat water to 212 degrees**,** it boils.  
> Metal expands **if** you heat it.

### Habits and repeated behavior

> **If** I skip lunch**,** I get a headache.  
> She calls her mother **if** she has a long drive.

### Rules, policies, and how things work

> **If** a package weighs over ten pounds**,** it ships separately.  
> **If** the balance isn't paid in thirty days**,** a late fee applies.

### Instructions, with an imperative result

The result clause of a zero conditional is often a command. This is where the pattern earns its keep in manuals, recipes, and directions.

> **If** the light turns red**,** press reset.  
> **If** you smell gas**,** don't touch the switch.

### Negatives

The negative can sit in either clause or both, and it is formed exactly as it would be in a standalone sentence — *doesn't*, *don't*, *isn't*, *aren't*.

> **If** you **don't** water it**,** it dies.  
> **If** you water it too much**,** it **doesn't** bloom.

### Questions and negative questions

The question is formed on the result clause; the *if*-clause is untouched.

> **Does** it hurt **if** you press here?  
> **What happens if** I click this?  
> **Doesn't** it bother you **if** people take calls at the table?

### Contracted forms

Zero conditionals live in speech, and in speech they contract everywhere the grammar allows.

> **If you're** cold**, there's** a blanket in the closet.  
> **If he's** driving**, he doesn't** drink.

### Passive

Either clause can be passive, or both. The rule is unchanged: present tense throughout.

> **If** a bill **is not paid** within thirty days**,** a late fee **is added**.  
> **If** the form **is signed** in the wrong place**,** it **is returned**.

### Continuous

The present continuous appears freely, usually to mark that the condition is in progress rather than habitual.

> **If I'm driving,** I don't answer the phone.  
> **If the baby is sleeping,** we keep the TV off.

### *if* vs. *when* in the zero conditional

This is the one place in the whole conditional system where *if* and *when* genuinely overlap, and it is worth being precise about where the overlap stops.

| | *if* | *when* |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic claim** | on the occasions this is true | at the times this is true |
| **General truth** | *If you heat ice, it melts.* | *When you heat ice, it melts.* — identical |
| **Habit** | *If I skip lunch, I get a headache.* | *When I skip lunch, I get a headache.* — identical |
| **Single future event** | *If I see her, I'll tell her.* — I may not see her | *When I see her, I'll tell her.* — I will see her |
| **Something that may never happen** | *If I win the lottery…* | ✗ *When I win the lottery…* — only as a joke |

**In a zero conditional they are interchangeable**, because a general truth has no particular occasion for the speaker to be uncertain about. *If you press this button, the light comes on* and *When you press this button, the light comes on* say the same thing.

**In a first conditional they are not**, because there the sentence is about one occasion and the choice reports whether the speaker expects it. *When the results come in, I'll call you* commits you to the results coming in. *If the results come in, I'll call you* does not. Choosing *if* where the listener expects *when* is a way of casting doubt, and it is heard.

*Whenever* is *when* with the generality made explicit, and is always safe in a zero conditional: *Whenever I skip lunch, I get a headache.* The full comparison with the other conditional conjunctions is in [subordinators of condition](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md).

### Zero or first?

If the sentence is about **one occasion in the future**, it is a first conditional and the result takes *will*. If it is about **how things go in general**, it is a zero conditional and the result stays in the present.

> **If it rains, the game is canceled.** — the standing rule; it is in the league handbook  
> **If it rains, the game will be canceled.** — this Saturday's game, this particular rain

## First conditional

### The form

| Slot | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if*-clause | present simple | *If it rains tomorrow,* |
| result clause | *will* + base form | *we'll cancel the picnic.* |

The *if*-clause is present **even though the meaning is future**. This is the single most-violated rule in the topic and it has its own section below.

The slots are wider than the table suggests. The *if*-clause also takes the present continuous, the present perfect, and the present perfect continuous. The result clause takes *will*, *be going to*, the present continuous, any modal, the future continuous, the future perfect, or an imperative.

### What it means

The first conditional describes a **real, open possibility** and says what will follow from it. The speaker is treating the condition as genuinely on the table — likely enough to plan around, uncertain enough to need an *if*.

**Time reference: the future**, nearly always. The condition is a future event and the result is a later future event. A present-time first conditional is possible when the condition is about something unknown right now — *If he's in his office, he'll answer* — but the pattern is built for the future.

### *will* and *won't*, both orders

> **If** it rains tomorrow**, we'll** cancel the picnic.  
> **We'll** cancel the picnic **if** it rains tomorrow.  
> **If** you leave now**, you'll** make the 6:40.  
> **You'll** make the 6:40 **if** you leave now.

### Negatives, in one clause or both

> **If** you **don't** hurry**,** we**'ll** be late.  
> **If** you **don't** hurry**,** we **won't** get a table.  
> **I won't** say anything **if** you **don't** want me to.

### Contracted forms as they are actually said

Nobody says *I will call you*. The contraction is the default, and the full form is emphatic or formal.

> **If** you need anything**, I'll** be right here.  
> **If** it rains**, we'll** move it inside.

The *'ll* is nearly inaudible in fast speech — *we'll move it* is one syllable away from *we move it* — which is why the future often disappears from what a learner hears. That reduction is treated in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

### Questions

The question is formed on the result clause. The *if*-clause keeps statement word order no matter which end of the sentence it sits at.

> **Will** you call me **if** anything changes?  
> **If** anything changes**, will** you call me?  
> **What will** you do **if** they say no?

### Negative questions

Negative questions in a conditional carry a strong expectation — the speaker thinks the answer is yes and is half-arguing for it.

> **Won't** you be cold **if** you wear that?  
> **If** I'm late**, won't** they start without me?

### *be going to* in the result

*Be going to* replaces *will* wherever the speaker is pointing at present evidence or a plan that already exists.

> **If** you keep skipping practice**, you're going to** get cut.  
> **If** he drives like that**, he's going to** hit something.

The difference from *will* is the same one it always is: *will* is a prediction made at the moment of speaking, *be going to* reports something already visible or already decided. *If you drop that, it'll break* is a prediction; *If you keep tilting it, it's going to spill* is a warning based on what the speaker can see.

### Present continuous as a future result

A fixed arrangement keeps its present continuous inside a conditional.

> **If** the weather holds**, we're leaving** at six.  
> **If** the sale goes through**, they're moving** in August.

### Modals in the result clause

Anything that can head a main clause can head the result of a first conditional. This table is the inventory.

| Result form | What it adds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [*will*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#will) / *won't* | plain prediction | *If you leave now, you'll make it.* |
| *be going to* | present evidence or an existing plan | *If you keep that up, you're going to break it.* |
| present continuous | a fixed arrangement | *If the weather holds, we're leaving at six.* |
| [*may*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#may) | possibility; permission | *If you ask her nicely, she may say yes.* |
| [*might*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#might) | weaker possibility | *If we hurry, we might still catch it.* |
| [*can*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#can) | permission; ability | *If you've finished, you can go.* |
| [*could*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#could) | possibility; a suggestion | *If we miss the 6:40, we could take the bus.* |
| [*should*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#should) / [*ought to*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#ought-to) | advice; expectation | *If it still hurts tomorrow, you should see someone.* |
| [*must*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#must) / *have to* | obligation | *If you want the discount, you must apply by Friday.* |
| [*had better*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#had-better) | urgent advice | *If you're driving, you'd better leave now.* |
| imperative | an instruction | *If it rings, answer it.* |
| future continuous | in progress at a future moment | *If you call at nine, I'll be driving.* |
| future perfect | finished before a future moment | *If you don't hurry, the store will have closed.* |
| present simple | a schedule or a standing rule | *If the flight is delayed, the connection leaves without us.* |

The complete inventory of what each modal means, and how it behaves in negatives and questions, is in [modal, semi-modal and defective verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md).

### Imperative results

An imperative result turns the conditional into a conditional instruction. The *if*-clause is unchanged; the result simply has no subject.

> **If** it rings**, answer** it.  
> **If** you see him**, don't say** anything.  
> **Call** me **if** you get lost.

A *let's* result works the same way: *If it's still raining at noon, let's just stay in.*

### *if* + present perfect

The present perfect in the *if*-clause insists that the condition is **complete** before the result begins.

> **If you've finished,** you can go.  
> **If she hasn't called** by six**,** we'll go without her.

Compare *If you finish, you can go* with *If you've finished, you can go*. They are close, and in casual speech either will do. The perfect makes completion the point, which is why it is the natural choice with *already*, *yet*, and *by then*. It is never wrong to use it where the completion matters, and never required where it does not.

The present perfect continuous appears too, though less often: *If you've been waiting more than twenty minutes, tell the desk.*

### *if* + present continuous

The present continuous in the *if*-clause covers an action in progress, or a future arrangement.

> **If you're looking** for Dana**,** she's in the back.  
> **If you're driving** tonight**,** don't drink.  
> **If she's flying** on Sunday**,** she'll have to leave early.  
> **I'll bring you dinner if you're still working** at six.

Note *if she's flying on Sunday*: a future arrangement sitting in the *if*-clause — a present continuous with future meaning, allowed where *will* is not, because the form is grammatically present.

### Passive

> **If** the offer **is accepted,** we'll close on the fifteenth.  
> **If** you don't pay by Friday**,** your account **will be suspended**.  
> **If** the shipment **has been delayed,** we'll hear about it today.

### Continuous results

> **If** you call at nine**, I'll be driving.**  
> **This time next week, if all goes well, we'll be sitting on a beach.**

## Second conditional

### The form

| Slot | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if*-clause | past simple (*were* for all persons with *be*) | *If I had more time,* |
| result clause | *would* + base form | *I'd learn Spanish.* |

The past tense here is **unreal past**. It is not reporting anything that happened.

### What it means

The second conditional describes a situation that is **not the case**, or that the speaker considers **unlikely to become the case**, and says what would follow from it. Two uses that look different are the same grammar:

- **Contrary to fact now.** *If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish.* I do not have more time.
- **Unlikely in the future.** *If they offered me the job, I'd take it.* They probably will not offer it.

**Time reference: now and the future.** Nothing about the second conditional points backward. *If I lived in Chicago* is about where I live today; *If it rained tomorrow* is about tomorrow. The past tense is a marker of unreality, and it has been borrowed for that job from the tense system, which is exactly why it confuses people.

The contrast with the first conditional is **probability, not time**. Both of these are about next Saturday:

> **If it rains, we'll cancel.** — rain is a real prospect  
> **If it rained, we'd cancel.** — rain is not expected; this is close to hypothetical

Neither one is more polite, more formal, or further in the future than the other. The second simply puts the condition further from reality.

### Affirmatives, both orders

> **If** I had more time**, I'd** learn Spanish.  
> **I'd** learn Spanish **if** I had more time.  
> **If** he called her**, she'd** forgive him.  
> **She'd** forgive him **if** he called her.

### Negatives

> **If** I **didn't** have to work**, I'd** come with you.  
> **If** I had a car**, I wouldn't** need a ride.  
> **If** it **weren't** so far**, we'd** drive.

### Questions and negative questions

> **What would** you do **if** you lost your job?  
> **Would** you move **if** they offered you the job?  
> **If** money were no object**, where would** you live?  
> **Wouldn't** it be easier **if** we just drove?  
> **If** you didn't have to work**, wouldn't** you travel more?

### Contracted forms as they are actually said

The *'d* of *would* is the most-used contraction in the pattern, and it attaches to every pronoun.

> **If** I had the money**, I'd** buy it.  
> **If** you asked him**, he'd** say yes.

Two warnings about *'d*. First, written *'d* is ambiguous between *would* and *had*; in a second conditional it is always *would*, because a base form follows (*he'd say*), while *had* is followed by a past participle (*he'd said*). Second, *wouldn't* is the only negative — there is no ✗ *would not've* in a second conditional, because no perfect is involved.

### *was* or *were*: the honest version

*Were* in *If I were* is the last surviving piece of the English past subjunctive. It is invariant across all persons — *if I were*, *if he were*, *if the price were*, *if there were* — and it exists only in this unreal context.

> **If I were** taller**, I'd** play center.  
> **If he were** serious about it**, he'd** have said so by now.  
> **If there were** a faster way**, someone would** have found it.

And the honest part: **in American English, *if I was* is extremely common in speech and passes unremarked.** *If I was taller, I'd play center* is what a great many Americans say, and hardly anyone hears it as an error.

Here is how the choice actually distributes:

| Context | Form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Casual speech | *if I was* or *if I were* | Both normal. *Was* is probably more common. |
| Edited writing | *if I were* | The expected form; *was* will be changed by an editor. |
| Formal or academic prose | *if I were* | Effectively required. |
| The fixed advice formula | ***If I were you*** | Fixed. ✗ *If I was you* reads as dialect. |
| Inversion without *if* | ***Were I*** *in charge…* | Only *were* inverts. There is no ✗ *Was I in charge…* |
| *as if* / *as though* | *as if he were* / *as if he was* | Same split as above. |

The advice formula deserves its own line, because it is the single highest-frequency second conditional in the language and it does not tolerate *was*:

> **If I were you, I'd** get that in writing.  
> **If I were you, I wouldn't** mention it.

There is a second trap here, and it is the reverse of the usual one. **Not every *if I was* is a failed subjunctive.** When the *if*-clause is about something that really may have happened in the past, it is a real condition, not a second conditional, and *was* is the only correct form:

> **If I was** rude last night**, I apologize.** — maybe I was; I don't remember  
> ✗ *If I were rude last night, I apologize.*

> **If he was** at the meeting**, he already knows.** — a real, open question about the past  
> ✗ *If he were at the meeting, he already knows.*

The test is whether the condition could turn out to be true. If it could, it is real, and *was* is right. If it could not — if the speaker already knows it is false — the subjunctive *were* is available.

### *could* and *might* in the result

*Would* is the default, and two other modals substitute for it with a shift of meaning.

| Result modal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *would* | this is what would happen | *If I had the money, I'd buy it.* |
| *could* | this would be possible; I would be able to | *If I had the money, I could buy it.* |
| *might* | this would perhaps happen | *If I had the money, I might buy it.* |

> **If** we left now**, we could** be there by three.  
> **If** you asked him directly**, he might** tell you.

*Should*, *must*, and *may* do not appear in the result of a second conditional. The unreal result clause takes *would*, *could*, or *might* and nothing else.

### Passive

> **If** I **were offered** the job**, I'd** take it.  
> **If** the bill **were passed,** millions of people **would be affected**.  
> **If** this **were handled** properly**,** we **wouldn't be having** this conversation.

### Continuous

The continuous appears in either clause: *were* + *-ing* in the condition, *would be* + *-ing* in the result.

> **If I weren't working** tomorrow**, I'd** come with you.  
> **If** we left now**, we'd be sitting** on the beach by noon.

### The other job: politeness

The unreal form also makes a request less direct, and therefore more polite. The grammar is identical, and nothing about it is hypothetical — these are requests the speaker expects to be granted.

> **It would be great if you could** send that over today.  
> **Would you mind if I opened** a window?  
> **I'd appreciate it if you didn't** mention this.

## Third conditional

### The form

| Slot | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if*-clause | past perfect (*had* + past participle) | *If I had known,* |
| result clause | *would have* + past participle | *I would have called.* |

Both halves are as far back as English goes, which is why nothing in a third conditional can be shifted any further — including in reported speech, where it survives untouched.

### What it means

The third conditional describes a **past situation that did not happen** and the past result that therefore also did not happen. It is the only type where the speaker knows for certain that the condition was false. Every third conditional carries a matching pair of facts:

> *If I had known, I would have called.* → I did not know. I did not call.  
> *If she hadn't helped us, we would have missed the deadline.* → She helped. We did not miss it.

Note the second one: **negating the condition flips the fact.** A negative *if*-clause means the thing did happen. This is the part learners misread most often.

**Time reference: the past, and only the past.** Both the condition and its result are over. When the *result* has spilled into the present — *If I'd studied medicine, I'd be a doctor now* — the sentence has become a [mixed conditional](02-mixed-and-variations.md#mixed-conditionals).

### Affirmatives, both orders

> **If** I **had known,** I **would have** called.  
> **I would have** called **if** I **had known.**  
> **If** we **had left** earlier**, we would have** made the train.  
> **We would have** made the train **if** we **had left** earlier.

### Negatives

> **If** I **hadn't** missed the exit**, we would have** been on time.  
> **If** you **had** told me**, I wouldn't have** worried.  
> **She wouldn't have** signed it **if** she **had read** it.

### Questions and negative questions

> **What would** you **have done if** you'd been there?  
> **Would** you **have told** me **if** I hadn't asked?  
> **Wouldn't** it **have been** easier to call?  
> **Wouldn't** you **have said** something **if** you'd known?

### Contracted forms as they are actually said

This is where the pattern gets hard to hear, and where nearly all of its written errors come from.

> **If I'd** known**, I'd have** called.  
> **If she'd** asked**, I'd have** said yes.

Look at *If I'd known, I'd have called*. The same two letters appear twice and mean different things: the first *'d* is *had* and the second is *would*. The word after it tells you which — a past participle alone means *had*, and *have* + participle means *would*.

In speech *would have* reduces to *would've* /ˈwʊdəv/, and *wouldn't have* to /ˈwʊdn̩t‿əv/. Two consequences:

- ✗ ***would of*** is not a spelling variant; it is a misheard word. *Of* is never a verb. The same goes for ✗ *could of*, ✗ *should of*, ✗ *might of*. See [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).
- ***If I'd've known*** is what a great many Americans actually say — an extra *'ve* in the *if*-clause, echoing the one in the result. It is genuinely widespread in speech across the country. It is **not written**, and in anything edited it is corrected to *If I'd known*.

A third form belongs with those two, and it needs to be flagged clearly because it is so common: ✗ ***If I would have known***. In some regional American speech this is ordinary. In writing, and in any formal setting, it is a clear error — *would* does not belong in the *if*-clause of any conditional type. Use *If I had known*.

### *could have* and *might have* in the result

| Result form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *would have* | this is what would have happened | *If you'd asked, I would have helped.* |
| *could have* | this would have been possible | *If you'd asked, I could have helped.* |
| *might have* | this would perhaps have happened | *If you'd asked, I might have helped.* |
| *would never have* | emphatic negative | *If I'd known, I would never have agreed.* |
| *would still have* | the result was unavoidable anyway | *If we'd left at six, we'd still have hit traffic.* |

> **If** we'd caught the earlier flight**, we could have** been home by eight.  
> **If** you'd said something**, I might have** changed my mind.

### Passive

> **If** the alarm **had been set,** the fire **would have been** contained.  
> **If** I **had been told** earlier**, I would have** rearranged everything.

### Continuous

> **If I hadn't been driving,** I **would have answered**.  
> **If** we'd left on time**, we would have been eating** dinner in Santa Fe by eight.

### What the third conditional is used for

The grammar is one thing; the social work it does is another, and it is narrow enough to list.

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Regret | *If I'd taken that job, I would have been happier.* |
| Criticism or blame | *If you'd checked the address, we wouldn't have driven around for an hour.* |
| Excuse or explanation | *If the road hadn't been closed, I would have been on time.* |
| Relief | *If we'd taken the highway, we would have sat in traffic all afternoon.* |

The criticism row is the one to watch. *If you had checked…* is rarely a neutral observation; in most contexts it is an accusation with a grammatical wrapper, and speakers soften it — *If any of us had checked…* — when they do not mean it that way.

## Why ✗ *If it will rain* is wrong

**The *if*-clause takes a present form even when its meaning is future.** *If it rains tomorrow* is about tomorrow, and it is still the present simple.

> ✗ *If it **will rain** tomorrow, we'll cancel.*  
> ✓ *If it **rains** tomorrow, we'll cancel.*

> ✗ *If I **will have** time, I'll call you.*  
> ✓ *If I **have** time, I'll call you.*

The reason is that English marks the future **once per sentence**, and the main clause is where the mark goes. The subordinate clause is already located in the future by the main clause, so repeating the marker adds nothing and English rejects it. This is not a fact about *if* — the same rule governs every future time clause:

> I'll call you **when I get** home. — ✗ *when I will get home*  
> We'll start **as soon as she arrives**. — ✗ *as soon as she will arrive*  
> Wait here **until I come back**. — ✗ *until I will come back*

The same constraint is what keeps *would* out of the *if*-clause of a second conditional (✗ *If I would know*) and *would have* out of a third (✗ *If I would have known*). One clause carries the modal; the other carries the tense.

### The narrow cases where *if* + *will* IS correct

Three of them, and in all three *will* is doing something other than marking the future.

**1. Willingness — *will* meaning "be willing to."** This is the oldest sense of *will*, and it survives in polite formulas.

> **If you will wait here,** someone will be with you shortly.  
> **If you'll excuse me,** I have a call to make.  
> **If you would sign** at the bottom, we're finished.

The *would* version is the politer one and is extremely common at a service desk. Note that none of these is hypothetical; the clerk fully expects you to wait.

**2. Insistence — stressed *will*, meaning "if you insist on doing it."** The tone is exasperated, and the *will* is never contracted.

> **If you will smoke,** expect a cough.  
> **If you will leave the door open,** don't complain about the draft.

**3. The *if*-clause names a consequence, not a condition.** Here the future is genuine, because the clause describes what the main clause would bring about.

> **If it will make you feel better,** I'll call him. — the calling produces the feeling better  
> **If it'll help,** I can drive.

There is also a fourth *if* that looks like a violation and is not: *if* meaning **whether**, introducing a noun clause rather than a condition.

> I don't know **if it will rain**.  
> Ask her **if she'll be there**.

That *if* is a complementizer, the clause is the object of the verb, no comma is involved, and no conditional rule applies to it at all. It is catalogued with the [complementizers](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md), not with the [conditional subordinators](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if).

All four cases, with the tests for telling them apart from the error, belong to [*If* + *will* and *if* + *would*](02-mixed-and-variations.md#if--will-and-if--would) in section 2. The rule to carry out of this section is the plain one: **in a conditional, the *if*-clause does not take *will*.**

## The four types side by side

| | **Zero** | **First** | **Second** | **Third** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *If*-clause | present simple | present simple | past simple / *were* | past perfect |
| Result clause | present simple | *will* + base | *would* + base | *would have* + participle |
| Time referred to | any — general | future | present and future | past |
| Is the condition real? | yes, always | yes, open | no, or unlikely | no, and settled |
| Did it happen? | it happens every time | not yet known | it is not the case | it did not happen |
| Other results allowed | imperative, present | *going to*, present continuous, any modal, imperative, future perfect, future continuous | *could*, *might* | *could have*, *might have* |
| Other *if*-clauses allowed | present continuous, present perfect | present continuous, present perfect, present perfect continuous | past continuous, *were* passive | past perfect continuous, past perfect passive |
| Typical example | *If you heat ice, it melts.* | *If it rains, we'll cancel.* | *If it rained, we'd cancel.* | *If it had rained, we'd have canceled.* |
| Typical use | rules, facts, habits, instructions | plans, warnings, promises, offers, threats | hypotheticals, advice, polite requests | regret, blame, excuses, relief |
| Can *when* replace *if*? | yes, freely | yes, but it changes the claim | no | no |

## For Russian speakers

Five interference points, in rough order of how often they show up.

**1. Russian puts the future in both clauses. English puts it in one.**

«Если **будет** дождь, мы **останемся** дома» has a future verb in each half. The English sentence has exactly one:

> ✗ *If it **will rain**, we will stay home.*  
> ✓ *If it **rains**, we**'ll** stay home.*

The trap is worse than it looks, because the Russian conditional clause often uses a perfective present that *is* the future — «Если ты **придёшь**…» — so the learner's instinct correctly produces a present-looking form in some sentences and a *will* in others, with no felt difference between them.

**2. *бы* appears twice in Russian. *Would* appears once in English.**

«**Если бы** я знал, я **бы** сказал» carries the particle in both clauses. English puts *would* in the result clause only, and putting it in the *if*-clause is the most recognizable Russian-speaker error in the whole topic.

> ✗ *If I **would know**, I would tell you.*  ✓ *If I **knew**, I'd tell you.*  
> ✗ *If I **would have known**, I would have told you.*  ✓ *If I **had known**, I'd have told you.*

Some American speakers say the second one too, which does not make it writable. See the note under the third conditional above.

**3. One Russian form covers two English ones.** This is the consequential one, because it is a difference of meaning rather than of form. Russian «бы» makes no distinction of time:

| Russian | English — unreal now | English — unreal past |
|---|---|---|
| «Если бы я знал, я бы тебе сказал» | *If I **knew**, I'd tell you.* — I don't know | *If I **had known**, I'd have told you.* — I didn't know |
| «Если бы у меня были деньги, я бы купил» | *If I **had** the money, I'd buy it.* | *If I **had had** the money, I'd have bought it.* |

Russian leaves the time to context and to verb aspect. English forces the speaker to choose, and choosing wrong does not sound vague — it sounds like a different claim. A learner who produces only the second conditional will be understood to be talking about now when they mean last week.

**4. The comma.** Russian separates the two clauses with a comma in **both** orders; English drops it when the *if*-clause comes second — ✗ *We'll stay home, if it rains.*

**5. *если* and *ли* are one word in English.** Russian uses «если» for conditions and «ли» for indirect questions. English uses *if* for both, and only the conditional one takes a comma when fronted. *I don't know if he's coming* is not a conditional and has no comma in it.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *If it **will rain**, we'll cancel.* | *If it **rains**, we'll cancel.* | The future is marked once, in the main clause. The *if*-clause stays present. |
| ✗ *If I **would know**, I'd tell you.* | *If I **knew**, I'd tell you.* | *Would* belongs in the result clause, never in the condition. |
| ✗ *If I **would have known**, I would have called.* | *If I **had known**, I would have called.* | Heard in American speech; not written, and not correct in an edited sentence. |
| ✗ *If I **would of** known…* | *If I **had** known…* | *Of* is never a verb. *Would've* is what is being misheard. |
| ✗ *If I **am** you, I'd say something.* | *If I **were** you, I'd say something.* | The advice formula is fixed. Even *If I was you* reads as dialect. |
| ✗ *We'll leave, if you're ready.* | *We'll leave if you're ready.* | No comma before a trailing *if*-clause. |
| ✗ *If it rains tomorrow we'll cancel.* | *If it rains tomorrow**,** we'll cancel.* | Comma after a fronted *if*-clause. |
| ✗ *If I **had** more time yesterday, I would have stayed.* | *If I **had had** more time yesterday, I would have stayed.* | A past condition needs the past perfect. The plain past is the unreal present. |
| ✗ *If I **would be** rich, I'd travel.* | *If I **were** rich, I'd travel.* | Unreal *be* in the condition is *were*, not *would be*. |
| ✗ *When I win the lottery, I'll quit.* | *If I win the lottery, I'll quit.* | *When* presupposes the event. Use *if* for what may never happen. |
| ✗ *If you will smoke, you will get sick.* (meaning: plain future) | *If you smoke, you'll get sick.* | *If you will smoke* exists, but it means "if you insist on smoking." |

## Quick reference

**Pick a type in two questions.**

```
   Is the condition real — could it be, or could it have been, true?

   YES ─── Is it about one occasion, or about how things go in general?
       │
       ├── in general, every time ......................  ZERO    present + present
       └── one future occasion ........................  FIRST   present + will

   NO ──── Is the unreality about now, or about the past?
       │
       ├── now or the future ..........................  SECOND  past + would
       └── the past, and it's over ....................  THIRD   past perfect + would have
```

**The four sentences to remember.**

> **If you heat ice, it melts.** — always, no occasion (zero)

> **If it rains, we'll cancel.** — it may well rain (first)

> **If it rained, we'd cancel.** — it probably won't (second)

> **If it had rained, we'd have canceled.** — it didn't (third)

**Four rules that cover most of the errors.**

1. **No *will* in the *if*-clause.** Present for a future condition. The exceptions are willingness, insistence, and consequence, and all three mean something other than "later."
2. **No *would* in the *if*-clause.** *Would* is the result clause's word. The condition takes past (second) or past perfect (third).
3. **Comma only when the *if*-clause is first.**
4. **The past tense in types 2 and 3 is not past time.** It is distance from reality, and it is the same unreal past that follows *wish* and *if only*.

## Practice

Put the verb in brackets into the right form, and decide which type the sentence is.

1. If you (heat) copper, it (turn) green over time.
2. If it (rain) tomorrow, we (move) the party inside.
3. If I (be) you, I (not sign) that.
4. If she (leave) earlier, she (catch) the train. (She didn't leave earlier.)
5. If you (finish) your homework, you can watch TV.
6. If I (have) a car, I (drive) you to the airport. (I don't have one.)
7. If the alarm (set), the fire (contain). (It wasn't set. Use the passive.)
8. If you (press) this button, the machine (stop).
9. If he (not call) by six, I (go) without him.
10. If they (offer) me the job, I (take) it. (I doubt they will.)
11. If I (know) you were in town, I (call) you.
12. If water (not reach) 212 degrees, it (not boil).
13. If you (drive) tonight, don't drink.
14. If we (leave) now, we (sit) on the beach by noon. (We're not leaving.)
15. Wait here if you (want) a seat.
16. If the shipment (delay), we (hear) about it today. (Use the present perfect in the condition.)
17. What you (do) if you (lose) your passport in a foreign country?
18. If you (be) rude last night, I don't remember it.
19. If I (not miss) the exit, we (be) on time. (I missed it.)
20. If you (call) at nine tomorrow, I (drive).
