# Mixed conditionals and other variations

> Everything past the four textbook patterns — a condition in one time with a result in another, the modals that replace *would*, continuous and perfect forms on either side, the *if* + *will* that is not an error, and the conditionals that fit in two words.

[← Conditionals](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The four numbered conditionals are a teaching device, not a description of the language. They are four combinations lifted out of a system that generates dozens, and a learner who has memorized only those four will meet an ordinary American sentence like *If I had taken the job, I'd be living in Chicago now* and have nowhere to file it.

Here is the system those four came out of. A conditional sentence is **two clauses that choose their forms independently**:

```
        if + [ condition ]          ,        [ result ]
               |                                |
               real or unreal?                  which modal?
               which time?                      which time?
               which aspect?                    which aspect?
```

The *if*-clause answers two questions: is the condition **open** — it may well be so — or **unreal**, meaning the speaker is treating it as not so? And what **time** does it point at? The result clause answers two more of its own: which **modal** carries it, and what **time** does *it* point at? Nothing requires the two times to match. Nothing requires the result to be *will* or *would* rather than *can*, *might*, *should*, *must*, or a bare imperative.

The four textbook types are simply the four cells where everything lines up neatly:

| Type | *If*-clause | Result clause | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present simple | present simple | *If you **heat** water, it **boils**.* |
| First | present simple | *will* + base | *If it **rains**, we**'ll cancel**.* |
| Second | past simple / *were* | *would* + base | *If I **had** more time, I**'d help**.* |
| Third | past perfect | *would have* + participle | *If I **had known**, I **would have called**.* |

Everything below is what happens when the clauses are allowed to choose separately. None of it is exotic; most of it is commoner in speech than the third conditional is.

## The one real constraint

**The *if*-clause does not use *will* to mark future time.** It is the only thing in the whole system that is genuinely forbidden.

> ✗ *If it **will rain** tomorrow, we'll cancel.*  →  ✓ *If it **rains** tomorrow, we'll cancel.*

> ✗ *If you **will be** late, text me.*  →  ✓ *If you **are** late, text me.*

> ✗ *If she **will call**, tell her I'm out.*  →  ✓ *If she **calls**, tell her I'm out.*

The *if*-clause is already future by position, and the present simple inside it is the same tenseless future that *when*, *after*, *as soon as*, *before*, and *until* require. Nothing marks it twice.

Two things look like violations and are not. ***Will* in another meaning** — willingness, insistence, a fixed politeness formula — belongs in an *if*-clause; see [*If* + *will* and *if* + *would*](#if--will-and-if--would). And ***be going to*** is the verb *be* plus a fixed phrase rather than a future auxiliary, so *If you're going to be late, text me* is fine; see [*Be going to* in conditionals](#be-going-to-in-conditionals).

## Mixed conditionals

A **mixed conditional** is one whose two clauses sit in different times. The forms are not new — you already know every piece. What is new is that the pieces are combined across the textbook rows.

For a Russian speaker this is the corner of the system with no counterpart at home. Russian marks unreality once, with **бы**, and lets context supply the time — *Если бы я знал* covers both *If I knew* and *If I had known*. English marks the condition's time and the result's time **separately**, which is what makes a mixed conditional possible in the first place, and what makes it the pattern Russian speakers most often flatten into a matching pair.

### Past condition → present result

**Form: *if* + past perfect, *would* + base form.** Something did not happen in the past, and the speaker points at the present it left behind.

> *If I **had taken** the job, I**'d be living** in Chicago now.*

> *If she **hadn't missed** the train, she**'d be** here already.*

> *If I **hadn't quit** smoking, I**'d probably be** in the hospital.*

| More | The present it explains |
|---|---|
| *If we **had bought** the house in 2015, we**'d own** it outright by now.* | we are still paying a mortgage |
| *If they **hadn't fired** her, she**'d still be running** the department.* | somebody else runs it |
| *If I **had set** an alarm, I **wouldn't be** so tired.* | I'm exhausted right now |
| *If she **had accepted** the offer, she**'d be** a partner by now.* | she isn't |
| *If the company **had gone** public, those shares **would be** worth a fortune.* | they're worth nothing |
| *If I **hadn't broken** my wrist, I**'d be playing** on Saturday.* | I'm out for the season |

Three signals identify the pattern: the *if*-clause is a past perfect, the result has **no *have*** in it, and the result usually carries a present-time word — *now*, *today*, *still*, *by now*, *at this point*, *anymore*.

The contrast with the plain third conditional is the whole point, and one form is doing all the work:

| Sentence | Condition time | Result time |
|---|---|---|
| *If I **had taken** the job, I **would have moved** to Chicago.* | past | past — the move itself |
| *If I **had taken** the job, I**'d be living** in Chicago now.* | past | present — where I live today |
| *If I **had taken** the job, I**'d be moving** to Chicago next month.* | past | future — a move still ahead |

### Present condition → past result

**Form: *if* + past simple (or *were*), *would have* + past participle.** Something is not the case — a trait, an ability, a standing fact — and that ongoing state explains a past outcome.

> *If I **were** more organized, I **wouldn't have missed** the deadline.*

> *If he **weren't** so stubborn, he **would have apologized** years ago.*

> *If I **spoke** Japanese, I **would have understood** the announcement.*

| More | The standing fact behind it |
|---|---|
| *If she **didn't hate** flying, she **would have come** with us.* | she hates flying, permanently |
| *If you **were** a better driver, you **wouldn't have hit** the mailbox.* | your driving is a chronic condition |
| *If I **had** a car, I **would have driven** you.* | I don't own one |
| *If I **didn't have** three kids, I **would have taken** the transfer.* | the kids are not going away |
| *If I **were** the kind of person who plans ahead, I **would have booked** it in March.* | I am not that person |

The logic is that the *if*-clause is **timeless rather than present**. *If I were more organized* is not a claim about this afternoon; it is a claim about what I am generally like, which is exactly why it can explain something that went wrong last Tuesday.

One ambiguity to watch. *If I **had** a car* is a present condition wearing the past-simple form that every unreal *if*-clause wears:

| Sentence | What it means |
|---|---|
| *If I **had** a car, I **would have driven** you.* | I don't own a car — that's why I couldn't drive you. |
| *If I **had had** a car, I **would have driven** you.* | On that day no car was available. I may own one now. |

### Past condition → future result

The rarest of the three and completely idiomatic: a past event that did not happen would have produced an outcome still ahead of us.

> *If she **had caught** the early flight, she**'d be landing** at noon.*

> *If we **had mailed** it Monday, it **would arrive** tomorrow.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If he **had passed** the bar in May, he**'d be starting** at the firm in September.* | he isn't starting |
| *If they **had approved** the budget, we**'d be hiring** in the fall.* | no hiring is planned |
| *If the contractor **hadn't quit**, the kitchen **would be finished** by Christmas.* | it won't be |
| *If the paperwork **had gone** through, we**'d be closing** on Friday.* | Friday is off |

The result clause is built exactly as in the past-to-present mix — *would* + base, very often continuous. What makes it future is the **time expression**, not the verb form. Strike *tomorrow* from *If we had mailed it Monday, it would arrive* and the sentence collapses back into an ordinary counterfactual about the past.

### The mixes nobody calls mixed

Open conditions cross time zones just as freely, and because they cause no trouble, no textbook numbers them. They are worth listing anyway, because learners who believe the *if*-clause must be present simple write around them for no reason.

| Example | Condition | Result |
|---|---|---|
| *If the package **shipped** Monday, it**'ll arrive** tomorrow.* | past | future |
| *If it **rained** last night, the trail **will be** muddy.* | past | future |
| *If they **left** at six, they**'re** probably in Denver by now.* | past | present |
| *If he **said** that, he**'s** lying.* | past | present |
| *If you**'ve finished** the form, you **can** go.* | present perfect | present |
| *If she**'s** still at the office, she**'ll be** exhausted tomorrow.* | present | future |

The past tenses in those sentences are **real pasts**, not unreal ones. *If it rained last night* means "I don't know whether it did"; *If it rained more often here, the lawn would be greener* means "it doesn't." The result clause tells them apart: *will* signals an open condition, *would* an unreal one.

### Picking the pattern: three questions

Every conditional in English, mixed or not, falls out of three questions asked in order.

| Question | Answer | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Open or unreal?** | open | *if*-clause keeps ordinary tenses; result takes *will*, *can*, *may*, or an imperative |
| | unreal | *if*-clause steps back one; result takes *would*, *could*, *might* |
| **2. When is the condition?** | present or general | unreal: past simple or *were* &middot; open: present simple |
| | past | unreal: past perfect &middot; open: past simple or present perfect |
| | future | unreal: past simple, *were to*, or *should* &middot; open: present simple |
| **3. When is the result?** | past | *would have* + participle |
| | present | *would* + base |
| | future | *would* + base, plus a future time word |

The four textbook types are the cases where the answers happen to match. Everything else on this page is the cases where they do not have to.

### The mixed-conditional summary

| Mix | *If*-clause | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past → present | past perfect | *would* + base | *If I **had studied** medicine, I**'d be** a doctor now.* |
| Past → future | past perfect | *would* + base + future time word | *If we**'d mailed** it Monday, it **would arrive** tomorrow.* |
| Present → past | past simple / *were* | *would have* + participle | *If I **weren't** so shy, I **would have introduced** myself.* |
| Present → past, ability | past simple / *were* | *could have* + participle | *If I **spoke** French, I **could have helped** them.* |
| Future → past | *were to* / past simple | *would have* + participle | *If the audit **were** next week, we **would have started** already.* |

## Modals in the result clause

The result clause is an ordinary main clause, and it takes the full modal inventory. *Will* and *would* are not required parts of the first and second conditionals — they are the most neutral modals available. Swap one in and you change certainty, obligation, permission, or politeness, and nothing else. What each modal means on its own is in [the modal verb dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md); this is what each one does inside a conditional.

### With an open condition

| Modal | What it adds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [*will*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#will) | neutral prediction | *If it rains, the game **will** be canceled.* |
| *be going to* | prediction from evidence, or a settled intention | *If this keeps up, the game**'s going to** be canceled.* |
| *can* | ability or permission | *If you finish early, you **can** leave.* |
| [*could*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#could) | a thinner possibility, or a suggestion | *If you finish early, you **could** leave.* |
| *may* | permission (formal) or possibility | *If you have the receipt, you **may** exchange it.* |
| [*might*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#might) | possibility, weaker than *may* | *If the flight is late, we **might** miss the connection.* |
| [*should*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#should) | expectation, or advice | *If you leave now, you **should** make it.* |
| [*must*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#must) | obligation, or a deduction | *If you want in, you **must** show ID.* &middot; *If the lights are off, they **must** be asleep.* |
| [*ought to*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#ought-to) | expectation, slightly formal | *If the parts arrive today, it **ought to** be done by Friday.* |
| [*had better*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#had-better) | urgent advice with a threat behind it | *If you're driving, you**'d better** leave now.* |
| *have to* / *need to* | obligation from outside | *If the server goes down, someone **has to** be on call.* |
| imperative | an instruction | *If it rains, **move** the reception indoors.* |

Run one condition down the scale and the differences come out clean:

| Result | What the speaker is doing |
|---|---|
| *If you take the highway, you**'ll** save twenty minutes.* | predicting |
| *If you take the highway, you **should** save twenty minutes.* | expecting, with a hedge |
| *If you take the highway, you **may** save twenty minutes.* | allowing a real possibility |
| *If you take the highway, you **might** save twenty minutes.* | allowing a thinner one |
| *If you take the highway, you **could** save twenty minutes.* | suggesting |
| *If you take the highway, you **must** pay a toll.* | stating an obligation |
| *If you take the highway, **watch** for the split at exit 9.* | instructing |

### With an unreal condition

Unreal conditions take the past-form modals, and only those.

| Modal | What it adds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [*would*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#would) | the neutral hypothetical result | *If I had the money, I **would** buy it.* |
| *could* | would be able to, or might possibly | *If I had the money, I **could** buy it.* |
| *might* | perhaps, inside the hypothetical | *If I had the money, I **might** buy it.* |
| *would be able to* | ability, spelled out | *If I spoke Spanish, I **would be able to** read it.* |
| *would have to* | obligation inside the hypothetical | *If we moved to Denver, we**'d have to** sell the boat.* |
| *might well* / *could well* | a strong possibility | *If she found out, she **might well** quit.* |

> ✗ *If I had the money, I **will** buy it.*  →  ✓ *…I **would** buy it.*

> ✗ *If I had the money, I **can** buy it.*  →  ✓ *…I **could** buy it.*

That is not a tense error; it is a mismatch of **stance**. The *if*-clause has declared the situation unreal, and a real-world modal in the result contradicts it.

### Past unreal results: modal + *have* + participle

For a result in past time, all of them take the perfect infinitive, and each says something different about the imaginary past.

| Example | What the modal contributes |
|---|---|
| *If you had told me, I **would have helped**.* | that is simply what would have happened |
| *If you had told me, I **could have helped**.* | I would have been able to |
| *If you had told me, I **might have helped**.* | perhaps — no promises |
| *If she had left earlier, she **might have caught** the flight.* | a real but uncertain chance |
| *If they had asked, we **would have had to** say no.* | obligation inside the counterfactual |
| *If the alarm had worked, nobody **would have been** hurt.* | plain counterfactual |
| *If you knew the ice was thin, you **should have said** something.* | reproach, not prediction |
| *If she had really wanted the job, she **ought to have followed** up.* | what was owed and not done |
| *If he got here at six, he **must have driven** all night.* | a deduction, on an open condition |

*Should have* and *ought to have* stand apart from the rest. They do not say what would have happened; they say what was owed and not delivered, which turns the sentence into a criticism.

### Modals in the *if*-clause

The ban on *will* is a ban on *will*, not on modals generally.

> *If you **must** leave early, sign out at the desk.*

> *If I **may** make a suggestion, try the other route.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If you **can't** reach me, try the office.* | ability |
| *If she **could** be persuaded to chair it, the committee would function.* | remote possibility |
| *If you **should** need anything, dial zero.* | remote open condition — see below |
| *If you **would rather** wait outside, that's fine.* | preference |

*If you must* is worth a line of its own: it grants permission grudgingly, and *If you must know* is a small act of reluctance rather than a statement about necessity.

## Continuous and perfect forms

Aspect is chosen clause by clause, exactly like tense and modal. Any combination that makes sense is available, and several are so common that learners who avoid them sound stilted.

### Continuous in the *if*-clause

> *If you**'re waiting** for Dan, he**'s** already **left**.*

> *If we**'re leaving** at seven, I need to shower now.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If they**'re still arguing** about it, we'll never get out of here.* | in progress now |
| *If you**'re looking** for the stapler, it's in the top drawer.* | in progress now |
| *If he**'s driving**, he shouldn't be answering texts.* | in progress now |
| *If you**'re going** to the store anyway, grab milk.* | an arranged plan |
| *If you**'re coming** on Saturday, bring a jacket.* | an arranged plan |

*We're leaving at seven*, *you're going to the store*, and *you're coming on Saturday* use the present continuous in its **arranged-future** sense — a plan already in place, not a prediction — which is why no future marker is needed or permitted.

### Continuous in the result clause

> *If I hadn't taken this job, I**'d be teaching** high school.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *This time next week I**'ll be sitting** on a beach, if the flight isn't canceled.* | future in progress |
| *If she'd caught the early flight, she**'d be landing** at noon.* | unreal future in progress |
| *If the loan had come through, we**'d be breaking** ground in March.* | unreal future in progress |

A continuous result is the ordinary way to say the imaginary situation would be **under way**, which is why it dominates the mixed conditionals above.

### Unreal continuous in the *if*-clause

Backshift applies to the continuous one step at a time: *are waiting* → *were waiting* → *had been waiting*.

> *If I**'d been paying** attention, I **would have seen** it.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If I **were sitting** where you're sitting, I'd take the deal.* | unreal present, in progress |
| *If he **weren't working** two jobs, he'd have time for this.* | unreal present, in progress |
| *If she**'d been wearing** a seatbelt, she wouldn't have been hurt.* | unreal past, in progress |
| *If I **hadn't been driving**, I would have taken a picture.* | unreal past, in progress |

### Perfect in the *if*-clause of an open condition

The present perfect in an *if*-clause is a **real** present perfect — completion, experience, a result that still stands. It marks nothing about unreality.

> *If you**'ve finished** the report, you can go home.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If they**'ve already left**, there's no point driving over.* | completion |
| *If the check **has cleared**, the order ships today.* | a result that stands |

The past perfect behaves the same way in an open condition when the reference point is itself past: *If he **had already left** by the time you called, that explains it.*

### Perfect continuous, on either side

> *If she**'s been working** all night, she**'ll be** exhausted.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If they**'ve been planning** this for months, why tell us now?* | open condition |
| *If it**'d been raining** all afternoon, the field would have been unplayable.* | unreal past |

### The conditional perfect continuous: *would have been waiting*

The heaviest form in the system — *would have been* + `-ing` — and a real one. It describes an activity that would have been in progress across a stretch of imaginary past time.

> *If the train hadn't been delayed, I **would have been waiting** at the station for an hour.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If she'd started in January, she **would have been teaching** there for a year by now.* | duration up to now |
| *If we'd kept the old lease, we **would have been paying** twice the rent.* | duration across the period |

Speakers often simplify it to *would have waited*, *would have paid*, and little is lost. Keep the continuous when the **duration** is the point of the sentence — *for an hour*, *all night*, *until midnight* — and drop it otherwise.

## *Be going to* in conditionals

### In the result clause

*Be going to* competes with *will* here exactly as it does elsewhere: it predicts from present evidence, or it reports a decision already made.

> *If you don't slow down, you**'re going to crash**.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If this rain keeps up, the game**'s going to be** canceled.* | prediction from evidence |
| *If they offer me the job, I**'m going to take** it.* | the decision is already made |
| *If they offer me the job, I**'ll take** it.* | neutral; the decision may be forming as I speak |

### In the *if*-clause — the legal route to a future condition

> *If you**'re going to be** late, text me.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If it**'s going to rain**, I'll take the car.* | if that is the way things are headed |
| *If you**'re going to argue** about it, I'm leaving.* | if that is your intention |
| *If she**'s going to quit** anyway, she should say so now.* | if that is already decided |

The meaning is not plain future. It is "if it is already the case that this is planned, intended, or visibly headed that way," and that present-tense anchoring is exactly why the form is allowed where *will* is not.

### *Was/were going to*: the plan that did not come off

*Was going to* is the past of *be going to*, and it carries a strong implication that the plan was never carried out — which makes it a natural fit for unreal conditionals.

> *If I **was going to** quit, I would have quit last year.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If she **were going to** say no, she would have said so by now.* | the plan would already have shown |
| *If they **were going to** cancel, they **would have told** us.* | the plan would already have shown |
| *If he **was going to** propose, that was the moment.* | the plan and its missed window |

It also turns up as the *result* of an unreal past condition in conversational American English, where careful writing prefers *would have*:

> *If the deal had gone through, I **was going to buy** a house.* — spoken

> *If the deal had gone through, I **would have bought** a house.* — written

The same form and the same unrealized-plan flavor appear in reported speech; see the *be going to* row in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md).

## Imperatives and other non-declarative results

An *if*-clause can attach to any kind of main clause. The result does not have to be a statement at all.

### Imperative results

> *If you see her, **say hi**.*

> *If anyone calls, **take a message**.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If the alarm goes off, **leave** by the back stairs.* | instruction |
| *If you get lost, **call** me.* | instruction |
| *If it's raining, **take** an umbrella.* | advice |
| *If you must smoke, **do it** outside.* | grudging permission |
| ***Don't wait** if I'm not there by eight.* | result first, no comma |
| ***Call** me if anything changes.* | result first, no comma |

Either order works. When the *if*-clause leads it takes a comma; when it follows the imperative, it usually does not. The imperative has no tense, so nothing in the result has to agree with anything in the condition — which makes these the easiest conditionals in English and the first ones a learner should be able to produce on demand.

### Question results

> *If you're so tired, **why don't you go** to bed?*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If she calls, **should I tell** her you're out?* | asking for instructions |
| *If it rains, **do we still play**?* | asking about the rule |
| *If you knew, **why didn't you say** anything?* | open past condition, past question |
| *If we're going to be there anyway, **why not stay** for dinner?* | a suggestion in question shape |

### Offers, suggestions, and plain statements

> *If it rains, **let's take** a cab.*

> *If you're hungry, **there's pizza** in the fridge.*

That last one is a **relevance conditional**: the pizza is in the fridge whether you are hungry or not. The *if*-clause is not a condition on the fact but on the speaker's reason for mentioning it. Others of the same kind: *If you want my honest opinion, it's overpriced*; *If you're wondering, she's fine*; *If it helps, I've done this before*; *If I may, I'd suggest waiting until Monday.*

## *If* + *will* and *if* + *would*

"Never *if* + *will*" is a good rule about **futurity** and a bad rule about ***will***. The verb has meanings other than future — willingness, insistence, and one fixed polite formula — and in those it is entirely at home in an *if*-clause. This is the real exception, and it is common enough in service English that a learner meets it within a week of landing.

### Willingness

*Will* here means "be willing to."

> *If you **will follow** me, I'll show you to your table.*

The negative is far commoner than the positive, because refusal makes a better condition than consent:

| Example | |
|---|---|
| *If he **won't apologize**, there's nothing more to discuss.* | he refuses |
| *If she **won't sign** it, the deal is off.* | she refuses |
| *If the bank **won't lend** us the money, we'll have to wait.* | it refuses |
| *If the car **won't start**, check the battery.* | machines refuse too |
| *If someone **will hold** the door, I can get the rest of these boxes.* | positive willingness |

### Politeness: *will* and *would* in requests

> *If you **would kindly sign** here, we can process the refund.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If you **would be so kind as to** hold the door…* | maximally formal |
| *If you **will just bear** with me a moment, I'll pull up your account.* | crisply official |
| *If you **would step** to the side, sir.* | the result clause left unsaid |
| *If you **would**, please keep this between us.* | the verb itself left unsaid |
| *If anyone **would like** a copy, see me afterward.* | a softened *want* |

These are barely conditions. They are requests with an *if* wrapper, and the result clause simply says what happens next. *Would* is the more distant and therefore more polite of the two; *will* sounds crisply official. The whole family belongs to formal service English — hotels, banks, courtrooms, airline cabins — and it is not how you ask a friend for anything.

### Insistence: stressed *will*

> *If you **will insist on** arguing with the referee, don't be surprised when you get ejected.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If he **will drive** like that, he's going to get a ticket.* | reproach |
| *If you **will eat** that late, you're going to sleep badly.* | reproach |

The meaning is "if you insist on doing this, as you evidently do," and the tone is reproach. Two mechanical signs come with it: the stress falls on *will*, and it is **never contracted** — ✗ *if you'll leave the door open* is a different sentence, or none at all.

### *If it will* — when the clause states a result

When the *if*-clause describes an **outcome** rather than a condition, *will* is not merely permitted but required.

> *If it **will make** you feel better, I'll come with you.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If it **will save** time, let's take the highway.* | the clause names the payoff |
| *If that **will be** all, I'll head out.* | the clause names the state of play |

The test: the clause is not "if X happens" but "if X is going to be the result of what we are already discussing."

### The summary

| *If*-clause | What *will* / *would* means | Status |
|---|---|---|
| *If it **will rain** tomorrow…* | future time | ✗ — use *If it rains* |
| *If you **will be** late…* | future time | ✗ — use *If you're going to be late* |
| *If you **will follow** me…* | willingness | ✓ |
| *If she **won't sign** it…* | refusal | ✓ — the commonest of all |
| *If you **would kindly sign** here…* | polite request | ✓ formal |
| *If you **will insist** on…* | reproach, stressed *will* | ✓ |
| *If it **will make** you feel better…* | the clause states a result | ✓ |
| *If you **would like** a copy…* | a softened *want* | ✓ |

## *If* + *should*: the remote open conditional

**Form: *if* + subject + *should* + base form.** The condition is possible but not expected — "if by any chance." It is not the *should* of advice; it lowers the odds of the condition and leaves the result clause alone.

> *If you **should need** anything, dial zero.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If the package **should arrive** while I'm out, leave it with the neighbor.* | instruction |
| *If it **should rain**, the ceremony moves indoors.* | contingency plan |
| *If you **should change** your mind, the offer stands.* | a standing offer |
| *If anything **should happen** to me, my sister has the documents.* | the classic legal use |

The result clause takes *will*, an imperative, *can*, or anything else it likes. Four degrees of remoteness on one sentence:

| Version | Effect |
|---|---|
| *If you **need** anything, call the desk.* | neutral |
| *If you **should need** anything, call the desk.* | remote, polite, written or formal |
| *Should you **need** anything, call the desk.* | the same, inverted and more formal still — see [Inversion](04-inversion-and-formal.md) |
| *If you **needed** anything, you could call the desk.* | unreal; the speaker doubts you will |

Register: contracts, hotel scripts, instructions, and safety notices. ✗ *If you would need anything* is not a substitute — *would* cannot do this job. The connector entry is at [*should*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#should).

## *If* + *were to*

**Form: *if* + subject + *were to* + base form.** A hypothetical **future** event, held at arm's length.

> *If the company **were to relocate**, half the staff would quit.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If she **were to find out**, she'd never forgive me.* | an outcome the speaker is imagining |
| *If the bridge **were to fail**, the damage would be catastrophic.* | risk analysis |
| *If Congress **were to pass** the bill, the rules would change in January.* | policy writing |
| *If the two firms **were to merge**, the combined company would be the largest here.* | business prose |

Why it sounds formal: three syllables of machinery where the plain past would serve, and it belongs to proposals, legal drafting, negotiation, and academic writing. It also does real work — it **forces the future reading**, which the plain past leaves open. *If the company relocated* could be a future hypothesis or a past habit; *If the company were to relocate* can only be the first.

Three constraints. All persons take *were*: *If I were to*, *If he were to* — *was to* is heard but non-standard in edited prose. There is no past version: ✗ *If he were to have known* → *If he had known*. And it wants an **event**, not a state: ✗ *If I were to be taller* → *If I were taller*.

## *If* + *happen to*, and *if by any chance*

*Happen to* makes the condition accidental rather than planned.

> *If you **happen to see** Dan, tell him I called.*

| More | |
|---|---|
| *If I **happen to** have the receipt, I'll send it.* | low expectation |
| *If you **should happen to** find it, let me know.* | *should* + *happen to*, maximally remote |
| ***If by any chance** you're free Saturday, we're having people over.* | a soft invitation |
| ***If by any chance** the store is closed, try the one on Fifth.* | a fallback |
| ***On the off chance** that he calls, take a number.* | the same job, no *if* |
| ***In the unlikely event that** the doors fail, use the manual release.* | the formal version |

The whole family — [*on the off chance that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#on-the-off-chance-that), [*in the event that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#in-the-event-that), [*in case*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#in-case) — is laid out in the [condition connectors](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md).

## Ellipsis and reduced clauses

An *if*-clause can shrink to almost nothing when the missing words are recoverable. These are not casual abbreviations; several are fixed expressions with their own entries in the dictionary.

| Reduced | Full form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if possible* | if it is possible | *Send it today **if possible**.* |
| *if necessary* | if it is necessary | *I'll drive you **if necessary**.* |
| *if in doubt* | if you are in doubt | ***If in doubt**, ask.* |
| *if need be* | if there is a need | *We can add a shift **if need be**.* |
| [*if so*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-so) | if that is the case | *Are you coming? **If so**, bring a chair.* |
| [*if not*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-not) | if that is not the case | *Is that clear? **If not**, ask now.* |
| *if not X* | even if not quite X | *It's the best solution, **if not** the cheapest.* |
| *if any* | if there are any | *Note the errors, **if any**.* |
| *if ever* | if it ever happens | *He rarely, **if ever**, calls.* |
| *if anything* | if there is any difference | *The second draft was worse, **if anything**.* |
| *if at all* | if it happens at all | *It'll be done Friday, **if at all**.* |
| *if you like* / *if you want* | if you would like to | *Come along **if you like**.* |
| *if you must* | if you insist on it | *Tell him, **if you must**.* |

**If ever there was one** is a frozen emphatic tag meaning "a prime example," and it inverts the clause permanently: *That was a bad idea **if ever there was one***; *He's a professional **if ever there was one***.

### Reduced clauses with a participle or adjective

When the subject of the *if*-clause is the same as the main clause's — or a generic *you* or *one* — the subject and *be* both drop.

| Reduced | Expanded |
|---|---|
| ***If accepted**, the proposal takes effect in July.* | if it is accepted |
| ***If asked**, say nothing.* | if you are asked |
| ***If heated**, water expands.* | if it is heated |
| ***If left** untreated, the infection spreads.* | if it is left |
| ***If traveling** alone, keep your bag in sight.* | if you are traveling |
| ***If paying** by check, allow ten days.* | if you are paying |
| ***If unsure**, leave the field blank.* | if you are unsure |
| ***If interested**, email the address below.* | if you are interested |

This is the language of instructions, forms, signs, and technical and legal prose. The one danger is a **dangler**: the dropped subject must match the main clause's. ✗ *If approved, we can start construction* says that *we* were approved. Repair it either way: *If **it's** approved, we can start construction*, or *If approved, **the plan** can go ahead.*

### Ellipsis in the result, and answers

> *"Do you want a ride?" "**If you don't mind**."* — the whole result is left out.

> *I'd help **if I could**.* &middot; *He would have said so **if he'd known**.* &middot; *"Will they cancel?" "**Only if it snows**."*

## Double and chained conditions

### Joined by *and* — both must hold

> *If it rains **and** the field floods, they**'ll** cancel.*

> *If you finish the report **and** Dana signs off, we can ship it.*

> *If he'd studied **and** taken the practice test, he would have passed.*

### Joined by *or* — either is enough

> *If it rains **or** the wind picks up, they'll cancel.*

> *If you can't reach me **or** the line is busy, text.*

### The other shapes

| Shape | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *if…and if…* | *If the test is negative **and if** you have no symptoms, you can work.* | repeating *if* marks each condition as separately required — legal and technical prose |
| chained | *If it rains, the field floods; and if the field floods, they cancel.* | each result becomes the next condition |
| chained | *If they cancel, we get a refund — **and if** we get a refund, we can rebook.* | the same, conversational |
| nested | *If she calls — **if** she calls at all — tell her I'm out.* | the inner clause qualifies the outer one |
| embedded | *Ask him whether he'd come **if we paid** his airfare.* | a conditional inside a reported question |
| condition on a result | *If you're going, I'll come too, **unless** it's raining.* | a second condition attached to the result |
| *then* | *If the tests pass **and** the build is green, **then** we deploy.* | *then* is optional; it earns its place when the conditions are long |

The conjunctions that stack alongside *if* — [*unless*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#unless), [*only if*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#only-if), [*even if*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#even-if), [*provided that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#provided-that), [*as long as*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#as-long-as), [*in case*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#in-case) — are covered in the [condition connectors](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md).

## The master table: form → meaning → time

| *If*-clause | Result clause | Reality | Condition time | Result time | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| present simple | present simple | real | general | general | *If you **heat** water, it **boils**.* |
| present simple | *will* + base | open | future | future | *If it **rains**, we**'ll cancel**.* |
| present simple | modal / imperative | open | future | future | *If you **see** her, **say hi**.* |
| present simple | *be going to* | open | present | future | *If you **don't slow down**, you**'re going to crash**.* |
| present continuous | present or imperative | open | now | now | *If you**'re waiting** for Dan, he**'s** gone.* |
| present perfect | *can* / *will* | open | up to now | now or future | *If you**'ve finished**, you **can** go.* |
| present perfect continuous | *will be* | open | up to now | future | *If she**'s been working** all night, she**'ll be** exhausted.* |
| past simple (real) | present or *will* | open | past | present or future | *If he **said** that, he**'s** lying.* |
| *should* + base | *will* / imperative | open, remote | future | future | *If you **should need** anything, dial zero.* |
| *happen to* + base | *will* / imperative | open, accidental | future | future | *If you **happen to see** Dan, tell him.* |
| *will* + base | *will* / imperative | open — willingness | now | future | *If you **will follow** me, I'll show you in.* |
| *would* + base | *will* / imperative | open — politeness | now | future | *If you **would sign** here, we can proceed.* |
| past simple / *were* | *would* + base | unreal | now or always | now or future | *If I **had** time, I**'d help**.* |
| *were to* + base | *would* + base | unreal | future | future | *If they **were to relocate**, half the staff **would quit**.* |
| past continuous | *would* + base | unreal | now, in progress | now | *If you **were listening**, you**'d know**.* |
| past simple / *were* | *would have* + participle | unreal, **mixed** | always | past | *If I **were** organized, I **wouldn't have missed** it.* |
| past perfect | *would have* + participle | unreal | past | past | *If I **had known**, I **would have called**.* |
| past perfect continuous | *would have* + participle | unreal | past, in progress | past | *If I**'d been paying** attention, I**'d have seen** it.* |
| past perfect | *would have been* + `-ing` | unreal | past | past, over time | *If the train hadn't been late, I**'d have been waiting** an hour.* |
| past perfect | *would* + base | unreal, **mixed** | past | present | *If I**'d taken** the job, I**'d be living** there now.* |
| past perfect | *would* + base + future word | unreal, **mixed** | past | future | *If she**'d caught** the flight, she**'d be landing** at noon.* |
| reduced (*if possible*, *if asked*) | anything | either | inherited | inherited | ***If asked**, say nothing.* |

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *If I **would have known**, I would have called.* | *If I **had known**, I would have called.* | *Would* belongs in the result clause only. This is the single commonest conditional error in American speech, and it is still non-standard in writing. |
| *If I **had have known**…* | *If I **had known**…* | The extra *have* is a spoken-only intrusion with no written standing. |
| *If it **will rain** tomorrow, we'll cancel.* | *If it **rains** tomorrow, we'll cancel.* | The *if*-clause never marks future time with *will*. |
| *If I had taken the job, I **would have been living** in Chicago now.* | *If I had taken the job, I**'d be living** in Chicago now.* | *Now* is present. A perfect result puts the result in the past. |
| *If she had caught the flight, she **will be landing** at noon.* | *If she had caught the flight, she**'d be landing** at noon.* | An unreal condition cannot take a real-world *will*. |
| *If I were more organized, I **wouldn't miss** the deadline last week.* | *…I **wouldn't have missed** the deadline last week.* | A past result needs *would have* + participle, whatever the condition's time. |
| *If I had the money, I **will** buy it.* | *If I had the money, I **would** buy it.* | Stance mismatch: unreal condition, real result. |
| *If he **was to** relocate the company…* | *If he **were to** relocate the company…* | *Were to* takes *were* in all persons in edited prose. |
| *If you **would need** anything, call the desk.* | *If you **should need** anything, call the desk.* | *Would* cannot mark a remote open condition; *should* can. |
| *If **approved**, we can start construction.* | *If **it's approved**, we can start construction.* | A reduced clause takes its subject from the main clause, and *we* were not approved. |
| *If it rains, **so** we'll cancel.* | *If it rains, we'll cancel.* / *…**then** we'll cancel.* | The result clause takes no conjunction of its own. *Then* is the only marker English allows. |
| *If she's been working all night, she**'s** exhausted tomorrow.* | *…she**'ll be** exhausted tomorrow.* | A future time word needs a future result. |
| *If I **would be** you, I'd take it.* | *If I **were** you, I'd take it.* | The fixed advice formula is *if I were you*, never *would be*. |

## Quick reference

**The generator.** Ask three questions and the sentence writes itself: (1) open or unreal? (2) when is the condition? (3) when is the result? The two clauses answer separately, and any combination that makes sense is legal.

| If you want… | Use |
|---|---|
| a past cause with a present effect | *if* + past perfect → *would* + base |
| a standing fact with a past effect | *if* + past simple / *were* → *would have* + participle |
| a past cause with a future effect | *if* + past perfect → *would* + base + a future time word |
| a condition that is possible but unexpected | *if* + *should* + base, or inverted *should* |
| a hypothetical future event, formally | *if* + *were to* + base |
| an accidental condition | *if* + *happen to* + base |
| a polite request dressed as a condition | *if you would* / *if you will* + base |
| to reproach a habit | *if you **will** insist on…*, with stress on *will* |
| a future condition without *will* | *if* + *be going to*, or the bare present |
| the shortest possible condition | *if so*, *if not*, *if necessary*, *if in doubt*, *if any* |

**The three sentences to remember.**

> *If I **had taken** the job, I**'d be living** in Chicago now.* — past cause, present result

> *If I **were** more organized, I **wouldn't have missed** the deadline.* — standing cause, past result

> *If she **had caught** the early flight, she**'d be landing** at noon.* — past cause, future result

## Practice

Complete or correct each one. Some are already right.

1. If I had studied harder, I (be) _____ in a better program now.
2. If she weren't afraid of dogs, she (come) _____ to the park with us yesterday.
3. If we had left at six, we (drive) _____ through Denver right about now.
4. If you (see) _____ Marcus, tell him the meeting moved.
5. If you should need anything, you would call the front desk.
6. If it will rain on Saturday, we'll move the party inside.
7. If the board were to approve it, we (start) _____ in January.
8. If he's been driving all night, he (be) _____ too tired to help.
9. If I would have known about the deposit, I would have brought a check.
10. If you're going to be late, (call) _____ me.
11. If they won't lower the price, we (walk) _____ away.
12. If I'd been paying attention, I (see) _____ the sign.
13. If the flight hadn't been canceled, we (wait) _____ at the gate for three hours by now.
14. Send me the draft today if (possible) _____.
15. If approved, we can begin construction in March.

### Answers

| # | Answer |
|---|---|
| 1 | *…I**'d be** in a better program now.* Past condition, present result. |
| 2 | *…she **would have come** to the park with us yesterday.* Standing fact, past result. |
| 3 | *…we**'d be driving** through Denver right about now.* Past condition, present result in progress. |
| 4 | *If you **see** Marcus, **tell** him…* Open condition, imperative result. |
| 5 | *If you should need anything, **call** the front desk* — or *…**you can call** the desk*. ✗ *would call*: *should* marks the condition, and the result stays real. |
| 6 | *If it **rains** on Saturday…* — or *If it**'s going to rain***. Never *if it will rain* for future time. |
| 7 | *…we **would start** in January.* *Were to* is unreal, so the result takes *would*. |
| 8 | *…he**'ll be** too tired to help.* Perfect continuous condition, future result. |
| 9 | *If I **had known** about the deposit…* The *would have* virus in the *if*-clause. |
| 10 | *…**call** me.* *Be going to* is legal in the *if*-clause; the imperative result needs no tense. |
| 11 | *…we**'ll walk** away.* *Won't* = refuse, a real meaning of *will* and fine after *if*. |
| 12 | *…I **would have seen** the sign.* Unreal past in progress, unreal past result. |
| 13 | *…we **would have been waiting** at the gate for three hours by now.* The duration is the point, so keep the continuous. |
| 14 | *…**if possible**.* The reduced clause; *if it is possible* is not wrong, only longer. |
| 15 | ✗ Dangling: *we* were not approved. *If **it's** approved, we can begin* — or *If approved, **the project** can begin*. |
