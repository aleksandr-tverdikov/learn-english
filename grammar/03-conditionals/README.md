# Conditionals

> Everything English does with *if* — the four patterns, the mixed ones, the sentences that carry a condition without the word *if* at all, and the tense shift that marks the whole system.

[← All grammar topics](../README.md) &middot; [Library home](../../README.md)

A conditional sentence has two halves: a **condition** and a **result**. English lets you say which of the two you believe in, and it does that with tense.

```
If it rains, we'll cancel.          ← open: it may well rain
If it rained, we'd cancel.          ← unlikely, or hypothetical
If it had rained, we'd have canceled. ← it didn't rain
```

Nothing about the weather changed between those three sentences. What changed is how far the speaker has stepped back from reality, and the only signal is the verb form. That is the idea the whole topic runs on: **a past tense in a conditional is not about past time — it is distance from fact.** Once that clicks, the numbered patterns stop being four things to memorize and become one mechanism you can run forward and backward.

For a Russian speaker there are two specific obstacles, and both are structural rather than a matter of vocabulary. Russian puts the future after *если*; English does not, which is why ✗ *If it will rain* is the most common conditional error in the language. And Russian marks unreality once, with **бы**, letting context supply the time — so *Если бы я знал* is both *If I knew* and *If I had known*, and English forces a choice between them that Russian never asks you to make.
