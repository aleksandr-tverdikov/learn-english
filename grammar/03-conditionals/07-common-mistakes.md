# Common mistakes with conditionals

> Every error learners actually make with *if* — the wrong sentence, the repair, and the reason the mistake was the natural one to make.

[← Conditionals](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

Conditionals produce more durable errors than any other structure in English. A learner who has stopped saying ✗ *I have seen him yesterday* will still say ✗ *If it will rain, we'll cancel* ten years later, because the error is not a gap in knowledge — it is a habit imported whole from another language, and it keeps being reinforced by the fact that everyone understands the sentence anyway.

Almost everything in this section comes from one of three sources.

1. **English marks the future once per sentence.** Russian marks it in both clauses, and so do most other languages the learner knows. Everything in [§1](#1-tense-errors) follows from this.
2. **English marks unreality by a division of labor.** The *if*-clause takes a past form, the result clause takes *would*, and neither marker is allowed to do the other one's job. Everything in [§3](#3-modal-and-would-errors) follows from this.
3. **Russian draws its lines in different places.** One particle, **бы**, covers what English splits into two conditionals; one preposition, *в случае*, covers what English splits into three connectors; and *если* covers what English splits between *if* and *whether*. [§6](#6-russian-interference) is devoted to these.

Each entry below gives the wrong sentence, the repair, and why the error happens. Where a rule has genuine exceptions — and several of the famous ones do — the exceptions are set out rather than hidden, because a learner who has been told "never *will* after *if*" and then meets *If you will wait here, someone will be with you shortly* concludes that the rule was a lie and stops trusting all of it.

## 1. Tense errors

### 1.1 *Will* in the *if*-clause

The single most recognizable learner error in English.

> ✗ *If it **will rain**, we'll cancel the picnic.*
> ✓ *If it **rains**, we'll cancel the picnic.*

**Why it happens.** The sentence is about the future twice — the rain is in the future and the canceling is in the future — so marking both looks like simple consistency. Russian does exactly that: *Если **будет** дождь, мы **отменим** пикник*, with a future form in each half. English refuses. The future is marked **once**, in the result clause, and the *if*-clause uses a present form that is not really a present tense at all. It is the same tenseless present that follows *when* and *as soon as* ([§1.4](#14-will-after-when-as-soon-as-and-the-other-time-words)), and it carries no claim about present time.

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *If you **will call** me tonight, I'll explain everything.* | *If you **call** me tonight, I'll explain everything.* |
| *If the package **will arrive** late, we'll ship a replacement.* | *If the package **arrives** late, we'll ship a replacement.* |
| *If I **will have** time tomorrow, I'll stop by.* | *If I **have** time tomorrow, I'll stop by.* |
| *If you **will heat** water to 212 degrees, it boils.* | *If you **heat** water to 212 degrees, it boils.* |

The ban is on *will*, not on every forward-looking form. Three present forms are fully available in the *if*-clause, and learners who over-apply the rule end up avoiding them too:

> *If you**'re driving**, I'll pay for the gas.* (present continuous)

> *If you**'ve finished** the form, we can start.* (present perfect)

> *If you**'re going to be** late, call me.* (*be going to*, for a present intention or present evidence)

That last one surprises people who were taught that the *if*-clause must be "simple present." *If you're going to argue about it, I'm leaving* is ordinary English. What it cannot mean is a neutral prediction — for that, the present simple is the only option.

**The genuine exceptions.** *Will* does appear after *if*, in five situations, and in every one of them *will* is not a future marker. It is a lexical verb meaning *be willing to*, *insist on*, or *refuse to*, or it points at a future that is settled independently of the main clause.

| Use of *will* | Example | The test |
|---|---|---|
| **Willingness** — polite requests and offers | *If you **will** wait here, someone will be right with you.* | substitute *are willing to*: ✓ |
| **Insistence** — *will* is stressed | *If you **will** leave the door open, don't be surprised when the cat gets out.* | substitute *insist on leaving*: ✓ |
| **Refusal** — *won't* | *If the engine **won't** start, check the battery.* | substitute *refuses to*: ✓ |
| **Result before condition** | *If it **will make** you feel better, I'll call them myself.* | the calling is what produces the feeling better — the *if*-clause event comes second |
| **A future already settled** | *If you**'ll be** downtown tomorrow anyway, could you pick up the tickets?* | the trip is arranged regardless of the request |

Substitute an ordinary future into any of those and the meaning changes or collapses: *If you wait here* is a plain condition, while *If you will wait here* is a courteous instruction. The polite version has a still more polite cousin with *would*, and it is completely standard:

> *If you **would** sign at the bottom, we can file it today.*

This is the one place *would* is welcome in an *if*-clause, and it is why [§3.1](#31-would-in-both-clauses) has to be stated as a rule about unreal conditions rather than as a blanket ban.

### 1.2 *Would have* in the *if*-clause

> ✗ *If I **would have known**, I would have told you.*
> ✓ *If I **had known**, I would have told you.*

**Why it happens.** The third conditional puts *would have* in one clause and the past perfect in the other, and there is no way to hear which is which — both halves feel equally "unreal about the past," so the more distinctive-sounding marker gets copied into both. Russian encourages this directly, since **бы** appears in both halves of *Если бы я знал, я бы сказал*. English divides the work: the *if*-clause carries the unreality as a past perfect, the result clause carries it as *would have*.

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *If you **would have called**, I would have picked you up.* | *If you **had called**, I would have picked you up.* |
| *If we **would have left** earlier, we wouldn't have missed it.* | *If we **had left** earlier, we wouldn't have missed it.* |
| *If she **wouldn't have helped**, I would have failed.* | *If she **hadn't helped**, I would have failed.* |
| *If they **would have told** us, we would have come.* | *If they **had told** us, we would have come.* |

**This one deserves an honest note.** ✗ *If I would have known* — and its spoken form *if I woulda known* — is said constantly in the United States, in every region and at every level of education. It is not a foreign-learner error; it is a native-speaker variant. It is also marked wrong by every style guide, every editor, every teacher, and every exam. The practical position: expect to hear it, never write it, and do not take hearing it as permission. In the reverse direction, a learner who produces it in an essay is not passing for a native speaker — they are producing the one construction American graders are trained to circle.

**A contraction trap sits on top of this.** In writing, *I'd* is ambiguous between *I had* and *I would*, and learners resolve it the wrong way. Inside an *if*-clause, *'d* is **always** *had*:

> *If I**'d** known* = *If I **had** known*, never ✗ *if I would known*.

> *If she**'d** asked me, I**'d** have said yes.* — the first *'d* is *had*, the second is *would*.

The following word settles it: a past participle after *'d* means *had*; a bare verb means *would*. The same test is laid out in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md#3-the-two-ambiguous-forms).

### 1.3 *If I would be* for *If I were*

> ✗ *If I **would be** rich, I'd buy a house on the lake.*
> ✓ *If I **were** rich, I'd buy a house on the lake.*

**Why it happens.** The learner knows the sentence is unreal, knows *would* is the unreal word, and puts it where the unreality starts. But the second conditional marks the condition with a **past form**, not with *would* — and with the verb *be*, that past form is *were* for every subject in careful English.

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *If I **would be** you, I'd take the job.* | *If I **were** you, I'd take the job.* |
| *If he **would be** here, he'd know what to do.* | *If he **were** here, he'd know what to do.* |
| *If it **wouldn't be** so expensive, we'd go.* | *If it **weren't** so expensive, we'd go.* |
| *If I **would have** a car, I'd drive you.* | *If I **had** a car, I'd drive you.* |
| *If they **would live** closer, we'd see them more.* | *If they **lived** closer, we'd see them more.* |

Note the last two rows. The error is not limited to *be*: any verb can attract a stray *would*, and the repair is always the same — replace *would* + base with the past form.

### 1.4 *Will* after *when*, *as soon as*, and the other time words

Exactly the same rule, in a place learners do not expect it.

> ✗ *I'll call you when I **will arrive**.*
> ✓ *I'll call you when I **arrive**.*

**Why it happens.** Nothing about this clause is conditional — the arrival is certain — so the reasoning that produced ✗ *if it will rain* has even more force here. But English treats future time clauses exactly like *if*-clauses: the subordinate clause takes a present form and the main clause carries the future.

| Conjunction | ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|---|
| *when* | *Call me when you **will get** home.* | *Call me when you **get** home.* |
| *as soon as* | *We'll start as soon as she **will arrive**.* | *We'll start as soon as she **arrives**.* |
| *until* / *till* | *Wait here until I **will come** back.* | *Wait here until I **come** back.* |
| *before* | *I'll finish before he **will notice**.* | *I'll finish before he **notices**.* |
| *after* | *We'll eat after the game **will end**.* | *We'll eat after the game **ends**.* |
| *once* | *Once the check **will clear**, I'll ship it.* | *Once the check **clears**, I'll ship it.* |
| *by the time* | *By the time you **will get** here, I'll be gone.* | *By the time you **get** here, I'll be gone.* |
| *whenever* | *Come over whenever you **will be** free.* | *Come over whenever you**'re** free.* |

The present perfect is available in these clauses too, and it is the natural choice when completion is the point:

> *I'll call you after I**'ve finished** the report.*

But not the future perfect: ✗ *I'll call you when I **will have arrived*** is wrong for the same reason as the rest.

**The exception that matters: noun clauses.** *When* and *if* also introduce clauses that serve as the **object** of a verb like *know*, *wonder*, *tell*, *ask*, *find out*, or *be sure*. Those are not time clauses or conditions at all, and they take *will* freely.

| Clause type | Example | *Will*? |
|---|---|---|
| Time clause — tells you *when* the main action happens | *I'll let you know when the package **arrives**.* | no |
| Noun clause — is the *thing* you are told | *I'll let you know when the package **will arrive**.* | yes |
| Condition | *If she **comes**, we'll start.* | no |
| Noun clause after *know*, *wonder*, *ask* | *I don't know if she**'ll** come.* | yes |

Both rows in each pair are correct English with different meanings. *I'll let you know when the package arrives* means you will hear from me at the moment of delivery. *I'll let you know when the package will arrive* means you will hear the delivery date. The test: if you can replace the clause with *it* or *that*, it is a noun clause and *will* is allowed.

### 1.5 One past too many

> ✗ *If I **had had** more time, I **would have helped** you.* — said about a present shortage of time
> ✓ *If I **had** more time, I **would help** you.*

**Why it happens.** A learner who has just been taught the third conditional applies it to everything unreal, and the third conditional is the form that feels the most unreal, so it gets used for the most hopeless-sounding situations regardless of when they are. The Russian source sentence gives no help at all: *Если бы у меня было больше времени, я бы тебе помог* is the same string of words whether the shortage is today's or yesterday's.

The fix is mechanical. **Count the steps back from the present.**

| Steps back | Meaning | Condition | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | a real, open condition | present | *will* + base | *If I **have** time, I**'ll help** you.* |
| one | unreal now, or unlikely in the future | past simple | *would* + base | *If I **had** time, I **would help** you.* |
| two | unreal in a finished past | past perfect | *would have* + participle | *If I **had had** time, I **would have helped** you.* |
| three | — | — | — | does not exist |

One step means the situation is contrary to fact **now**. Two steps means it was contrary to fact **then**, and nothing can be done about it. A learner who uses two steps for a present situation has not said something more hypothetical; they have said something about a different day.

The error runs in both directions, and the second direction is just as common:

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|
| *If I **had known** you were in town, I**'d call** you.* | *If I **had known** you were in town, I**'d have called** you.* | past condition, present-shaped result |
| *If I **knew** you were coming yesterday, I **would bake** a cake.* | *If I **had known** you were coming, I **would have baked** a cake.* | a finished past given the one-step form |
| *If I **had had** a car now, I'd drive you.* | *If I **had** a car, I'd drive you.* | two steps for a present shortage |

**[Mixed conditionals](02-mixed-and-variations.md) are not exceptions to this — they are the honest application of it.** When the condition and the result belong to different times, each half takes the form that matches its own time:

> *If I **had studied** medicine, I **would be** a doctor now.* (past condition, present result)

> *If I **spoke** Spanish, I **would have understood** the announcement.* (permanent condition, past result)

The errors learners produce here are the un-mixed versions: ✗ *If I had studied medicine, I would have been a doctor now* pushes the result into the past, where *now* cannot follow it, and ✗ *If I were more careful, I wouldn't have lost my keys* says that a permanent trait would have prevented one past event — sometimes what you mean, often not. If the keys are the point, the condition belongs in the past too: *If I **had been** more careful, I wouldn't have lost them.*

## 2. The *was* or *were* question

This is the one place in conditionals where the honest answer is not a rule but a register split, and pretending otherwise leaves learners either over-correcting or embarrassed.

**One: *If I were you* is frozen.** It is a fixed formula for giving advice, and *was* does not appear in it in any register, spoken or written. ✗ *If I was you* is the version that gets noticed.

> *If I **were** you, I'd sleep on it.*

**Two: everywhere else, *was* is ordinary American speech.** *If I was rich*, *if he was here*, *if it wasn't so late* — these are what Americans say, and a learner who says *were* in casual conversation sounds careful rather than wrong.

**Three: *were* is what writing and exams expect.** In edited prose, in formal email, in academic work, and on every standardized test, the unreal condition takes *were* for every subject.

| Context | Write | You will hear |
|---|---|---|
| *If I were you* and its variants | *were* — always | *were* |
| Ordinary unreal condition, *I* / *he* / *she* / *it* | *were* | *was*, routinely |
| After *wish* | *I wish I **were** taller.* | *I wish I was taller.* |
| After *as if*, *as though* | *He talks as if he **were** in charge.* | *as if he was* |
| Inversion, with *if* deleted | *Were I you…* — *were* only | *were* |
| A **real** condition about the past | *was* — required | *was* |

That last row is the one nobody explains, and it is where the over-correctors go wrong. A condition can be about the past and still be entirely **open** — the speaker does not know whether it is true, and it may well be. Open conditions take ordinary tenses, and for *be* in the past that means *was*.

| Sentence | Reading |
|---|---|
| *If he **was** at the meeting, he heard the announcement.* | I don't know whether he was there. Open condition, real past. |
| *If he **were** at the meeting, he would hear the announcement.* | He is not at the meeting. Unreal. |

So ✗ *If I were there yesterday, I didn't notice anything* is an over-correction, not a refinement: the speaker may genuinely have been there, so it is *If I **was** there yesterday…*

**Two more errors cluster here.**

> ✗ *If I were you, I **will** talk to her.* → ✓ *If I were you, I **would** talk to her.*

An unreal condition cannot take a real-future result. The clauses have to agree about which world they are in.

> ✗ ***Was** I you, I'd take the job.* → ✓ ***Were** I you, I'd take the job.*

Inversion is built on the subjunctive *were* alone. There is no ✗ *was I*, ✗ *did I have*, or ✗ *would I have* version of it; the three inverted forms English allows are *Were I…*, *Had I…*, and *Should you…* — see [Inversion and formal conditionals](04-inversion-and-formal.md).

## 3. Modal and *would* errors

### 3.1 *Would* in both clauses

> ✗ *If I **would have** time, I **would come**.*
> ✓ *If I **had** time, I **would come**.*

**Why it happens.** Russian marks the unreality twice, with the same particle in both halves: *Если **бы** у меня было время, я **бы** пришёл*. English also marks it twice — but with **two different markers, in two fixed places**. The past form does it on the left of the comma; *would* does it on the right. Neither may cross over.

```
   Russian    Если бы + [past],        [subject] бы + [past]
                     ↑ бы                        ↑ бы          — same particle, twice

   English    If     + [past form],    [subject] would + [base]
                     ↑ past tense               ↑ would        — two markers, one each
```

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *If you **would ask** him, he'd help.* | *If you **asked** him, he'd help.* |
| *If it **wouldn't be** raining, we'd walk.* | *If it **weren't** raining, we'd walk.* |
| *If they **would offer** me the job, I'd take it.* | *If they **offered** me the job, I'd take it.* |
| *If we **would have** left earlier, we'd be there.* | *If we **had** left earlier, we'd be there.* |

The mirror-image error is dropping *would* from the result clause, which happens when the learner has put all the unreality into the *if*-clause and has none left over:

> ✗ *If I had money, I **buy** a car.* → ✓ *If I had money, I **would buy** a car.*

> ✗ *If she asked me, I **say** yes.* → ✓ *If she asked me, I **would say** yes.*

**The exception, again.** *Would* after *if* is correct in a polite request or a stipulation of willingness — *If you would follow me*, *I'd appreciate it if you would let me know*. The rule is about unreal conditions, not about the word *would*. See [§1.1](#11-will-in-the-if-clause) and [§3.5](#35-would-or-will-in-a-polite-request).

### 3.2 Two modals in a row

> ✗ *If I had the money, I **would can** buy it.*
> ✓ *If I had the money, I **could** buy it.* / *I **would be able to** buy it.*

**Why it happens.** In Russian, "would" and "can" are separate words that stack freely: *я бы смог*. In English, a verb phrase takes **one modal, and one only**. Everything else has to be said with a semi-modal — a phrase that has real tense forms and can therefore sit under a modal.

| ✗ Two modals | ✓ Repair | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *I **would can** help.* | *I **could** help.* / *I **would be able to** help.* | *could* already carries both meanings |
| *I **will can** finish by Friday.* | *I**'ll be able to** finish by Friday.* | |
| *I **would must** sell the car.* | *I **would have to** sell the car.* | obligation goes to *have to* |
| *You **will must** register first.* | *You**'ll have to** register first.* | |
| *I **would should** tell her.* | *I **ought to** tell her.* / *I **should** tell her.* | |
| *He **would might** come.* | *He **might** come.* | *might* is already the remote form |

Two footnotes, both honest.

**Double modals exist in American English** — *might could*, *might should*, *used to could* — in Southern and South Midland speech. They are a real dialect feature, not an error in that dialect, and they are not part of standard written English. A learner producing *might could* is not being regional; they are being ungrammatical. Avoid.

**A modal takes a bare infinitive**, so the neighboring errors belong here too: ✗ *I would to help*, ✗ *She would helps*, ✗ *He would to be angry*. After *would*, *could*, *might*, *should*, *will*, and *may*, the next verb has no *to* and no ending: *would help*, *could be*, *might come*.

### 3.3 *Would have* in the wrong result clause

> ✗ *If I **knew** her number, I **would have called** her.* — said about right now
> ✓ *If I **knew** her number, I **would call** her.*

**Why it happens.** *Would have* sounds more thoroughly unreal, so it gets attached to any condition that feels hopeless. But *would have* + participle places the result in a **finished past**, and a present condition cannot produce one — unless the sentence is genuinely mixed, which is why this needs care rather than a flat ban.

| Sentence | Verdict |
|---|---|
| *If I knew her number, I would call her.* | ✓ present condition, present result |
| *If I knew her number, I would have called her.* | ✓ only as a mixed conditional: I still don't know it, and that is why I didn't call yesterday |
| *If I had known her number, I would have called her.* | ✓ past condition, past result |
| *If I had known her number, I would call her.* | ✓ mixed: I didn't know it then, so I can't call now |

All four are possible English. The error is choosing one at random because they all feel unreal. Ask when the condition holds and when the result would have landed, and the form follows.

### 3.4 The modals that belong in the result clause

Not every modal in a conditional is a mistake. *Could*, *might*, *may*, and *should* are all at home in the result clause, and replacing them with *would* flattens the meaning:

> *If we leave now, we **might** catch the 6:10.* (possibility)

> *If you had asked, I **could** have lent you the car.* (ability or possibility in the past)

What none of them may do is move into the *if*-clause when the condition is unreal: ✗ *If I could have the money, I'd buy it* → *If I **had** the money*. And *could* in a condition is fine when it means real ability: *If you **could** come on Tuesday, that would help* is a polite request, of the same family as *If you would*.

### 3.5 *Would* or *will* in a polite request

A separate problem, since the choice is about tone rather than truth. English backs away from a direct request by putting it one step into the past — *will* → *would*, *can* → *could* — and a learner who uses the present forms sounds abrupt without intending to.

| Form | Effect |
|---|---|
| *Will you hold the door?* | direct; between friends it is fine, to a stranger it can read as an order |
| *Would you hold the door?* | the neutral polite form |
| *Could you hold the door?* | equally polite; slightly softer, since it asks about ability |
| *Would you mind holding the door?* | more polite still, and it takes `-ing` |

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *Would you mind **to wait** a moment?* | *Would you mind **waiting** a moment?* |
| *I'd be grateful if you **will** send it today.* | *I'd be grateful if you **would** send it today.* |
| *I **would want** a coffee, please.* | *I **would like** a coffee, please.* |
| *Do you want **that I help** you?* | *Would you like **me to help** you?* |
| ***Couldn't** you send it today?* | ***Could** you send it today?* |
| *If you **can** just sign here, please.* | *If you **could** just sign here, please.* |

The last two rows catch a specific Russian habit. *Не могли бы вы…* is negative in form and perfectly polite in Russian; its literal English translation, *Couldn't you…*, is not a request at all — it is a complaint, on the pattern of *Couldn't you have told me sooner?* Drop the negative.

## 4. Connector errors

### 4.1 *Unless* and *if not*

> ✗ *Unless you **would call** me, I'll assume you're not coming.*
> ✓ *Unless you **call** me, I'll assume you're not coming.*

*Unless* introduces a condition, so everything in [§1.1](#11-will-in-the-if-clause) applies to it unchanged: no *will*, no *would*, present form for a future meaning.

**The negative is already inside the word.** *Unless* means *except if*, so adding a negative reverses the sentence:

> ✗ *Unless you **don't** hurry, we'll miss the train.*
> ✓ *Unless you hurry, we'll miss the train.* = *If you **don't** hurry, we'll miss the train.*

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *Unless you **won't** pay, they'll cut off the power.* | *Unless you **pay**, they'll cut off the power.* |
| *Unless she **doesn't** apologize, I'm not going.* | *Unless she **apologizes**, I'm not going.* |
| *Unless it **will rain**, we'll eat outside.* | *Unless it **rains**, we'll eat outside.* |

**Where *unless* and *if not* stop being interchangeable.** The swap works for a plain condition — *Unless you pass the test, you won't get the job* is *If you don't pass the test, you won't get the job* — but it fails in two places.

**Reactions, feelings, and surprise take *if not* only.**

> ✓ *I'll be **amazed if he doesn't** call.*   ✗ *I'll be amazed unless he calls.*

The reason is that *unless* names the one circumstance that would cancel the main clause, and a feeling is not canceled that way — the speaker is describing a reaction to a specific event, not fencing off an exception.

**Counterfactuals take *if not* only.** *Unless* is at home in conditions that are still open, even remote ones, but not in conditions known to be contrary to fact:

> ✗ *Unless she **had helped** me, I would have failed.*
> ✓ *If she **hadn't helped** me, I would have failed.*

> ✗ *I'd be on the beach right now unless I **had** this deadline.*
> ✓ *I'd be on the beach right now if I **didn't have** this deadline.*

But an open remote condition is fine, which is why the ban has to be stated narrowly:

> ✓ *I wouldn't ask unless it **were** serious.*

### 4.2 *In case* is not *if*

The single commonest conditional error among Russian speakers, and one that native listeners genuinely misunderstand rather than simply notice.

> ✗ *In case it rains, we'll cancel the picnic.*
> ✓ *If it rains, we'll cancel the picnic.*

**Why it happens.** Russian *в случае* and *на случай* both start with the same word, and *в случае если* is a perfectly ordinary way to say *if*. English splits them. *If* states the circumstance under which the main clause happens. *In case* states a **reason for taking a precaution in advance**, and the main clause happens either way.

| Sentence | What it means |
|---|---|
| *I'll take an umbrella **if** it rains.* | I look out the window first. No rain, no umbrella. |
| *I'll take an umbrella **in case** it rains.* | I take the umbrella now, whatever the sky is doing. |
| *Call me **if** you get lost.* | Only if it happens. |
| *Take my number **in case** you get lost.* | Take it now, as a precaution. |

**The test.** *In case* answers *why are you doing that?* — *Why are you taking an umbrella?* *In case it rains.* *If* answers *when will you do it?* If the main clause is an action taken in advance, *in case* is right; if the main clause depends on the outcome, *if* is right.

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *In case you need help, I'll come over.* | *If you need help, I'll come over.* |
| *In case of you need anything, call me.* | *If you need anything, call me.* |
| *Take a coat in case it **will be** cold.* | *Take a coat in case it **is** cold.* / *in case it **gets** cold.* |
| *I took a sweater in case it **would get** cold.* | *I took a sweater in case it **got** cold.* |
| *In the case of emergency, break the glass.* | *In case of emergency, break the glass.* |

Three related forms, kept apart:

- ***in case*** + a clause — a precaution: *Bring cash **in case** the card reader is down.*
- ***in case of*** + a noun — the sign-and-notice form, meaning *if there is*: ***In case of** fire, use the stairs.* Never followed by a clause.
- ***in the event that*** / ***in the event of*** — the formal, contractual equivalent of plain *if*: *In the event that the tenant fails to pay, the lease terminates.*

**One real American exception.** In a fixed opener with a past-tense clause, *in case* does mean *if*: *In case you were wondering, the answer is no.* *In case you missed it, the deadline moved.* These are frozen conversational formulas. They do not license ✗ *In case it rains, we'll cancel.*

### 4.3 *Even if* and *even though*

> ✗ *Even if he's rich, he's not happy.* — said about a man who is, in fact, rich
> ✓ *Even though he's rich, he's not happy.*

**Why it happens.** Russian *даже если* and *хотя* are easy to keep apart in Russian and easy to collapse in English, because both come out starting with *even*. The distinction is about whether the speaker is asserting the clause or just entertaining it.

| Connector | Russian | The clause is | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *even if* | *даже если* | hypothetical, or true-but-irrelevant | *Even if it rains, we're going.* |
| *even though* | *хотя*, *несмотря на то что* | a fact the speaker asserts | *Even though it's raining, we're going.* |

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Even if she's my sister, I won't lie for her.* | *Even though she's my sister, I won't lie for her.* | she is your sister — a fact |
| *Even though it rains tomorrow, the game is on.* | *Even if it rains tomorrow, the game is on.* | tomorrow's weather is not a fact yet |
| *Even it's late, I'll come.* | *Even though it's late, I'll come.* | *even* alone is not a connector |
| *Even if I **would have** money, I wouldn't buy it.* | *Even if I **had** money, I wouldn't buy it.* | *even if* obeys the conditional tense rules |
| *Even if it **will snow**, the flight will leave.* | *Even if it **snows**, the flight will leave.* | no *will* in the condition |

*Even if* also covers the case where the condition is already known to be true but is being brushed aside: *Even if he apologized — and he did — I'm still angry.* That use is what makes the pair feel interchangeable, and it is the narrow exception, not the rule.

### 4.4 *If* and *whether*

Two words, one Russian word behind each use, and a clean division of labor in English: **conditions take *if*; alternatives inside a noun clause take *whether*.**

> ✗ *Whether it rains, we'll cancel.* → ✓ *If it rains, we'll cancel.*

> ✗ *I can't decide **if to** go.* → ✓ *I can't decide **whether to** go.*

After *ask*, *know*, *wonder*, *tell*, *find out*, and *be sure*, both are allowed and *if* is the more conversational choice: *I don't know **if** she's coming* / *I don't know **whether** she's coming*. Four places take *whether* only.

| Where | ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|---|
| Before a *to*-infinitive | *I don't know **if to** call.* | *I don't know **whether to** call.* |
| After a preposition | *It depends on **if** he comes.* | *It depends on **whether** he comes.* |
| Directly before *or not* | *Tell me **if or not** you're coming.* | *Tell me **whether or not** you're coming.* |
| As the subject of the sentence | ***If** he comes is up to him.* | ***Whether** he comes is up to him.* |

The *or not* row has a wrinkle worth knowing: *or not* at the **end** of the sentence is fine with *if* — *I don't know if he's coming or not* is standard. It is only the adjacent *whether or not* that refuses *if*.

**The ambiguity test.** Where *if* could be read as a condition, formal writing chooses *whether*:

> *Let me know **if** you need a ride.* — two readings: "tell me in the event that you need one" or "tell me which it is."

> *Let me know **whether** you need a ride.* — one reading: I want an answer either way.

**The Russian source of the confusion.** Russian has two separate words — *ли* for the noun-clause sense and *если* for the condition — while English has *if* doing both jobs and *whether* covering only one of them. Learners map *если* onto *if* and use it everywhere, producing ✗ *Whether you need help, call me* when they over-correct in the other direction. The entries for both words are in [the complementizer dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#whether).

### 4.5 *Provided that*

> ✗ *Provided that it rains, the match will be canceled.*
> ✓ *If it rains, the match will be canceled.*

**Why it happens.** *Provided that* is taught as a synonym for *if*, and it is listed that way in most bilingual dictionaries. It is not a synonym. It means "**only on this condition, and the condition is a requirement somebody is imposing or accepting**." It belongs to permission, agreements, and contracts, and it needs a human decision behind it.

> ✓ *You can use the pool **provided that** you shower first.*

> ✓ *We'll extend the deadline **provided that** you notify us in writing.*

Nobody is granting permission for rain, which is why the opening example fails.

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Provided that you get sick, call me.* | *If you get sick, call me.* | the condition is undesirable; nobody stipulates it |
| *Provided that I **were** rich, I'd buy it.* | *If I were rich, I'd buy it.* | *provided that* does not take unreal conditions |
| *Provided that you **will pay**, we'll start.* | *Provided that you **pay**, we'll start.* | no *will* in a condition |
| *Provided that it **would rain**, we'd cancel.* | *If it rained, we'd cancel.* | both errors at once |

The family it belongs to, with registers — each one is worked through in [Alternatives to *if*](03-alternatives-to-if.md):

| Connector | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *as long as*, *so long as* | neutral, spoken | *You can stay as long as you pay rent.* |
| *provided (that)*, *providing (that)* | formal, written | *Provided that the funds clear, we'll ship Monday.* |
| *on condition that* | formal, explicit bargain | *He was released on condition that he surrender his passport.* |
| *only if* | emphatic, exclusive | *I'll sign only if my lawyer reads it first.* |

*That* is droppable in all of them, and *as long as* has a second, purely temporal meaning — *Stay as long as you like* — which context always settles. The full entries are in [subordinators of condition](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#provided-that).

### 4.6 Smaller connector slips

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *But for you **helped** me, I'd have failed.* | *But for **your help**, I'd have failed.* | *but for* takes a noun phrase only |
| *In the event of **you are late**, call ahead.* | *In the event **that** you are late, call ahead.* | *in the event of* takes a noun, *in the event that* takes a clause |
| *Otherwise you leave now, you'll miss it.* | *Leave now; **otherwise** you'll miss it.* | *otherwise* is an adverb, not a subordinator |
| *What if he **will say** no?* | *What if he **says** no?* / *What if he **said** no?* | present for a real possibility, past for a remote one |
| *Supposing that it **will rain**?* | *Supposing it **rains**?* | *suppose*, *supposing*, *what if* follow the conditional tense rules |
| *If should you need anything, call.* | ***Should** you need anything, call.* | inversion replaces *if*; you cannot have both |
| *In the case of an emergency, call 911.* | *In case of an emergency, call 911.* | *in the case of* means "regarding," not "if" |

## 5. Punctuation

One rule covers ninety percent of it. **A conditional clause that comes first takes a comma. A conditional clause that comes second does not.**

> ✓ *If it rains, we'll cancel.* &middot; ✗ *If it rains we'll cancel.* — the comma is missing

> ✓ *We'll cancel if it rains.* &middot; ✗ *We'll cancel, if it rains.* — the comma is spurious

**Why the second one happens.** Russian punctuates a subordinate clause with commas regardless of where it sits: *Мы отменим, если будет дождь.* The comma is obligatory there and transfers straight into English, where it is not merely unnecessary but actively wrong. This is the commonest punctuation error Russian speakers make in English, and it is invisible to them because their own language demands it.

The rule applies identically to every conditional connector:

| ✓ Fronted — comma | ✓ Second — no comma |
|---|---|
| *If you need help, call me.* | *Call me if you need help.* |
| *Unless it rains, we'll eat outside.* | *We'll eat outside unless it rains.* |
| *Even if she apologizes, I'm not going.* | *I'm not going even if she apologizes.* |
| *Provided that the check clears, we'll ship Monday.* | *We'll ship Monday provided that the check clears.* |
| *Had I known, I would have called.* | *I would have called had I known.* |

**The real exceptions**, which are about meaning rather than position:

- **An afterthought or a politeness** takes a comma even in second position, because it is added rather than integrated: *I'll stop by later, if that's all right.* &middot; *We'll go ahead, unless you object.*
- **Inversion always takes one when fronted**: *Were I you, I'd wait.* &middot; *Should you need anything, let me know.*
- ***If so*** and ***if not*** as short pro-forms take one: *If not, we'll reschedule.*

**Four smaller errors:**

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *If it rains; we'll cancel.* | *If it rains, we'll cancel.* | a semicolon cannot join a subordinate clause to its main clause |
| *If, you need anything, let me know.* | *If you need anything, let me know.* | nothing goes between *if* and its subject |
| *If it rains, then, we'll cancel.* | *If it rains, then we'll cancel.* | *then* is optional but takes no comma after it |
| *We'll cancel. If it rains.* | *We'll cancel if it rains.* | an *if*-clause alone is a fragment, except in dialogue |

## 6. Russian interference

The errors in this section are not random. Each one is a faithful translation of a Russian sentence that happens to be built out of different parts, and a learner who can see the seam stops making the error permanently, which is not true of the errors they simply memorize.

### 6.1 The future in both clauses

Russian marks the future wherever the future is meant. English marks it once, in the result clause, and leaves a present form in the condition.

| Russian | Literal — ✗ | ✓ English |
|---|---|---|
| *Если **будет** дождь, мы **отменим** пикник.* | *If it **will be** rain, we will cancel the picnic.* | *If it **rains**, we'll cancel the picnic.* |
| *Если ты **позвонишь**, я всё объясню.* | *If you **will call**, I'll explain everything.* | *If you **call**, I'll explain everything.* |
| *Если у меня **будет** время, я зайду.* | *If I **will have** time, I'll stop by.* | *If I **have** time, I'll stop by.* |
| *Когда я **приеду**, я тебе позвоню.* | *When I **will arrive**, I will call you.* | *When I **arrive**, I'll call you.* |
| *Как только он **придёт**, мы начнём.* | *As soon as he **will come**, we will start.* | *As soon as he **arrives**, we'll start.* |
| *Подожди, пока я не **вернусь**.* | *Wait until I **will** not **come** back.* | *Wait until I **come** back.* |

Two things to notice in the last row. The Russian *не* after *пока* has no English counterpart, so ✗ *until I won't come back* carries a negative that English does not want, and the ✗ *will* is wrong on top of it.

### 6.2 One particle, two conditionals

Russian **бы** covers both of English's unreal conditionals. *Если бы у меня было время, я бы помог* is the same string of words whether the time was missing yesterday or is missing now; Russian leaves it to context or to an adverb. English must choose, and the choice is visible in the form.

**The procedure: ask when the condition belongs to.**

| Russian | The condition is about | ✓ English |
|---|---|---|
| *Если бы я знал, я бы сказал.* (I don't know **now**) | now | *If I **knew**, I'd tell you.* |
| *Если бы я знал, я бы сказал.* (I didn't know **then**) | a finished past | *If I **had known**, I'd have told you.* |
| *Если бы я выучил испанский, я бы сейчас работал в Мадриде.* | past condition, present result | *If I **had learned** Spanish, I **would be** working in Madrid now.* |
| *Если бы я говорил по-испански, я бы понял объявление.* | standing condition, past result | *If I **spoke** Spanish, I **would have understood** the announcement.* |

If the Russian carries *сейчас*, *теперь*, or *сегодня*, the condition is present and the second conditional is the target. If it carries *тогда*, *вчера*, *в тот день*, it is past and the third conditional is the target. If one half carries each, the sentence is mixed and each half takes its own form — see the last two rows, and [Real, unreal, and time](06-real-unreal-and-time.md) for why the system works this way.

### 6.3 The doubled marker

Russian puts **бы** in both halves. English puts a different marker in each half, and each one is banned from the other's territory.

```
   Russian    Если бы + [past],     [subject] бы + [past]
              Если бы я знал,       я бы сказал.
                     ↑ бы                  ↑ бы        — one particle, used twice

   English    If + [past form],     [subject] would + [base]
              If I knew,            I would tell you.
                   ↑ past tense           ↑ would      — two markers, one apiece
```

| ✗ The literal translation | ✓ English |
|---|---|
| *If I **would have** time, I would help.* | *If I **had** time, I would help.* |
| *If you **would ask** him, he would help.* | *If you **asked** him, he would help.* |
| *If I **would have known**, I would have told you.* | *If I **had known**, I would have told you.* |
| *If she **would be** here, she would explain.* | *If she **were** here, she would explain.* |

And the reverse slip, from learners who have absorbed "no *would* after *if*" and then apply it to the whole sentence: ✗ *If I had time, I **help** you.* The result clause keeps its *would*.

### 6.4 *If it weren't for* and *but for*

Russian *если бы не* is a single fixed phrase covering a range English splits across several.

| Russian | ✓ English | Register |
|---|---|---|
| *Если бы не ты, я бы сейчас был в беде.* | *If it **weren't for** you, I'd be in trouble right now.* | neutral, present |
| *Если бы не ты, я бы провалился.* | *If it **hadn't been for** you, I would have failed.* | neutral, past |
| *Если бы не твоя помощь, я бы провалился.* | ***But for** your help, I would have failed.* | formal; takes a noun only |
| *Если бы не дождь, мы бы закончили.* | ***If not for** the rain, we would have finished.* | American, common in speech and print |
| *Без тебя я бы не справился.* | ***Without** you, I wouldn't have managed.* | neutral, shortest |

The errors: ✗ *If not you, I would fail* (drops *for* and gets the tense wrong), ✗ *But for you helped me* (*but for* takes a noun, never a clause), and ✗ *If it wasn't for you* — which is ordinary American speech but *weren't* in writing, exactly as in [§2](#2-the-was-or-were-question).

### 6.5 *In case*, *in the event of*, and plain *if*

Russian *в случае* and *на случай* differ by one letter and split three ways in English.

| Russian | ✓ English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *на случай если пойдёт дождь* | *in case it rains* | a precaution taken in advance |
| *в случае если вам понадобится помощь* | *if you need help* | an ordinary condition |
| *в случае пожара* | *in case of fire* | sign-and-notice form, noun only |
| *в случае неуплаты* | *in the event of non-payment* | formal and contractual |
| *в случае если арендатор не платит* | *in the event that the tenant fails to pay* | formal, with a clause |
| *если что* | *if anything comes up* | conversational |

The error to watch for is the second row translated as ✗ *In case you need help, I'll come over*, which tells an American listener that you are coming over now, as a precaution. [§4.2](#42-in-case-is-not-if) has the test.

### 6.6 Aspect has no slot in an English conditional

Russian encodes completion and repetition in the verb itself, and the perfective–imperfective pair survives into the conditional untouched. English conditionals have no such slot. The distinction has to be carried by the choice of verb, by an adverb, or not at all.

| Russian | Aspect | ✓ English |
|---|---|---|
| *Если бы я **знал**…* | imperfective — a state | *If I **knew**…* |
| *Если бы я **узнал**…* | perfective — an event | *If I **found out**…* / *If I **had found out**…* |
| *Если бы он **приходил** каждый день…* | imperfective — repeated | *If he **came** every day…* |
| *Если бы он **пришёл**…* | perfective — once | *If he **came**…* / *If he **had come**…* |
| *Если бы я **прочитал** книгу…* | perfective — completed | *If I **had read** the book…* |

Three consequences, each of which produces its own error.

**English reuses one form where Russian uses two.** *Came* does duty for both *приходил* and *пришёл*, and that is not a loss the learner has to repair. Adding something to mark the difference is what creates the error.

**The continuous is not the imperfective.** Reaching for `-ing` to render an imperfective gives ✗ *If I **were knowing** the answer* and ✗ *If I **would be working** there*. The repairs are *If I knew* and *If I worked there*. Stative verbs — *know*, *be*, *have*, *want*, *believe* — refuse the continuous anyway, in conditionals as everywhere else.

**The perfect is not the perfective.** *Прочитал* is perfective, and it does not become *would have read*: ✗ *Если бы я прочитал книгу, я бы знал ответ* → ✓ *If I **had read** the book, I **would know** the answer.* The English perfect marks time relative to another point, not completion.

### 6.7 The rest of the transfers

| Russian | ✓ English | ✗ The literal version |
|---|---|---|
| *Я бы хотел кофе.* | *I **would like** a coffee.* | *I would want a coffee.* |
| *Не могли бы вы прислать это сегодня?* | ***Could** you send it today?* | *Couldn't you send it today?* — reads as a complaint |
| *Что если он откажется?* | *What if he **says** no?* | *What if he will say no?* |
| *Даже если он богат…* / *Хотя он богат…* | *Even **if** he's rich…* / *Even **though** he's rich…* | *Even he's rich…* |
| *Я не знаю, придёт **ли** он.* | *I don't know **whether** he's coming.* | *I don't know, if he is coming.* |
| *Мы отменим, если будет дождь.* | *We'll cancel if it rains.* | *We'll cancel, if it rains.* — no comma in English |
| *Если бы только я знал!* | *If only I **had known**!* | *If only I would know!* |

## Quick reference

**Six rules that prevent most conditional errors.**

1. **No *will* after *if*** — or after *when*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before*, *after*, *once*, *unless*, *in case*, or *provided that*. Use the present. The exceptions are willingness, insistence, and refusal, and you will know when you mean them.
2. **No *would* after *if*** in an unreal condition. The condition marks unreality with a past form; *would* lives in the result clause. The exception is the polite request: *If you would sign here.*
3. **Count the steps back.** Present form = real. One step (past simple) = unreal now. Two steps (past perfect) = unreal then. There is no third step.
4. **Write *were*, expect to hear *was*** — except in *If I were you* and after inversion, where only *were* is possible, and in real past conditions, where only *was* is.
5. **One modal per verb phrase.** *Would can* is *could*; *will must* is *will have to*.
6. **Comma in front, no comma behind.** *If it rains, we'll cancel.* &middot; *We'll cancel if it rains.*

**The four shapes, on one line each.**

| Type | Condition | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present | present | *If you heat water, it boils.* |
| First | present | *will* + base | *If it rains, we'll cancel.* |
| Second | past simple | *would* + base | *If it rained, we'd cancel.* |
| Third | past perfect | *would have* + participle | *If it had rained, we'd have canceled.* |
| Mixed | either | the other | *If it had rained, we'd be indoors now.* |

| 12 | *If I had the money, I **could** buy it.* | one modal per verb phrase — [§3.2](#32-two-modals-in-a-row) |
| 13 | *If she asked me, I **would say** yes.* | the result clause keeps its *would* — [§3.1](#31-would-in-both-clauses) |
| 14 | *Would you mind **closing** the window?* | *mind* takes `-ing` — [§3.5](#35-would-or-will-in-a-polite-request) |
| 15 | *I'd be grateful if you **would** confirm by Friday.* | the polite request takes *would*, not *will* — [§3.5](#35-would-or-will-in-a-polite-request) |
| 16 | *If I **was** there yesterday, I didn't see anything unusual.* | a real, open condition about the past — [§2](#2-the-was-or-were-question) |
| 17 | *If I had studied medicine, I **would be** a doctor now.* | *now* forces a present result — [§1.5](#15-one-past-too-many) |
| 18 | *Take a coat in case it **is** cold.* | *in case* takes the present, like every other condition — [§4.2](#42-in-case-is-not-if) |
