Alternatives to if#

Every way American English states a condition without the word if — the exception unless carves out, the terms provided that imposes, the premise given that accepts, the precaution in case takes, and the connectors that carry a condition with no conjunction at all.

← Conditionals · All grammar topics

If is the default conditional, and for most sentences it is also the right one. But English has roughly forty other ways to attach a condition to a clause, and hardly any of them is a stylistic variant of if. Each narrows the meaning in a particular direction. Unless names the one exception that would overturn the main clause. Provided that names a term somebody imposed. Even if grants the condition in advance and refuses to care. Only if closes off every other route. And in case — the one every Russian speaker gets wrong at least once — does not state a condition at all; it names a risk being insured against.

The difference is not a matter of register. Take an umbrella if it rains and Take an umbrella in case it rains are two different instructions: the first says wait and see, the second says take it now. You can go provided you're back by ten and You can go unless you're back by ten are not near-synonyms; the second is nonsense. Picking the wrong connector changes what the sentence tells somebody to do. Three facts run through the whole list.

No will in the condition. Whatever word introduces it, a clause about a future condition takes the present tense: unless it rains tomorrow, provided the parts arrive Monday, as long as nobody objects, in case the power goes out. Never ✗ unless it will rain, ✗ in case the power will go out. The exceptions are the connectors that are counterfactual by definition — but for, if it weren't for, if only — which need a past or past perfect precisely because the condition is known to be unmet.

Punctuation follows the part of speech, not the meaning. A fronted conditional clause takes a comma after it (Unless something changes, we close in June); the same clause placed second usually takes none. But several items here are not subordinating conjunctions at all. Otherwise, in that case, and either way are conjunctive adverbs and need a semicolon or a period in front of them. Or else is coordinating and takes a plain comma. But for, without, in case of, and in the event of are prepositions and take a noun phrase, never a clause. Fronted only if is stranger still: it inverts the main clause and takes no comma at all.

Real and unreal are carried by the verb, not the connector. Almost every connector here heads a real condition with a present tense and an unreal one with a past or past perfect, and the word itself never changes. The short list of connectors that cannot do both is the most useful thing on this page, and it is the second column of the table below.

The connectors themselves — pronunciation, Russian glosses, punctuation, and six examples each — are in the dictionary: 67 subordinators of condition. This section is about choosing between them.

Unless#

What it actually means#

Unless means except if. It does not mean if not, even though the two overlap most of the time. The difference is direction: if not states a condition and lets you draw the consequence, while unless asserts the main clause and then cuts one hole in it. The store closes at six unless there's a holiday sale claims the store closes at six and names the only circumstance in which the claim fails; The store closes at six if there isn't a holiday sale claims nothing until the condition is settled.

In ordinary sentences the two land in the same place, which is why they are usually taught as equivalents:

Unless somebody objects, I'll book the room for Thursday. = If nobody objects, I'll book the room for Thursday. Don't sign anything unless your lawyer has read it. = Don't sign anything if your lawyer hasn't read it. We'll be there by noon unless traffic is terrible. = We'll be there by noon if traffic isn't terrible.

The clause takes the present tense for future reference, exactly as an if clause does: ✗ unless it will rain → unless it rains. A fronted unless clause takes a comma; a trailing one does not.

The substitution test#

Here is the test that settles every hard case, and it comes straight out of the meaning. Replace unless with except if. If the sentence still works, unless is correct. If it doesn't, you need if … not.

SentenceWith except ifVerdict
We'll go ahead unless she objects.…except if she objects.✓ fine
Don't call unless it's urgent.…except if it's urgent.✓ fine
✗ I'd be happy unless it rained.…except if it rained✗ nonsense — say if it didn't rain
✗ Unless I had known, I'd have called.…except if I had known✗ nonsense — say If I hadn't known
✗ She'd look better unless she wore so much makeup.…except if she wore✗ nonsense — say if she didn't wear

Where unless cannot go#

1. Unreal conditions in the past. This is the flat prohibition. A past counterfactual says the thing did not happen; unless carves an exception out of something presented as holding, and there is nothing there to carve.

✗ Unless she had called, I would have left. → If she hadn't called, I would have left. ✗ Unless the company had disclosed the defect, the recall would have been larger. → If the company hadn't disclosed it…

2. Conditions whose failure produces the result. When the main clause states what follows from the condition not being met — rather than what holds except when it is met — unless breaks. The classic cases involve a reaction or an improvement:

✗ I'd be happy unless it rained. → I'd be happy if it didn't rain. ✗ He'd be a nice guy unless he had such a temper. → He'd be a nice guy if he didn't have such a temper.

The honest version of the rule: unless is perfectly good in a hypothetical when it really is an exception — I wouldn't take the job unless they doubled the salary, She wouldn't have signed unless her lawyer approved it. What kills the sentence is not unreality; it is asking unless to mean plain negation. The except if test catches both cases.

3. Noun clauses. If doubles as a complementizer in indirect questions. Unless never does.

✗ I don't know unless he's coming. → I don't know if / whether he's coming. ✗ She asked unless the room was free. → She asked whether the room was free.

4. Scalar and elliptical if not. Two of the jobs if not does have nothing to do with negative conditions, and unless cannot take them over.

The repair will cost hundreds, if not thousands. (✗ unless thousands) Let me know by Friday. If not, I'll give the spot away. (✗ Unless, I'll give the spot away)

5. Doubled negatives. Unless already carries the negation, so a negative inside the clause usually means the writer lost track: ✗ Don't call unless you don't need anything → Don't call unless you need something. A sentence like Nobody leaves unless the alarm doesn't stop is parseable, and editors rewrite it every time.

Questions, replies, and afterthoughts#

Unless is at home in questions, where it names the escape hatch, and in replies, where it usually appears as not unless — an unless clause with the refusal left unsaid. It also works as an afterthought that reopens something the speaker had just settled, often as a bare fragment after a dash or a period.

Do they refund the deposit unless you cancel in writing? "Are you going to the reunion?" "Not unless Danny goes." "Can we still get a table?" "Not unless somebody cancels." I'll just mail it. Unless you need it today? We'll take the early flight — unless you'd rather sleep in.

Unless and until#

A legal doublet that closes two gaps at once: the thing does not happen unless the condition is met, and it does not happen until it is met. The main clause is nearly always negative, and the three words never take an internal comma.

No payment is due unless and until the work passes inspection. The account stays frozen unless and until a court says otherwise. Unless and until the tenant cures the default, the lease remains in breach.

Outside contracts and administrative prose, plain until says everything the sentence needs. The positive counterpart is if and when (We'll cross that bridge if and when we come to it).

The proviso family#

Five connectors that all mean "on this requirement," differing in who set the requirement and how binding it is. Every one of them prefers a condition somebody can actually control, which is why ✗ provided that it rains sounds wrong and provided that you bring the receipt does not.

ConnectorWho imposed itRegisterTypical home
provided (that)unspecified — just a requirementneutral to formalrules, policies, ordinary prose
providing (that)sameslightly informal, rarer in edited prosespeech, journalism
on condition thata person, who can enforce itformaldeals, permissions, reported agreements
on / with the understanding thatboth sides, informallyneutral to formalnegotiations, letters, minutes
with the proviso thatthe grantor, cutting back the grantformalminutes, resolutions, acceptances

Provided (that) and providing (that)#

Provided is frozen from the verb, so it never inflects — not ✗ provides that, not ✗ provided for that. That is optional in both forms and drops freely in speech. Negatives sit inside the clause in the ordinary way, questions are uncommon but grammatical, and unreal conditions are fine as long as the condition is still a stipulation.

You may return the item provided that you have the receipt. Provided nothing changes, the closing is on the ninth. The city will fund the repairs providing that the county matches the grant. She'll speak at the conference, provided her travel is covered. Can I return it provided I still have the tags? I'd take the job provided they covered the move.

A punctuation note. Legal drafting writes ; provided, however, that…, with commas on both sides. That formula is a survival of nineteenth-century contract style, it makes the sentence harder to parse, and it belongs nowhere else. In ordinary prose: comma after a fronted provided clause, and an optional comma before a trailing one — use it if the proviso is an afterthought, leave it out if it is part of the core statement.

On condition that#

The difference from provided that is that somebody is standing behind the term and can enforce it. There is always a grantor, and the clause almost always follows the main clause.

He agreed to testify on condition that his name never appear in print. They let him out on condition that he stay within the county. The loan was granted on condition that the company cut its dividend. She'll release the recording on the condition that both parties approve the transcript. I'll do it on one condition: that nobody mentions my name.

Look at those verbs. Formal writing puts the base-form subjunctive in an on condition that clause — not ✗ on condition that his name never appears. The same subjunctive appears after with the proviso that and is optional after provided that. It is tenseless, so nothing in it can be backshifted; see the subjunctive in reported speech.

On the understanding that and with the proviso that#

On the understanding that is the softest member of the family. It names an expectation both sides are taken to have agreed to without writing it down — and the usual reason for saying it aloud is that the speaker suspects it may not be shared. With the understanding that is the commoner American wording. With the proviso that is the hardest-edged: it announces in advance that a qualification is coming and cuts back a grant just made, which is why it takes a comma before it.

She agreed to chair the committee on the understanding that it would meet twice a year. We released the figures with the understanding that they wouldn't be published before Monday. The board approved the budget, with the proviso that the hiring freeze stay in place. The council granted the permit, with the proviso that work stop at seven each evening. He agreed to the interview, with the proviso that the tape not be edited.

As long as, so long as, just as long as#

The condition#

As long as sets a standing requirement: the main clause holds for exactly the cases in which the condition does. So long as is identical and a shade more formal; just as long as is emphatic and insists that the one requirement is the only one.

You can borrow the truck as long as you bring it back with a full tank. As long as everyone's comfortable with the date, I'll send the invitations. I don't care where we eat as long as it isn't that pizza place again. So long as the numbers hold up, we'll expand in the fall. Invite whoever you want, just as long as you tell me the headcount by Thursday.

The time meaning#

The same three words also measure duration, and then they are not conditional at all. Nothing in the form distinguishes the two readings — only what the clause is about.

ReadingExampleParaphrase
ConditionYou can stay as long as you pay rent.on the requirement that
DurationYou can stay as long as you like.for whatever length of time
ConditionThe offer stands as long as the stock lasts.provided there is stock
DurationHe talked as long as anybody would listen.for the whole time that

Two tests separate them. First, substitute provided that: it fits the conditional reading and produces nonsense on the temporal one (✗ You can stay provided that you like). Second, look for a stretch of time inside the clause. As long as you like, as long as it takes, as long as I live, as long as the battery holds out are durations; as long as you're careful, as long as nobody minds are conditions. Some sentences are genuinely ambiguous — I'll keep the car as long as it runs is either "for the whole period during which it runs" or "on the condition that it keeps running," and in practice the two amount to the same policy.

The "since you're already" use#

Conversational American English has a third as long as that is neither condition nor duration. It means "given that the situation already exists, we might as well," it is closest to since, and it works only when the clause describes something already true.

As long as you're up, could you get me a glass of water? As long as we're here, let's take a look at the new wing. As long as I have you on the phone — did the invoice ever go out?

In case: precaution, not condition#

This is the section to read twice. An in case clause does not state a condition — it names a risk, and the main clause is the precaution taken against it. The main clause happens first, because of the possibility, whether or not the possibility ever materializes.

Take an umbrella in case it rains. (take it now, rain or no rain) Take an umbrella if it rains. (take nothing until you see rain)

Those are two different instructions to two different people on two different mornings.

The contrast in full#

SentenceWhat you doWhen

More precautions, with the main clause always describing something done in advance:

I wrote down the address in case my phone dies. She keeps a spare charger in her bag in case somebody needs one. We booked two rooms in case the flight gets in late.

Tense inside the clause#

Present tense for a future risk, and never ✗ will. Past tense for a precaution taken in the past — real past time, not unreality. Formal writing sometimes inserts should to mark the risk as remote, which changes nothing but the register.

Save the receipt in case the part doesn't fit. (✗ will not fit) I took an umbrella in case it rained. Keep the receipt in case the part should need replacing.

In case of + noun phrase#

A preposition, not a conjunction. It takes a noun phrase, means "if there is," and lives on signs, in instructions, and in policies. It is the one member of the family that reads as a real condition rather than a precaution.

In case of fire, use the stairs. In case of emergency, break glass. In case of rain, the ceremony moves indoors.

It cannot take a clause: ✗ in case of the fire starts, ✗ in case of it rains. If a clause is coming, the connector is in case, if, or in the event that. And do not confuse it with in the case of, which is not conditional at all and means "as regards": In the case of part-time employees, the waiting period is ninety days.

Just in case#

Just intensifies the precaution and frequently lets the clause disappear altogether. Bare just in case is a complete adverbial.

Leave a key with the neighbor just in case. Take the charger just in case we get stuck at the airport. "Do you really need three copies?" "Just in case."

What Russian speakers get wrong here#

Russian splits this ground differently, and the mapping is not one to one.

The error that follows from the gap is using in case where English needs if:

✗ In case you fail the exam, you can retake it in August. → If you fail the exam, you can retake it in August. ✗ In case the store is closed, we'll go somewhere else. → If the store is closed, we'll go somewhere else. ✗ Let me know in case you need help. → Let me know if you need help.

The test: does the main clause happen before the risk or after it? Before → in case. After → if.

One honest complication. American English does use in case where the precaution reading is thin, most visibly in the discourse opener — In case you're wondering, the meeting got moved to Thursday; In case it helps, here's the original email. That is still precautionary in shape, since the speaker supplies the information in advance to cover a possibility, but it sits close enough to if that learners hear it as evidence the distinction does not hold. It does hold everywhere else.

Even if on its own#

Even if grants the condition the maximum benefit of the doubt and says the main clause holds anyway. It works at all three levels of reality, and negatives and questions behave normally.

Even if the flight leaves on time, we'll miss the connection in Denver. (real) Even if somebody offered him double, he wouldn't sell the house. (unreal present) Even if I had known, I wouldn't have said anything. (unreal past) Would you still go even if nobody else did?

The neighbors#

  • Even when is factual and repeated — the main clause holds on every occasion, and the occasion is not hypothetical: He's polite even when he's furious.
  • Even so is a conjunctive adverb, not a conjunction, and points backward at a whole previous sentence: The reviews were terrible. Even so, it sold out.
  • Even alone is a focusing adverb and states nothing conditional: She didn't even call.

The mistake to avoid is even if for something that actually happened: ✗ Even if it was raining, we went → Even though it was raining, we went. The full concessive family is in the concession file of the dictionary.

Whether (or not): the double condition#

Whether states a condition twice, once on each branch, and says the outcome is the same either way. That is why it feels concessive: it does not make the main clause depend on anything.

Whether or not you agree with the policy, you have to follow it. The train leaves at six whether or not everyone is on board. Whether it rains or not, the ceremony is outdoors. He's going to say something whether we ask him to or not. Whether we drive or fly, we'll need to leave by noon.

Three shapes, all standard:

ShapeExample
whether or not + clauseWhether or not he shows up, we start at nine.
whether + clause + or not (split)Whether he shows up or not, we start at nine.
whether + clause + or + clauseWhether he shows up or sends his deputy, we start at nine.

In the adverbial use above, or not cannot be dropped — ✗ Whether you agree, you have to follow it is not a sentence. In the noun-clause use it can, because the alternative is already built into whether: I don't know whether (or not) she got the message; It depends on whether (or not) the landlord approves the sublet.

Whether versus if#

They overlap in noun clauses and nowhere else. There are four places if cannot go:

Positionwhetherif
After a prepositionIt depends on whether he calls.✗ on if he calls
Before an infinitiveShe hasn't decided whether to accept.✗ if to accept
As the subjectWhether he comes is up to him.✗ If he comes is up to him.
Directly before or notI don't know whether or not it cleared.✗ if or not

Whether also removes a genuine ambiguity that if creates in requests: Let me know if he calls means either "report back either way" or "only in the event that he calls," while Let me know whether he calls means only the first. More on the split in Reported questions.

The emphatic relatives#

Regardless of whether and no matter whether are heavier versions of the same thing, and the no matter / -ever family extends it to every other question word. All of them cover the entire range of possibilities — which is another way of saying they are not really conditions, and that is exactly the effect.

Regardless of whether the bond passes, the district is cutting three positions. No matter what she says, don't sign it. Whatever happens, call me when you land. However you get here, get here by six.

Only if and if only#

Two phrases made of the same two words, doing unrelated jobs. Only if tightens a condition; if only expresses a wish.

Only if: the necessary condition#

Plain if states a sufficient condition — this is enough, and other routes may exist. Only if states a necessary one — this and nothing else.

SentenceWhat it allows
We ship if you sign.signing works; something else might too
We ship only if you sign.nothing but signing works
We ship if and only if you sign.signing works, and nothing else does

We'll refund the deposit only if you cancel a week in advance. The policy pays out only if the damage exceeds the deductible.

Two structural facts. In end position, only if takes no comma before it. In front position, it forces subject–auxiliary inversion in the main clause — not in its own clause — and takes no comma either:

Only if the tests come back clean will they reopen the plant. Only if you ask will she tell you. ✗ Only if you ask, she will tell you. → Only if you ask will she tell you.

That inversion is shared with only when, only after, not until, under no circumstances, and the rest of the negative-and-restrictive fronting family, and it is worked through in Inversion and formal conditionals.

If and only if is the two-way version, standard in mathematics, logic, and contract drafting, where it is often abbreviated iff. And only if and not unless set the same terms from opposite ends, one as a policy and one as a refusal.

A number is even if and only if it is divisible by two. They'll release the records only if a judge orders it. = They won't release the records, not unless a judge orders it.

If only: the wish#

If only is counterfactual by definition. The verb shifts back one step, exactly as after wish: past for an unfulfilled present, past perfect for an unfulfilled past, would for something annoying that somebody refuses to change. It needs no main clause — the regret is the whole sentence.

FormMeaningExample
if only + pastit isn't so nowIf only I knew her number.
if only + wereit isn't so nowIf only it were that simple.
if only + past perfectit wasn't so thenIf only we'd left an hour earlier.
if only + wouldsomebody won't change itIf only he would listen.

If only I had known you were in town. If only the rent were two hundred dollars cheaper. If only she'd asked, I would have driven her.

There is a second, unrelated if only, followed by to + infinitive. It means "even if for no other reason than," and carries no wish and no regret.

Call him back, if only to end the argument. Read the first chapter, if only to see what the fuss is about.

The two share nothing but their spelling, and neither one is ✗ If only you ask will they tell you — that sentence wants only if. The wish use is treated in full, alongside wish, would rather, and it's time, in Wishes and regrets.

The conversational conditionals#

Suppose, supposing, say, let's say, imagine, what if, and how about if all launch a hypothetical in speech. They share three properties: they are essentially clause-initial, they commonly stand as fragments with no main clause, and they take all three tenses, with the tense doing exactly what it does after if.

Tense in the clauseEffectExample
Presenta real proposal or a live worrySuppose we leave at five — do we still hit traffic?
Pastheld at a distance; tentative or unlikelySuppose we left at five. Would that help?
Past perfectcounterfactual, about something already settledSuppose we had left at five. Would we have made it?

Suppose and supposing (that)#

Suppose you miss the connection. What's your backup? Supposing the ferry stops running, how do we get back? Suppose you had a week off — where would you go? Suppose she had said yes. Would you have moved?

Supposing is essentially clause-initial: ✗ We'd be stuck supposing the ferry stopped running does not work, where …if the ferry stopped running would. A separate, non-conditional suppose means "I think" (I suppose so, I don't suppose you have a pen?) and has nothing to do with this.

Say, let's say, and imagine#

Say is the most casual member and nearly always sets up a question or a thought experiment rather than a rule. Imagine is the one member of the group that also takes a noun phrase or an -ing clause, because it is still a transitive verb — and imagine if is a fixed doubling the others do not allow (✗ suppose if, ✗ say if).

Say you miss the connection — what's your backup? Let's say the bid comes in over budget; then what? Imagine you're standing at the top of that ramp. Imagine if the vote had gone the other way. Imagine losing your keys on the first day.

Do not confuse conditional say with the "for example" say, which is fenced by commas on both sides and names an instance rather than a hypothesis: Give it a few days — say, until Thursday — and call them again.

What if and how about if#

What if raises a hypothetical and leaves the consequence for the listener to supply. It is a fragment ending in a question mark, and that is standard, not an error. The same shape does two opposite jobs: a proposal, where the past tense holds the suggestion at a polite distance rather than putting it in past time, and a worry, where a risk is named and nothing is offered.

What if we just told them the truth? (proposal) What if I said I could get you a table at seven? (proposal) What if it rains on Saturday? (worry) What if the flight gets canceled — do we still have the hotel? (worry) What if we'd taken the other offer? (worry, counterfactual)

How about if and what about if are nearly always offers rather than worries, and they expect an answer. So what if is the fixed dismissive — it grants the fact and denies that it matters.

How about if we meet at six instead? What about if I drive and you navigate? So what if he's ten minutes late? The doors don't open until eight.

The premise family and the formal set#

Assuming (that), given (that), granted (that)#

All three take a premise rather than a condition. They differ in how much weight the speaker puts on it.

ConnectorStatus of the premiseWhat comes next
assuming (that)unverified — flagged as an assumptionreasoning forward
given (that)established — treated as agreedreasoning forward, shading into cause
granted (that)conceded — accepted for the sake of argumentreasoning against it

Assuming the numbers hold, we break even in the third quarter. We'll be there by seven, assuming traffic isn't awful. Assuming that she accepts, when would she start?

Given that is halfway between if and because, and it also takes a bare noun phrase. Granted that concedes and then pushes past; bare Granted, with a comma, does the same job as a standalone adverb.

Given that the road is closed, we'll take the long way. Given the circumstances, I think she handled it well. Granted that the design is elegant, it's still far too expensive to build. Granted, the first season is slow, but it pays off. Granting that the witness is credible, the timeline still doesn't work.

In the event (that) and in the event of#

The formal-register equivalent of if, reserved for conditions that are occurrences rather than states — a cancellation, a default, a death, a claim. In ordinary prose it is longer than if without being clearer, and editors cut it. That is frequently dropped in contracts (in the event the tenant defaults), and the of version takes a noun phrase and never a clause: ✗ in the event of the flight is canceled.

In the event that the flight is canceled, the airline will rebook you at no charge. The deposit is refundable in the event that the inspection fails. In the event of a power outage, the generator starts automatically. Benefits are paid to the beneficiary in the event of the policyholder's death.

Should: the tentative condition#

Fronted should marks a future condition as possible but not expected, and it carries none of the obligation sense should has elsewhere. Should you need anything means "if you happen to need anything," never "you ought to need anything."

Should you need anything, the front desk is open all night. Should the tenant fail to pay, the landlord may terminate the lease. Call this number should your symptoms return. If you should see her before Friday, tell her the check is in the mail.

Three rules go with it. Never combine it with if — write If you should need anything or Should you need anything, never ✗ If should you need anything. Never contract the negative — Should you not wish to renew, never ✗ Shouldn't you wish, which is a question. And it is formal: business letters, customer service, insurance, leases. In conversation it sounds like a form letter. Should is one of three auxiliaries that head a conditional clause with no conjunction at all; had and were are the other two, and all three are in Inversion and formal conditionals.

The noun-phrase conditionals#

Five connectors that take a noun phrase instead of a clause. Most are counterfactual, which means the main clause has to carry would or would have.

But for#

Names the one factor without which the main clause would have gone the other way. This but is not the coordinating conjunction; it is the older but meaning "except." The construction is the but-for test of causation in American law, which is where most readers meet it. It takes a noun phrase and only a noun phrase: ✗ but for you helped me, ✗ but for the storm came.

But for the storm, the ceremony would have been outdoors. But for your help, I'd still be sitting in that parking lot. The company would have folded in 2009 but for an emergency loan.

If not for, if it weren't for, if it hadn't been for#

The everyday American versions. The short one is time-neutral; the two long ones pin the time down — if it weren't for at a present state, if it hadn't been for at a past event.

If not for the traffic, we would have been on time. If it weren't for the noise, this would be a perfect apartment. If it hadn't been for her, nobody would have caught the mistake. If it weren't for bad luck, he'd have no luck at all.

If it wasn't for is extremely common in speech and still flagged in edited prose; were is the safe written choice. The inverted were it not for and had it not been for are the formal register of the same idea, and they are in Inversion and formal conditionals.

Without and with#

Without is the plain-English member of the family and the only one nobody would call stiff. It takes a noun phrase or an -ing form and does both real and unreal conditions — the main clause tells you which. With is the positive counterpart, used less often and working the same way. Both also appear in the absolute construction, with a noun plus a participle or adjective.

Without the volunteers, the food bank would have closed in March. (unreal — there were volunteers) You can't board without a valid ID. (real — a standing rule) Without a second income, they never would have kept the house. She'd still be waiting on a diagnosis without her sister's pushing. With another week, we could have tested it properly. With the bridge closed, the detour adds forty minutes. Without the furnace running, the pipes will freeze by morning.

The conditional reading appears only when without governs the whole situation. In He left without saying goodbye it is the everyday preposition of lacking, modifying the verb, and no condition is being stated.

Otherwise#

A conjunctive adverb, which is why it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it and a comma after it. A bare comma before otherwise is a comma splice — the single most common punctuation error with this word, because it feels like a conjunction and is not one.

Take the earlier train; otherwise, you'll miss the opening. We need a deposit by Friday. Otherwise, the room goes back into inventory. ✗ Write it down, otherwise you'll forget. → Write it down; otherwise, you'll forget.

It also runs backward, as an inference marker rather than a warning. The second clause then carries would:

She must have left already — otherwise her coat would still be here. The tickets are nonrefundable; otherwise, I'd have canceled weeks ago.

Otherwise can also sit mid-clause (we'd otherwise be glad to give you another week), and or otherwise is a different word entirely — the plain adverb meaning "in other ways" (damaged or otherwise), with none of the punctuation rules above.

Or and or else#

Or else is coordinating, so it takes an ordinary comma, exactly like plain or. It names what happens if the first clause is not carried out — the coordinated twin of unless, and bare or does the same work with less menace.

Leave now, or else you'll hit rush hour. "Turn the music down, or else." Hurry up, or we'll miss it. Write it down, or you'll forget.

Do it, or else you're fired and You're fired unless you do it state the same deal from opposite ends — and note the punctuation split that goes with it: the coordinator takes its comma, the subordinator does not.

If so, if not, in that case, in which case#

Elliptical conditionals: the clause has been deleted and is recovered from the sentence before. In that case is a conjunctive adverb and needs the same heavy punctuation as otherwise; in which case heads a relative clause and attaches with a comma. Either way covers both branches, and failing that names a fallback.

Have you already filed? If so, ignore this notice. Is the room free at two? If not, we can meet in the lobby. Let me know by Friday. If not, I'll give the spot to somebody else. "The nine o'clock is full." "In that case, we'll drive." The inspection could turn up asbestos, in which case the closing gets pushed to May. Either way, somebody has to call the client. Try the pharmacy on Third. Failing that, there's one by the hospital.

And and or as conditionals#

The most compressed conditional in English uses no conditional word at all. An imperative joined to a clause by and means "if you do this"; joined by or it means "if you don't."

ShapeReads asExample
imperative + and + clauseif you do X, Y followsSay that again and I'm leaving.
imperative + or + clauseif you don't do X, Y followsHurry up or we'll miss it.
noun phrase + and + clauseif that happens, Y followsOne more word and you're out.

The and pattern works as a threat, a promise, or a plain prediction — the content of the two clauses decides which. The or pattern is always negative: it names the bad outcome of not acting.

Say that again and I'm leaving. (threat) Finish this and you can go home. (promise) Give him five minutes and he'll talk you into it. (prediction) Take the 405 at four o'clock and you're there for an hour. (prediction, no imperative force at all) One more word and you're out. Hurry up or we'll miss it. Keep your voice down or they'll hear you in the hallway.

Three things to notice. The first clause is not really an order — Take the 405 at four o'clock and you're there for an hour is not advice but a conditional dressed as an imperative. The tense in the second clause is present or future, never past; a past second clause turns the sentence into ordinary narrative (He said that and I left is a report, not a condition). The register is spoken — conversation, dialogue, advertising, and instructions, not formal prose, where if is the right word.

Common mistakes#

WrongRightWhy
✗ Unless it will rain, we'll eat outside.Unless it rains, we'll eat outside.A conditional clause takes the present for future time, whatever the connector.
✗ I'd be happy unless it rained.I'd be happy if it didn't rain.Unless means "except if." Here the negative condition causes the result instead of excepting it.
✗ Unless she had called, I would have left.If she hadn't called, I would have left.Unless cannot head a past counterfactual.
✗ I don't know unless he's coming.I don't know whether he's coming.Unless is never a complementizer.
✗ In case you fail, you can retake it in August.If you fail, you can retake it in August.The retake happens after the failure, so it is a condition, not a precaution.
✗ Take an umbrella in case it will rain.Take an umbrella in case it rains.No will in an in case clause either.
✗ In case of it rains, use the tent.In case of rain, use the tent.In case of is a preposition and takes a noun phrase.
✗ Even if it was raining, we went anyway.Even though it was raining, we went anyway.It rained. A fact takes even though.
✗ Even though he offers me double, I won't sell.Even if he offers me double, I won't sell.He hasn't offered. An open possibility takes even if.
✗ Whether you agree, you have to follow it.Whether or not you agree, you have to follow it.In the adverbial use, or not is not optional.
✗ It depends on if he calls.It depends on whether he calls.If cannot follow a preposition.
✗ Only if you ask, she will tell you.Only if you ask will she tell you.Fronted only if inverts the main clause and takes no comma.
✗ If only you ask will they tell you.Only if you ask will they tell you.If only is a wish; only if is a condition.
✗ If only I know her number.If only I knew her number.If only is counterfactual: the verb shifts back one step.
✗ We'd manage supposing the power went out.We'd manage if the power went out.Supposing is clause-initial; it does not trail the main clause.
✗ If should you need anything, call the desk.Should you need anything, call the desk.The inversion replaces if; the two never co-occur.
✗ But for you helped me, I'd still be stuck.But for your help, I'd still be stuck.But for takes a noun phrase, never a clause.
✗ Write it down, otherwise you'll forget.Write it down; otherwise, you'll forget.Otherwise is a conjunctive adverb, so a comma alone is a splice.
✗ Provided that it rains, the game is canceled.If it rains, the game is canceled.The proviso family wants a requirement somebody can meet, not a weather event.
✗ He'll testify on condition that his name never appears.He'll testify on condition that his name never appear.Formal on condition that takes the base-form subjunctive.

Quick reference#

Pick the connector in four questions.

  1. Is the clause a fact, or still open? A fact takes even though, although, given that. An open possibility takes everything else on this page.
  2. Does the main clause happen before the condition or after it? Before → in case. After → if, unless, provided that, and the rest.
  3. Is the condition an exception, a requirement, or a premise? Exception → unless. Requirement → provided that, as long as, on condition that, only if. Premise → assuming that, given that, granted that.
  4. Is it real or counterfactual? Counterfactual rules out unless, in the event that, given that, and should, and rules in but for, if it weren't for, had, and were.

The five one-line contrasts to memorize.

Take an umbrella if it rains. ≠ Take an umbrella in case it rains. — wait and see, versus take it now. We'll go unless it rains. = We'll go if it doesn't rain. — but only when except if also fits. Even though it rained, we went. ≠ Even if it rains, we're going. — a fact, versus an open possibility. She'll tell you if you ask. ≠ She'll tell you only if you ask. — sufficient, versus necessary. Only if you ask will she tell you. ≠ If only you'd asked. — a condition, versus a regret.

What cannot head an unreal condition
unless, in the event (that), in case of, given that, inverted should, imperative + and / or.
What can only head an unreal condition
if only, but for, if not for, if it weren't for, if it hadn't been for, were it not for, had it not been for, inverted had, inverted were.
What takes a noun phrase, not a clause
in case of, in the event of, but for, if not for, if it weren't for, were it not for, without, with, given + noun, regardless of.
What takes a semicolon or period in front of it
otherwise, in that case, either way, failing that. (Or else and in which case take a comma.)

Practice#

Rewrite each sentence using the connector in brackets, keeping the meaning. Some of them cannot be rewritten at all — say so, and say why.

  1. If you don't have a receipt, we can't process the return. [unless]
  2. I'd have been furious if she hadn't apologized. [unless]
  3. I'd be much happier if it didn't rain every weekend. [unless]
  4. You can use the car if you fill the tank. [provided that]
  5. He agreed to testify, but only if his name stayed out of print. [on condition that]
  6. Take a flashlight — the power might go out. [in case]
  7. If the fire alarm sounds, use the stairs. [in case of]
  8. It rained, and we went anyway. [even though / even if — pick one]
  9. He won't sell the house, and it wouldn't matter if you offered double. [even if]
  10. It makes no difference whether you agree; the rule stands. [whether or not]
  11. They'll reopen the plant only after the tests come back clean. [fronted only if, with inversion]
  12. I wish I'd left an hour earlier. [if only]
  13. Imagine the ferry stopped running. How would we get back? [supposing]
  14. If you happen to need anything, the front desk is open all night. [should, inverted]
  15. We would have been on time if there hadn't been traffic. [if not for]
  16. If she hadn't pushed, he'd still be waiting for a diagnosis. [without]
  17. We have to leave by six; if we don't, we're eating at ten. [or else]
  18. Take the earlier train, because you'll miss the opening if you don't. [otherwise]
  19. Say that again, and I'm leaving. [if]
  20. I don't know if the check cleared. [whether — and say why if is riskier]

1We can't process the return unless you have a receipt.
2Not possible. Unless cannot head a past counterfactual: ✗ unless she had apologized. Keep if she hadn't apologized.
3Not possible. The negative condition causes the result rather than excepting it: ✗ unless it rained. Keep if it didn't rain.
4You can use the car provided (that) you fill the tank.

11Only if the tests come back clean will they reopen the plant. (Inverted main clause, no comma.)
12If only I'd left an hour earlier. (Past perfect for an unfulfilled past.)

One more pass#

Write the negative and the question form of items 1, 6, and 9.
