# Inversion and formal conditionals

> Conditions built without the word *if* — the three auxiliaries that jump in front of the subject, the fronted negatives that carry conditional force, and the legal, academic, and literary registers where plain *if* is the last word anyone uses.

[← Conditionals](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

English can mark a condition without *if*. The main way it does so is by moving an auxiliary in front of the subject — the same movement that makes a question, put to an entirely different job:

```
    if-version                       inverted version
    ---------------------------------------------------------
    If I had known …            →    Had I known …
    If I were you …             →    Were I you …
    If you should need … …      →    Should you need …
```

Nothing is added; the conjunction is deleted and one word moves. The meaning does not change at all. What changes is **register**: every inverted conditional is more formal than its *if* twin, and two of the three are close to unusable in casual speech.

Only **three** auxiliaries do this in modern American English — *were*, *had*, and *should*. There is no fourth. You cannot invert *did*, *would*, *could*, *will*, or *do*, and a learner who tries produces sentences that are either archaic poetry or nothing at all: ✗ *Did I know, I would tell you.*

A note for speakers of Russian, because the interference is predictable. Russian marks unreality once, with the particle «бы», and it marks it in both clauses at the same strength, with no distinction between present and past: *Если бы я знал* covers both *if I knew* and *if I had known*. English splits the work in two — the **tense** of the auxiliary says which time frame you are in, and the **position** of the auxiliary says you are stating a condition. That is why inversion is possible at all: once *had* is sitting in front of the subject, it is already doing both jobs, and *if* has nothing left to contribute.

## The three inversions at a glance

| Inversion | Built from | Time frame | Plain version | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***Were* + subject** | second conditional | unreal present or future | *if I were*, *if it were* | formal, literary; fixed in *were it not for* |
| ***Were* + subject + *to*** | second conditional with *were to* | hypothetical, unlikely future | *if I were to*, *if it were to* | formal writing, proposals, analysis |
| ***Had* + subject** | third conditional | unreal past | *if I had* | formal writing, narration; the commonest of the three |
| ***Should* + subject** | first conditional made remote | open but unlikely future | *if you should*, *if you happen to* | business correspondence, notices, instructions |

The four patterns being inverted here are set out in [The four types](01-the-four-types.md) and [Mixed conditionals and variations](02-mixed-and-variations.md); the connectives that replace *if* without inverting anything are in [Alternatives to *if*](03-alternatives-to-if.md). What follows is three form-and-register studies, then the constructions that carry a condition with no inversion at all: fronted negatives, the legal *provided always that* family, academic hedges, and the frozen expressions where a whole conditional clause has collapsed into two or three words. The connectives named throughout have full dictionary entries — pronunciation, Russian gloss, pattern, punctuation, and six examples each — in [the conditional-connector catalog](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md), and the auxiliaries themselves are laid out in [the modal-verb catalog](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md).

## *Were I you*: the second conditional inverted

### The form

Take a second conditional, delete *if*, and move the subjunctive *were* in front of the subject. The main clause is untouched — it keeps *would*, *could*, or *might* exactly as it had them.

> *If I **were** you, I'd take the offer.*  →  ***Were** I you, I'd take the offer.*

> *If she **were** here, she would know what to do.*  →  ***Were** she here, she would know what to do.*

| *if*-clause | Inverted |
|---|---|
| *if I were* &middot; *if you were* | *were I* &middot; *were you* |
| *if he / she / it were* | *were he / she / it* |
| *if we were* &middot; *if they were* | *were we* &middot; *were they* |
| *if there were* | *were there* |
| *if it were not for* | *were it not for* |
| *if I were to* | *were I to* |

**Only *were* inverts — never *was*.** American speech allows *if I was you* and *if he was here*; the inversion does not. There is no ✗ *Was I you*, no ✗ *Was he here*. If the clause you want to invert has *was* in it, the inversion forces you back to the subjunctive:

> *If I **was** in your position, I'd say something.*  →  ***Were** I in your position, I'd say something.*  (Not ✗ *Was I in your position*.)

This is the one place where the *was* / *were* argument has a hard answer. Everywhere else the two compete; here *were* wins by default, because the alternative does not exist.

### A dense example set

| Inverted | Plain *if* version |
|---|---|
| ***Were** I you, I'd get that in writing.* | *If I were you …* |
| ***Were** it up to me, we'd start an hour later.* | *If it were up to me …* |
| ***Were** the decision mine, I would have made it differently.* | *If the decision were mine …* |
| ***Were** he any good at his job, we wouldn't be having this conversation.* | *If he were any good …* |
| ***Were** she less careful, the whole thing would have collapsed years ago.* | *If she were less careful …* |
| ***Were** they serious about the deadline, they'd have sent the files by now.* | *If they were serious …* |
| ***Were** there any real doubt, the board would have postponed the vote.* | *If there were any real doubt …* |
| ***Were** the numbers accurate, the company would already be insolvent.* | *If the numbers were accurate …* |
| ***Were** that true, everything in the report would have to be rewritten.* | *If that were true …* |
| ***Were** I not his sister, I'd say something to him myself.* | *If I weren't his sister …* |
| *I'd take the earlier flight **were** I traveling alone.* | *… if I were traveling alone.* |
| *We would move the meeting **were** it not already on the calendar.* | *… if it weren't already on the calendar.* |

The last two show the inverted clause **after** the main clause, which is perfectly normal and takes no comma. A fronted one always takes one.

### *Were it not for*

The most useful member of the family and the one you will meet most often. It takes a **noun phrase**, not a clause, and means "if [something] did not exist."

> ***Were it not for** the rain, we'd be on the road already.*

> ***Were it not for** her testimony, the case would have been dropped.*

> ***Were it not for** the grant, the lab would have closed in 2019.*

A variant takes a *that*-clause instead: ***Were it not that** the deadline is tomorrow, I'd rewrite the whole thing.* It is stiff even by the standards of this construction — the entry is in the [connector catalog](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#were-it-not-that).

Both fixed phrases sit on the same four-step register ladder, and the past-tense member is covered in the next section:

| Present / future | Past | Register |
|---|---|---|
| *But for the rain, we'd be on the road.* | *But for the fire drill, no one would have left.* | legal, very formal |
| *Were it not for the rain, …* | *Had it not been for the fire drill, …* | formal writing |
| *If it weren't for the rain, …* | *If it hadn't been for the fire drill, …* | neutral, everyday |
| *If not for the rain, …* | *If not for the fire drill, …* | neutral, clipped |

### *Were* + subject + *to*-infinitive

The *were to* conditional pushes an event into the **hypothetical or unlikely future**, and it inverts the same way.

> *If the bridge **were to** close, the detour would add an hour.*  →  ***Were** the bridge **to** close, the detour would add an hour.*

| Inverted | Plain *if* version |
|---|---|
| ***Were** she **to** call, tell her I'm in a meeting.* | *If she were to call …* |
| ***Were** the company **to** relocate, most of the staff would not follow.* | *If the company were to relocate …* |
| ***Were** the policy **to** take effect in January, employers would have six weeks.* | *If the policy were to take effect …* |
| ***Were** we **to** accept your terms, we would need the schedule in writing.* | *If we were to accept …* |
| ***Were** the court **to** rule otherwise, every contract of this kind would be void.* | *If the court were to rule otherwise …* |
| ***Were** anything **to** go wrong, the backup system takes over automatically.* | *If anything were to go wrong …* |
| ***Were** the merger **to** fall through, both stocks would drop.* | *If the merger were to fall through …* |
| ***Were** he **to** resign tomorrow, no one would be surprised.* | *If he were to resign …* |
| ***Were** that **to** happen again, I'd report it.* | *If that were to happen again …* |
| ***Were** the price **to** rise, demand would fall.* | *If the price were to rise …* |

Use *were to* when the plain *were* inversion would be awkward because the verb is an event rather than a state. *Were the bridge closed* describes a condition; ***were** the bridge **to** close* describes something happening. Both invert; they do not mean the same thing.

Note what *were to* buys you in register. In a memo, ***Were** we **to** delay the launch, we would miss the quarter* is measured and non-committal; *If we delay the launch, we'll miss the quarter* is a warning. That is why inverted *were to* is the standard hedging device of business analysis — it lets you model a possibility without appearing to expect it.

## *Had I known*: the third conditional inverted

### The form

Take a third conditional, delete *if*, and move *had* in front of the subject. This is the commonest of the three inversions in edited American prose, and the only one a learner will regularly need to **write**.

> *If I **had** known you were coming, I'd have made more food.*  →  ***Had** I known you were coming, I'd have made more food.*

> *If they **had** called ahead, we could have reserved a table.*  →  ***Had** they called ahead, we could have reserved a table.*

### A dense example set

| Inverted | Plain *if* version |
|---|---|
| ***Had** I known, I would have said something.* | *If I had known …* |
| ***Had** we left ten minutes earlier, we'd be there by now.* | *If we had left …* |
| ***Had** the company disclosed the defect, the recall would have been far smaller.* | *If the company had disclosed …* |
| ***Had** she **not** spoken up, the error would have gone into print.* | *If she hadn't spoken up …* |
| ***Had** the vote gone the other way, the department would have been reorganized.* | *If the vote had gone …* |
| ***Had** anyone checked the figures, the mistake would have surfaced in an hour.* | *If anyone had checked …* |
| ***Had** he taken the job in Seattle, he'd be running the division today.* | *If he had taken …* |
| ***Had** you told me the truth at the time, none of this would have happened.* | *If you had told me …* |
| ***Had** the tenant paid by the fifteenth, no late fee would have been assessed.* | *If the tenant had paid …* |
| ***Had** there been any warning, the plant would have been shut down.* | *If there had been any warning …* |
| ***Had** I any say in the matter, the policy would already be gone.* | *If I had any say …* |
| *I'd have driven you myself **had** I known your car was in the shop.* | *… if I had known.* |
| *The deal would have collapsed **had** the bank **not** stepped in.* | *… if the bank hadn't stepped in.* |
| *None of us would be here **had** she **not** insisted.* | *… if she hadn't insisted.* |

Three of those put the negative **after the subject** — *had she not spoken up*, *had the bank not stepped in*, *had she not insisted*. That placement is compulsory, and the rules section explains why.

*Had I any say* is a leftover of an older pattern in which lexical *have* itself inverts — *had I the money*, *had he the courage*. It survives in formal and literary writing and nowhere else; in ordinary prose, write *if I had any say*.

### *Had it not been for*

The past counterpart of *were it not for*, and it likewise takes a noun phrase.

> ***Had it not been for** the fire drill, no one would have left the building.*

> ***Had it not been for** her, I'd never have finished the degree.*

> ***Had it not been for** the second opinion, the diagnosis would have stood.*

> ***Had it not been for** the union contract, the layoffs would have been immediate.*

### Mixed conditionals invert too

A past condition with a present result inverts identically. The inverted clause is built from the *if*-clause alone, so what the main clause is doing makes no difference.

> *If I **had** taken that job, I'd be living in Denver now.*  →  ***Had** I taken that job, I'd be living in Denver now.*

> ***Had** she studied medicine, she'd be finishing her residency about now.*

> ***Had** the merger gone through, none of us would still be employed here.*

## *Should you need anything*: the remote first conditional

### The form

This one starts from a first conditional that already contains *should* — [a form](02-mixed-and-variations.md#if--should-the-remote-open-conditional) most learners never meet uninverted, because almost nobody uses it there.

> *If you **should** need anything, call the front desk.*  →  ***Should** you need anything, call the front desk.*

*Should* takes the **bare infinitive** and carries **none** of its usual meaning. ***Should** you need anything* does not mean "you ought to need anything"; it means "if you happen to need anything." The modal has been emptied out and left behind as a pure marker of a condition the speaker does not expect to come true. That emptiness is its whole contribution — **remoteness**:

| Version | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| *If the shipment **arrives** damaged, photograph it.* | a live possibility |
| *If the shipment **should arrive** damaged, photograph it.* | possible, not expected |
| ***Should** the shipment **arrive** damaged, photograph it.* | possible, not expected, and formally stated |

The main clause is not unreal in any way. It takes an imperative, a present tense, *will*, *may*, *can*, or *shall* — whatever an ordinary first conditional would take.

### A dense example set

| Inverted | Plain *if* version |
|---|---|
| ***Should** you need anything, the front desk is open all night.* | *If you need anything …* |
| ***Should** you have any questions, please don't hesitate to contact me.* | *If you have any questions …* |
| ***Should there be** any delay, we will notify you immediately.* | *If there is any delay …* |
| ***Should there be** any discrepancy between the versions, the English text governs.* | *If there is any discrepancy …* |
| ***Should** any of this change, I'll email everyone.* | *If any of this changes …* |
| ***Should** the tenant fail to pay, the landlord may terminate the lease.* | *If the tenant fails to pay …* |
| ***Should** the shipment arrive damaged, photograph it before signing.* | *If the shipment arrives damaged …* |
| ***Should** anything go wrong, the backup generator starts automatically.* | *If anything goes wrong …* |
| ***Should** your symptoms return, call this number.* | *If your symptoms return …* |
| ***Should** further testing be required, the sample will be retained.* | *If further testing is required …* |
| ***Should** you wish to opt out, follow the link at the bottom of this message.* | *If you wish to opt out …* |
| ***Should** you **have received** this message in error, please notify the sender.* | *If you have received this in error …* |
| *Call this number **should** your symptoms return.* | *… if your symptoms return.* |
| *We will refund the deposit in full **should** the event be canceled.* | *… if the event is canceled.* |

Two patterns in that list deserve names.

***Should there be*** is the existential version, and it is the standard opening of a contingency clause in business and technical writing: ***Should there be** any delay / any objection / any question / any doubt / any discrepancy / a change in circumstances …*

***Should you have received*** is *should* + a **perfect infinitive**, which puts the condition in the past while leaving the consequence in the present. Email boilerplate is built out of it: ***Should** you **have already submitted** your form, disregard this notice.* ***Should** the invoice **have been paid** in the meantime, please ignore this reminder.*

A caution about politeness. ***Should** you have any questions, please contact me* is formal and warm. ***Should** you fail to respond within ten days, the matter will be referred to collections* is formal and cold. The construction is neutral in itself and takes its temperature from the clause around it — but because it is the register of official letters, it can make a mild sentence land like a notice of default. In a friendly email, *if you need anything* is usually the better choice.

## Where each one belongs

| Setting | *were* | *had* | *should* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Casual conversation | No — *were I you* sounds like a costume drama. Say *if I were you*. | Rarely, and only in short shapes: *Had I known!* | No. Say *if you need anything*. |
| Email to a colleague | Only in the fixed *were it not for*. | Yes; it reads as polished, not stiff. | Yes — *Should you have any questions* is close to a fixed signature line. |
| Reports, proposals, formal writing | Yes, especially *were … to* and *were it not for*. | Yes. This is its home. | Yes, for contingencies. |
| Contracts, policies, notices | Yes, though *in the event that* is commoner. | Yes, in argument: *Had the defendant exercised reasonable care …* | Constantly: *Should the buyer fail to …* |
| Academic prose | Yes: *were this the case*, *were the effect genuine*. | Yes: *Had the sample been larger, the effect might have reached significance.* | Yes: *Should these results be replicated …* |
| Literary narration | Yes — *Were I a younger man* is a voice, and writers use it as one. | Heavily. Mid-century mystery fiction leaned on it so hard that critics named the habit the "Had-I-But-Known" school. | Less often; it reads as officialese rather than as a voice. |

## The rules that govern all three

### 1. No *if*. Ever.

The inversion **replaces** *if*; it does not accompany it. Writing both is the single most common error in this whole area, and it produces something that is not English in any register:

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| ✗ *If had I known …* | *If I had known …* / *Had I known …* |
| ✗ *If were I you …* | *If I were you …* / *Were I you …* |
| ✗ *If should you need anything …* | *If you should need anything …* / *Should you need anything …* |

Pick one device. Two markers of the same condition is one too many.

### 2. The movement is subject–auxiliary inversion, and nothing else moves

Exactly one word changes place: the auxiliary hops over the subject. Everything else in the clause stays where it was.

```
    If  [the company]  had  disclosed the defect …
    Had [the company]       disclosed the defect …
```

The subject can be as long as you like; the auxiliary lands in front of the whole of it:

> ***Had** the three engineers who signed off on the design **read** the test data, none of this would have happened.*

> ***Should** any employee covered by the policy **wish** to opt out, written notice is required.*

### 3. The negative cannot contract

This is the rule most likely to catch a learner who already knows the construction, and it has no exceptions. In an inverted conditional, the negative is a separate word **after the subject**:

> ✗ ***Hadn't** I known* → ✓ ***Had** I **not** known* &middot; ✗ ***Weren't** it for the rain* → ✓ ***Were** it **not** for the rain* &middot; ✗ ***Shouldn't** you receive a reply* → ✓ ***Should** you **not** receive a reply*

| Uninverted | Inverted |
|---|---|
| *If I hadn't known …* | *Had I not known …* |
| *If she hadn't spoken up …* | *Had she not spoken up …* |
| *If it hadn't been for the delay …* | *Had it not been for the delay …* |
| *If I weren't his sister …* | *Were I not his sister …* |
| *If the bank hadn't stepped in …* | *Had the bank not stepped in …* |
| *If you don't receive a reply …* | *Should you not receive a reply …* |

**Why the rule exists.** *Hadn't I known* is not ungrammatical — it is a **different sentence**. Auxiliary + *n't* + subject is the shape of a negative question, so ✗ *Hadn't I known about it, I would have called* trips the reader into reading a question and then strands them:

> *Hadn't I known about it?* is a question — "Didn't I know about it?" *Had I not known about it, I would have called* is a condition.

The uncontracted form keeps the two apart. The same logic covers *Weren't it for the rain* (heard as *Weren't it for the rain?*) and *Shouldn't you need anything* (heard as a question about obligation).

**Where the *not* goes.** After the subject, always. Not before the auxiliary, and not anywhere else:

> ✗ *Not had I known …*  ✗ *Had not I known …*  ✗ *Had I known not …*  ✓ *Had I not known …*

*Had not I known* is the one on that list that turns up in nineteenth-century prose. It is archaic, and in modern writing it reads as an error rather than as a flourish.

### 4. Only *were*, *had*, and *should* invert

There is no fourth auxiliary. The others produce nothing usable:

| Attempt | Verdict |
|---|---|
| ✗ *Did I know the answer, I'd tell you.* | Archaic and literary only — no *do*-support in a modern conditional inversion. Write *If I knew*. |
| ✗ *Would you like a copy, let me know.* | Not English. Write *If you would like a copy* or *Should you like a copy*. |
| ✗ *Could I help, I would.* | Archaic. Modern English wants *If I could help*. |
| ✗ *Will you need a ride, call me.* | Not English. Write *If you need a ride* or *Should you need a ride*. |
| ✗ *Have you any questions, call me.* | British and dated as a conditional. Write *Should you have any questions*. |

**No *do*-support.** This is the crucial structural point, and it separates conditional inversion from every other inversion in English. A question inserts *do* when there is no auxiliary to move — *Do you know?*, *Did she call?* A conditional inversion cannot. If your *if*-clause has no *were*, *had*, or *should* in it, **it cannot be inverted at all**:

| *if*-clause | Invertible? |
|---|---|
| *if I knew* | No. There is no auxiliary. (✗ *Did I know*) |
| *if I were* | Yes → *were I* |
| *if he should go* | Yes → *should he go* |
| *if he had gone* | Yes → *had he gone* |
| *if it rains* | No. (✗ *Does it rain*) |
| *if it were to rain* | Yes → *were it to rain* |
| *if they had been told* | Yes → *had they been told* |
| *if they were told* | No, in practice — *were they told* reads as a question or as a past indicative, not as a condition. |

The last row is a real restriction. The inverted *were* has to be the **subjunctive** *were*, not the ordinary past-tense *were* of a real past event. *Were they told, the meeting would have gone differently* is not how anyone writes it; the past-time version has to go through *had*: *Had they been told …*

**The workaround.** When an *if*-clause has no invertible auxiliary and you want the formal register anyway, insert *should* (for open conditions) or *were to* (for hypothetical ones) and then invert:

> *if it rains* → *if it should rain* → ***Should** it rain, the game moves to Sunday.*

> *if the price rose* → *if the price were to rise* → ***Were** the price **to** rise, demand would fall.*

### 5. Punctuation

A fronted inverted clause takes a comma. A trailing one does not.

> ***Had** I known, I would have called.*  &middot;  *I would have called **had** I known.*

The comma is not optional in the fronted position, and a trailing clause takes no comma even when it is long: *The whole project would have stalled **had** the second round of funding fallen through in the spring.*

### 6. The inverted clause can be embedded

It survives inside a *that*-clause, which is where it most often shows up in reported speech and in argument:

> *He said that **had** he known, he would have called.*

> *The court held that **had** the warning been adequate, the plaintiff would not have been injured.*

> *We agreed that **should** the vendor miss a second deadline, we would cancel.*

*If* cannot appear here either: ✗ *He said that if had he known.* And the inverted clause cannot be the complement of a preposition — there is no ✗ *in case had he known*.

## Negative inversion with conditional force

A second family of inversions has nothing to do with the three auxiliaries above. When a **negative or restrictive expression** is moved to the front of a sentence, the clause that follows inverts — and several members of that family state conditions.

The machinery is different, and the difference is worth learning as a contrast:

| | Conditional inversion | Negative inversion |
|---|---|---|
| What is fronted | nothing — the auxiliary simply moves | a negative or restrictive adverbial |
| Which clause inverts | the **condition** clause | the **main** clause |
| *do*-support | never | yes, when there is no other auxiliary |
| *if* | impossible | often present, inside the fronted phrase |
| Example | ***Had** I known, I would have called.* | *Only if you sign **will we** release the funds.* |

Read those two examples side by side until the difference is obvious. In *Had I known*, the inverted clause **is** the condition. In *Only if you sign will we release the funds*, the condition is the ordinary uninverted clause *you sign*, and the inversion happens in the **result** clause that follows it. Learners routinely invert the wrong one: ✗ *Only if will you sign, we release the funds.*

### The fronted expressions that state a condition

| Fronted element | Example |
|---|---|
| *only if* | ***Only if** the board approves the budget **will the project** move forward.* |
| *only if* | ***Only if** you sign the waiver **can we** admit you to the trial.* |
| *only when* | ***Only when** the check clears **do we** ship.* |
| *only when* | ***Only when** the alarm sounded **did they** leave the building.* |
| *only after* | ***Only after** the inspection is complete **can the certificate** be issued.* |
| *only then* | ***Only then did I** realize what she had meant.* |
| *only by* | ***Only by** filing an appeal within thirty days **can the decision** be challenged.* |
| *only in the event of* | ***Only in** the event of a total loss **does the higher rate** apply.* |
| *not until* | ***Not until** the contract is signed **will we** begin work.* |
| *not until* | ***Not until** all three signatures are in place **does the policy** take effect.* |
| *not until* | ***Not until** she read the second page **did she** understand what she had agreed to.* |
| *not unless* | ***Not unless** you ask **will they** tell you.* |
| *under no circumstances* | ***Under no circumstances should you** open the case while it is plugged in.* |
| *under no circumstances* | ***Under no circumstances will the deposit** be refunded after the cutoff date.* |
| *under no circumstances* | ***Under no circumstances is the vehicle** to be operated without a licensed driver.* |
| *on no account* | ***On no account should the two chemicals** be stored together.* |
| *in no event* | ***In no event shall the seller** be liable for indirect or consequential damages.* |
| *in no event* | ***In no event shall the total liability** exceed the amount paid under this agreement.* |
| *in no way* | ***In no way does this** constitute an admission of fault.* |

Note the *do*-support in *only when the check clears **do we** ship* and *only then **did I** realize*. There is no auxiliary in *we ship* or *I realized*, so English supplies one, exactly as it would in a question. *In no event shall …* is the boilerplate limitation-of-liability formula of American contracts; you will meet it in every software license you ever decline to read.

**Do not confuse *only if* with *if only*.** They are [different constructions](03-alternatives-to-if.md#only-if-and-if-only) with different syntax and different meanings:

> ***Only if** you apologize **will I** come.* — a condition, and the main clause inverts.

> ***If only** you had apologized!* — a wish or regret, and nothing inverts.

### The emphatic relatives

Not conditional in themselves, but built on the same machinery, and the conditional sense is often implicit — *never again would he* means "under no future condition would he."

| Fronted element | Example |
|---|---|
| *never* | ***Never had I** seen anything like it.* |
| *never again* | ***Never again would he** set foot in that house.* |
| *rarely* | ***Rarely does a case** of this kind reach trial.* |
| *seldom* | ***Seldom has a decision** been so thoroughly reversed.* |
| *little* | ***Little did she know** what the meeting was about.* |
| *no sooner … than* | ***No sooner had the meeting started than** the fire alarm went off.* |
| *hardly … when* | ***Hardly had we** arrived **when** the power went out.* |
| *not only … but* | ***Not only did he** miss the deadline, **but he** never explained why.* |
| *not only* | ***Not only is the policy** unenforceable, **it is** probably illegal.* |
| *so + adverb* | ***So rarely does he** call **that** I assumed something was wrong.* |
| *so + adjective* | ***So great was the demand that** the second printing sold out in a week.* |
| *such* | ***Such was the confusion that** the vote had to be retaken.* |

### What inverts, exactly

The **operator** — the first auxiliary or modal in the verb phrase — moves in front of the subject. If there is no auxiliary, *do* / *does* / *did* is supplied to carry the inversion.

| Plain clause | Operator | Inverted after a fronted negative |
|---|---|---|
| *we will release the funds* | *will* | *will we release the funds* |
| *we can admit you* | *can* | *can we admit you* |
| *the policy takes effect* | none → *does* | *does the policy take effect* |
| *they left* | none → *did* | *did they leave* |
| *I have seen it* | *have* | *have I seen it* |
| *the deposit is refunded* | *is* | *is the deposit refunded* |
| *the seller shall be liable* | *shall* | *shall the seller be liable* |

**One thing that does not invert.** If the negative word is the **subject** itself, nothing has moved, so nothing inverts: ✓ *Nobody knew* — not ✗ *Nobody did know*; ✓ *No warning was given*. And the fronted phrase itself stays uninverted: in *Only if you sign will we release the funds*, the clause *you sign* keeps ordinary word order every time.

## Formal and legal conditionals

Legal and contractual English states conditions constantly and almost never with a bare *if*. It has its own inventory, and the reason is not pomposity: a drafter needs each condition to attach to exactly one obligation, to survive being read aloud in court, and to resist the reader who would like it to mean something else. *If* is short and flexible, and flexible is the last thing a contract wants.

### The inventory

| Expression | Takes | Plain-English equivalent | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| [*in the event that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#in-the-event-that) | a clause | *if* | *In the event that the tenant vacates early, the deposit is forfeited.* |
| *in the event of* | a noun phrase | *if there is* | *In the event of a dispute, the parties shall mediate before filing suit.* |
| [*provided that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#provided-that) | a clause | *as long as*, *but only if* | *The license is transferable, provided that written notice is given.* |
| [*provided always that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#provided-always-that) | a clause | *but only if, in every case* | *Payment may be deferred, provided always that interest continues to accrue.* |
| [*provided, however, that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#provided-however-that) | a clause | *except that* | *The term renews automatically; provided, however, that either party may opt out.* |
| [*on condition that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#on-condition-that) | a clause | *only if* | *Bail was granted on condition that he surrender his passport.* |
| [*subject to*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#subject-to) | a noun phrase | *if X allows*, *depending on* | *Delivery is subject to availability.* |
| [*contingent on*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#contingent-on) | a noun phrase | *dependent on* | *The offer is contingent on financing.* |
| [*failing that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#failing-that) | stands alone | *if that does not work* | *Try the branch office; failing that, write to headquarters.* |
| *failing which* | clause-final | *and if that does not happen* | *Notice must be given within ten days, failing which the claim lapses.* |
| *failing* + noun | a noun phrase | *in the absence of* | *Failing payment, the account will be closed.* |
| [*save that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#save-that) | a clause | *except that* | *The terms are unchanged, save that the fee has been reduced.* |
| [*save*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#save) + noun | a noun phrase | *except* | *No warranty is given save the limited warranty in Section 8.* |
| [*except as otherwise provided*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#except-as-otherwise-provided) | stands alone | *unless stated elsewhere* | *Except as otherwise provided in Section 4, all fees are nonrefundable.* |
| [*unless and until*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#unless-and-until) | a clause | *until* | *No interest accrues unless and until the note matures.* |
| [*if and when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-and-when) | a clause | *when, if it happens* | *The bonus is payable if and when the milestone is met.* |
| [*absent*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#absent) | a noun phrase | *without, if there is no* | *Absent a written agreement, the default rule applies.* |
| [*but for*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#but-for) | a noun phrase | *if it were not for* | *But for the delay, the closing would have occurred in March.* |
| [*notwithstanding*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#notwithstanding) | a noun phrase | *despite* | *Notwithstanding Section 3, the buyer may cancel.* |
| *where* | a clause | *if, in any case in which* | *Where a party fails to appear, the court may proceed.* |

Two of those need a warning label. ***Where*** in statutory drafting is a **conditional**, not a place word: *Where an employee is dismissed without notice* means *if an employee is dismissed*, not *in the location where*. And ***provided that*** in a contract is a restriction, not a generosity — it names the one circumstance under which the preceding promise holds, and drafters fight about it precisely because it can be read as attaching to the whole preceding sentence or only to its last clause.

### *Should the buyer fail to* …

The *should*-inversion from earlier is the workhorse of drafted conditions, because it lets the drafter name a party and a failure in one clause, with no *if* and no *when*.

> ***Should the buyer fail to** make payment within thirty (30) days, the seller may terminate this Agreement.*

> ***Should either party fail to** perform, the non-defaulting party shall give written notice of the default.*

> ***Should the tenant remain** in possession after expiration, the tenancy becomes month-to-month.*

> ***Should any provision** of this Agreement **be held** invalid, the remaining provisions shall remain in full force.*

That last one is the severability clause, and some version of it appears in virtually every contract written in American English.

### *Shall* in contracts

In ordinary American English *shall* is nearly dead. In contracts it is everywhere, and it does not mean the future — it imposes an **obligation** on the party named as the subject.

| Form | Reads as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *X shall …* | X is obligated to | *The contractor **shall** maintain liability insurance.* |
| *X shall not …* | X is prohibited from | *The employee **shall not** disclose confidential information.* |
| *In no event shall X …* | X is never obligated / liable | *In no event **shall** the licensor **be** liable for lost profits.* |
| *X may …* | X is permitted to | *The seller **may** terminate this Agreement.* |
| *X must …* | X is obligated to (plain-language drafting) | *The contractor **must** maintain liability insurance.* |

Modern plain-language drafting is steadily replacing contractual *shall* with *must*, partly because *shall* had been used so loosely — sometimes as an obligation, sometimes as a future, sometimes as a definition — that courts stopped treating it as reliable. The United States federal rules were rewritten to drop it. You will still meet it in almost everything written before that and in plenty written since.

**Do not use *shall* for the future in your own writing.** *I shall call you tomorrow* is not American English; it is either British, dated, or a costume. The living American uses of *shall* are the contractual obligation above and the first-person offer — *Shall I get the door?*, *Shall we?* The full account is in [the modal-verb catalog](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#shall).

### A worked example

Contract prose in miniature — a termination clause built out of six conditions:

> **8.2 Termination for Cause.** *Should the Licensee fail to pay any fee when due, and such failure continue for fifteen (15) days after written notice, the Licensor may terminate this Agreement immediately; provided, however, that the Licensor shall first offer the Licensee a reasonable opportunity to cure. In the event of termination under this Section, all licenses granted hereunder terminate automatically, save that Section 9 (Confidentiality) survives. Should any amount remain unpaid at termination, interest shall accrue at 1.5% per month, failing which the Licensor may refer the account to collection. In no event shall the Licensor be liable for any loss arising from such termination.*

The same content in the English you would actually use:

> *If you don't pay a fee on time and you still haven't paid fifteen days after we tell you, we can cancel the contract — but we have to give you a fair chance to fix it first. If we cancel, your licenses end immediately, except that the confidentiality rules stay in force. If you still owe money when we cancel, it collects interest at 1.5% a month, and if you don't pay that, we can send the account to a collection agency. We aren't liable for anything you lose because of the cancellation.*

Count the devices in the first version: *should* + subject + bare infinitive (twice), *provided, however, that*, *in the event of*, *save that*, *failing which*, *in no event shall*, plus *hereunder* and *such* used as a pointing word. The second version uses *if* four times and nothing else.

### Read this register; do not write it

**This section is for reading.** A learner has real reasons to read contracts, leases, terms of service, policies, and notices, and every one of them is built from the list above. A learner has almost no reason to write in this register, and three good reasons not to. It is easy to get wrong in ways that change the meaning — *provided that* attaches to something, and attaching it to the wrong thing is how contracts get litigated. It is lawyer's work: if a sentence you write carries legal consequences, the job is not to imitate the style but to find someone who drafts for a living. And in any other kind of writing it reads badly — *Should you fail to respond, the matter will be escalated* in an email to a colleague is not formal, it is hostile.

Write *if*. Read *provided always that*.

## Academic and scientific hedging

Academic prose states conditions in order to **limit a claim** — to say "this holds, within these bounds" without either overclaiming or retreating. Most of the devices are conditionals with the *if* removed or softened, and several are inversions.

| Expression | What it hedges | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *were this the case* | a premise the writer is not asserting | *Were this the case, the anomaly in Figure 3 would disappear.* |
| *were the effect genuine* | the finding itself | *Were the effect genuine, one would expect a dose-response relationship.* |
| *should these results be replicated* | a future contingency | *Should these results be replicated, the standard model will require revision.* |
| *had the sample been larger* | a past limitation | *Had the sample been larger, the difference might have reached significance.* |
| [*assuming that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#assuming-that) | a stated premise | *Assuming that the model holds, the projection is accurate to within 3%.* |
| *assuming* + noun | the same, compressed | *Assuming no further shocks, growth returns to trend by 2027.* |
| *on the assumption that* | the same, more explicit | *The estimate rests on the assumption that reporting rates are stable.* |
| [*given that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#given-that) | a premise treated as settled | *Given that the data are self-reported, some inflation is expected.* |
| *given* + noun | the same, compressed | *Given the small sample, the result should be treated as preliminary.* |
| [*all else being equal*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#all-else-being-equal) | every uncontrolled variable | *All else being equal, a higher rate reduces borrowing.* |
| *other things being equal* | the same | *Other things being equal, the cheaper method is preferred.* |
| *ceteris paribus* | the same, in economics | *Ceteris paribus, demand falls as price rises.* |
| *provided that* | a condition on the claim | *The method is reliable, provided that the calibration is current.* |
| *insofar as* | the range of the claim | *The finding holds insofar as the population resembles the sample.* |
| [*to the extent that*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#to-the-extent-that) | the same | *To the extent that the proxy is valid, the correlation is meaningful.* |
| *unless otherwise stated* | the whole document | *Unless otherwise stated, all figures are in constant dollars.* |
| *barring* | an unforeseen event | *Barring a major revision to the dataset, the conclusion stands.* |
| *absent* + noun | a missing condition | *Absent further evidence, no causal claim is warranted.* |
| *if anything* | the direction of an error | *The estimate is conservative; if anything, the true effect is larger.* |
| *if at all* | whether it happens | *The effect is small, if it exists at all.* |
| *suppose that* / *let us suppose* | a thought experiment | *Suppose that the two variables are independent.* |

Three habits of the register are worth naming, because they show up together.

**The result clause goes impersonal.** Not *we would expect* but *one would expect*, *it would follow that*, *the model predicts*: *Were the mechanism as described, **one would expect** the effect to attenuate with distance.*

**The condition is stated as a premise, not a possibility.** *Given that* and *assuming that* both hand the reader a premise and get on with the argument. *Given that* treats it as settled; *assuming that* flags that it is not.

**Hedges stack**, two or three to a sentence, and that is normal rather than weaselly: *Assuming the calibration is current and all else being equal, the method should be accurate to within 3%, though the sample is small enough that the figure may not generalize.*

## Literary and fixed expressions

A conditional clause that gets used often enough stops being built and starts being remembered. The expressions below are **frozen**: their grammar is old, several of them break modern rules outright, and none of them can be varied. Learn them as units.

| Expression | Means | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *come what may* | whatever happens | literary, elevated | *Come what may, the ceremony goes ahead on Saturday.* |
| [*be that as it may*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#be-that-as-it-may) | even if that is true | formal | *The report is thorough. Be that as it may, it arrived two weeks late.* |
| [*if need be*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-need-be) | if it is necessary | neutral to formal | *We can move the meeting to Friday if need be.* |
| *if you will* | if you'll allow the expression | formal, and overused | *The department is the company's immune system, if you will.* |
| *perish the thought* | I hope that never happens | ironic, literary | *If the roof goes too — perish the thought — the repair bill doubles.* |
| *so be it* | then let it happen | neutral, final | *If they want to litigate, so be it.* |
| *God forbid* / *heaven forbid* | I hope not | neutral, spoken | *God forbid anything happen to the server on a Friday.* |
| *far be it from me* | I would not presume | ironic, formal | *Far be it from me to tell you how to run your department.* |
| *be it X or Y* | whether it is X or Y | formal | *Any notice, be it written or oral, must be logged.* |
| *be it noted* / *be it known* | let it be recorded | legal, archaic | *Be it known that the parties appeared voluntarily.* |
| *if all else fails* | as a last resort | neutral | *If all else fails, reinstall the driver.* |
| *if worst comes to worst* | in the worst case | neutral, spoken | *If worst comes to worst, we drive.* |
| *if push comes to shove* | if the pressure becomes real | informal | *If push comes to shove, I'll pay for it myself.* |
| *come hell or high water* | no matter what | informal, emphatic | *We're shipping Monday, come hell or high water.* |
| *if truth be told* | honestly | formal-ish, literary | *If truth be told, nobody read the appendix.* |
| *if ever there was one* | a clear case of it | neutral, emphatic | *That was a warning sign if ever there was one.* |
| *if I may* / *if I might* | with your permission | formal, polite | *If I may, I'd like to return to the second point.* |
| *should the need arise* | if it becomes necessary | formal | *A second shift can be added should the need arise.* |
| *had I but known* | if only I had known | literary, archaic | *Had I but known what the letter contained, I would have burned it.* |

Four of those hide a subjunctive that no longer exists anywhere else in the language. *Come what may*, *be that as it may*, *if need be*, *so be it*, *God forbid*, *far be it from me*, *be it X or Y*, and *if truth be told* all use the bare **subjunctive** — *come*, *be*, *forbid* — with no agreement and no tense. That is why ✗ *comes what may*, ✗ *be that as it may be*, ✗ *if need is*, and ✗ *God forbids* are all wrong: there is nothing to conjugate.

Two carry warnings.

***If you will*** means "if you'll permit me to put it that way," and it excuses a metaphor you have just used. It does **not** mean "if you want to," and American speakers who scatter it through a presentation as a verbal tic are widely mocked for it. Use it once or not at all. (The unrelated literal *if you will* — *if you will sign here* — means "if you are willing to," and is a different construction entirely.)

***Perish the thought*** is an interjection, not a clause you can attach things to. It sits inside a sentence between dashes or commas, or stands alone: *A third round of revisions? Perish the thought.*

## Ellipsis in formal conditionals

Formal writing shortens conditional clauses by deleting the **subject and the form of *be*** — the general mechanism is in [Ellipsis and reduced clauses](02-mixed-and-variations.md#ellipsis-and-reduced-clauses) and leaving whatever was left over. The result is two or three words that behave like an adverbial:

```
    if (it is) necessary  →  if necessary        if (there are) any  →  if any
    if (it is) possible   →  if possible         where (it is) applicable  →  where applicable
```

Nothing is implied that was not there; the reader reconstructs the missing words automatically. These fragments are punctuated as parentheticals — commas on both sides when they interrupt, one comma when they front or trail.

| Elliptical form | Full form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *if any* | *if there are any* | *Corrections, if any, should be sent to the editor by Friday.* |
| *few, if any* | *few, if there are any at all* | *Few, if any, of the original staff remain.* |
| *if necessary* | *if it is necessary* | *The deadline can be extended if necessary.* |
| *if applicable* | *if it is applicable* | *Enter your tax ID, if applicable.* |
| *where applicable* | *where it is applicable* | *Fees are shown in local currency where applicable.* |
| *as applicable* | *as is applicable* | *Complete Part A or Part B, as applicable.* |
| *where appropriate* | *where it is appropriate* | *Citations have been updated where appropriate.* |
| *if possible* | *if it is possible* | *Send the file today if possible.* |
| [*if and when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-and-when) | *if it happens and at the time it happens* | *We'll revisit the budget if and when the grant is renewed.* |
| *if so* | *if that is so* | *Did you already file? If so, ignore this notice.* |
| *if in doubt* | *if you are in doubt* | *If in doubt, leave the field blank.* |
| *if at all* | *if it happens at all* | *He rarely calls, if at all.* |
| *if anything* | *if there is anything to be said* | *If anything, the estimate is too low.* |
| *if ever* | *if it ever happens* | *She seldom, if ever, misses a deadline.* |
| *unless otherwise stated* | *unless it is stated otherwise* | *Unless otherwise stated, all times are Eastern.* |
| *unless otherwise agreed* | *unless it is agreed otherwise* | *Unless otherwise agreed, payment is due in thirty days.* |
| *subject to availability* | *if it is available* | *Upgrades are offered subject to availability.* |
| *if need be* | *if there is need* | *I'll drive out there myself if need be.* |
| *wherever possible* | *wherever it is possible* | *Wherever possible, cite the primary source.* |

One thing to watch: ***if any*** has two jobs. After a plural noun it is the elliptical conditional above — *errors, if any* means "the errors, if there are any." In *few, if any* and *little, if any* it is a **scalar tag** that pulls the quantity down to the edge of zero: *Few, if any, of the attendees noticed* says almost nobody did.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *If had I known …* | *Had I known …* / *If I had known …* | The inversion replaces *if*. Never both. |
| ✗ *Hadn't I known …* | *Had I not known …* | The negative cannot contract in an inverted conditional — the contracted form is a question. |
| ✗ *Weren't it for the rain …* | *Were it not for the rain …* | Same rule, same reason. |
| ✗ *Had not I known …* | *Had I not known …* | *Not* goes after the subject, not before it. |
| ✗ *Was I you, I'd wait.* | *Were I you, I'd wait.* | Only the subjunctive *were* inverts. There is no *was*-inversion. |
| ✗ *Did I know, I would tell you.* | *If I knew, I would tell you.* | Conditional inversion has no *do*-support. Only *were*, *had*, and *should* invert. |
| ✗ *Would you need help, call me.* | *Should you need help, call me.* | *Would* does not invert as a conditional. |
| ✗ *Had I have known …* | *Had I known …* | The spoken ✗ *if I'd have known* is an error in writing, and the inversion exposes it. |
| ✗ *Had I known, I would call.* | *Had I known, I would have called.* | An inverted *had*-clause is a third conditional; the main clause needs *would have*. |
| ✗ *Were I you, I will wait.* | *Were I you, I would wait.* | A *were*-clause is a second conditional; the main clause needs *would*. |
| ✗ *Should the buyer will fail to pay …* | *Should the buyer fail to pay …* | *Should* takes the bare infinitive. |
| ✗ *Were it not because of the rain …* | *Were it not for the rain …* | The fixed phrase takes *for*, and takes a noun phrase after it. |
| ✗ *Only if you sign, we will release the funds.* | *Only if you sign will we release the funds.* | A fronted *only if* inverts the **main** clause. |
| ✗ *Only if will you sign, we release the funds.* | *Only if you sign will we release the funds.* | The *only if* clause itself keeps ordinary word order. |
| ✗ *Under no circumstances you should open it.* | *Under no circumstances should you open it.* | A fronted negative forces inversion. |
| ✗ *In the event that he will fail to pay …* | *In the event that he fails to pay …* | Conditional clauses take the present, not *will*. |
| ✗ *Provided that always interest accrues …* | *Provided always that interest accrues …* | *Always* sits inside the fixed phrase, before *that*. |
| ✗ *Comes what may …* | *Come what may …* | Frozen subjunctive: no *-s*, no tense. |
| ✗ *Be that as it may be …* | *Be that as it may …* | The *be* is already the verb; nothing follows it. |
| ✗ *I shall send the file tomorrow.* | *I will send the file tomorrow.* | Contractual *shall* imposes duties; it is not the American future. |

## Quick reference

**To turn an *if*-clause into an inverted one, in four steps.**

1. **Find the auxiliary.** Is it *were*, *had*, or *should*? If it is none of those, stop — this clause cannot be inverted. Insert *should* (open condition) or *were … to* (hypothetical) first, then continue.
2. **Delete *if*.**
3. **Move the auxiliary in front of the subject.** Nothing else moves.
4. **Write any negative out in full, after the subject** — *not*, never *n't*. Then punctuate: comma after a fronted inverted clause, none before a trailing one.

**The three, compressed.**

| | Time frame | Inverted | Plain |
|---|---|---|---|
| *were* | unreal present / future | *Were I you, I'd wait.* | *If I were you …* |
| *were … to* | hypothetical future | *Were she to call, tell her I'm out.* | *If she were to call …* |
| *had* | unreal past | *Had I known, I'd have called.* | *If I had known …* |
| *should* | unlikely open future | *Should you need anything, call me.* | *If you should need …* |

**The two inversions, kept apart.** ***Had** I known, I would have called* is conditional inversion — the **condition** clause inverts, and there is no *do*. *Only if you sign **will we** release the funds* is negative inversion — the **main** clause inverts, and *do* is supplied when nothing else can move.

**The register rule of thumb.** In edited American prose, *had*-inversion is common, *should*-inversion is common in correspondence and boilerplate, and bare *were*-inversion is rare outside *were it not for* and *were … to*; in speech all three are uncommon, and *Were I you* will make you sound like you are quoting something. So: write *had* and *should* inversions freely in careful writing, keep bare *were* for the two fixed patterns, and read the *provided always that* register without writing it.

## Practice

Rewrite each sentence using an inversion or the formal expression named in brackets. Some cannot be inverted at all — say so.

1. If I had seen your message, I would have replied. [inversion]
2. If it weren't for the deposit, I'd have walked away. [inversion]
3. If you need any help, call the front desk. [inversion]
4. If she hadn't warned us, we'd have signed it. [inversion]
5. If I were in your position, I'd get it in writing. [inversion]
6. If there is any delay, we will notify you. [inversion]
7. If I knew the answer, I'd tell you. [inversion]
8. If the buyer fails to pay within thirty days, the seller may terminate. [inversion]
9. If you have received this email in error, please delete it. [inversion]
10. We will only begin work when the contract is signed. [*not until*]
11. You must never open the case while it is plugged in. [*under no circumstances*]
12. We'll release the funds only if you sign. [*only if*]
13. If the tenant leaves early, the deposit is forfeited. [*in the event that*]
14. Corrections should be sent to the editor, if there are any. [ellipsis]
15. Whatever happens, the ceremony goes ahead. [fixed expression]

### Answers

| # | Answer |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Had** I seen your message, I would have replied. |
| 2 | **Were it not for** the deposit, I'd have walked away. (Also *but for the deposit* — more formal.) |
| 3 | **Should** you need any help, call the front desk. |
| 4 | **Had** she **not** warned us, we'd have signed it. (Not ✗ *Hadn't she warned us*.) |
| 5 | **Were** I in your position, I'd get it in writing. |
| 6 | **Should there be** any delay, we will notify you. |
| 7 | **Cannot be inverted.** *If I knew* has no auxiliary, and there is no *do*-inversion. Recast as *Were I to know* only if the meaning allows it; otherwise leave the *if*. |
| 8 | **Should** the buyer **fail** to pay within thirty days, the seller may terminate. (Bare infinitive.) |
| 9 | **Should** you **have received** this email in error, please delete it. |
| 10 | **Not until** the contract is signed **will we** begin work. |
| 11 | **Under no circumstances should you** open the case while it is plugged in. |
| 12 | **Only if** you sign **will we** release the funds. |
| 13 | **In the event that** the tenant vacates early, the deposit is forfeited. |
| 14 | Corrections, **if any**, should be sent to the editor. |
