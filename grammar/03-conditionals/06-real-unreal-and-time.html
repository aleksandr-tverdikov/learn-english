Real and unreal: what the tenses actually mean#

Why the past tense in if I had the money has nothing to do with the past, how any condition-time can pair with any result-time, and why one Russian бы has to be sorted into two different English patterns.

← Conditionals · All grammar topics

Most references teach conditionals as four numbered patterns to be memorized. That holds up until you meet a sentence that fits none of them — If I'd studied medicine, I'd be a doctor now; If he were going to quit, he'd have said something by now; Should you need anything, call the front desk — and then the numbers are no help, because there was never really a list of four. There is one distinction and one variable, and every pattern in the topic falls out of them.

The distinction is real against unreal. A real condition leaves the question open: the speaker takes no position on whether it holds. An unreal condition announces that the speaker is treating it as contrary to fact, or at least as improbable enough not to be worth betting on.

The variable is time, and it is set separately in each clause. The condition points at some moment; the result points at some moment; the two do not have to be the same moment.

Put those together and the grammar becomes mechanical. Unreality is marked by shifting the verb one step further back — and by nothing else. Time is marked by ordinary tense, plus have + past participle wherever a clause needs to reach into the past. The four numbered patterns are simply the four combinations that come up most often. The combinations that have no number are built exactly the same way, which is why a learner who has the system can produce them without ever having been taught them.

Real and unreal: the one distinction underneath the numbers#

What a real condition claims#

Nothing. That is the point of it. A real — or open — condition says if p, then q and leaves p entirely undecided.

If it rains, we'll cancel the picnic.

The speaker is not predicting rain and is not ruling it out. They have described what follows from rain, and left the weather alone. The same neutrality holds when the condition is about the present or the past:

If she's in her office, the light is on. If he took the early train, he's already there. If you paid twice, we'll refund one of them. If the invoice is over ten pounds, it ships separately.

All four are open. In the second and third, the event is in the past and settled as a matter of fact — he either took the train or he didn't — but the speaker's knowledge of it is open, and that is what the form tracks.

What an unreal condition claims#

An unreal condition adds a comment on top of the if p, then q: the speaker signals that p is false, or unlikely, or merely being entertained for the sake of argument.

If it rained, we'd cancel the picnic.

Now the speaker has leaned in a direction. The sentence suggests rain is not expected. Nothing else has changed — the picnic logic is identical — and the only difference in the grammar is that rains became rained and will became would.

The marker is a backward shift of tense, and nothing else#

Set the pairs side by side and the mechanism is impossible to miss. Read each row left to right: the meaning of the condition does not change, only the speaker's stance toward it.

Real / openUnreal / distancedWhat the shift did

If she calls, I'll tell her.If she called, I'd tell her.calls → called
If they have the receipt, they can return it.If they had the receipt, they could return it.have → had, can → could

The last two rows are the ones textbooks skip, and they are the proof of the rule. A real condition about the past exists and is extremely common — If he was there, he saw it is an ordinary English sentence about an open question — and when you distance it, it takes the same single step back that the present-tense rows take. There is no separate rule for the past. There is one operation applied to whatever was there.

The shift is one step, and the ladder is short#

The step back is the same one that governs backshift in reported speech, and it walks down the same ladder:

        real form                  unreal form
        --------------------------------------------
   1    will / can / may     →     would / could / might
   2    present              →     past
   3    past                 →     past perfect
   3'   present perfect      →     past perfect
   4    past perfect         →     past perfect   (floor)

Two consequences follow, and both explain things learners find arbitrary.

The result clause always ends up with would, could, or might. Not because conditionals "use would," but because will, can, and may are what an open result clause contains, and one step back from those is would, could, and might. If the open version had can, the unreal version has could; if the open version had may, the unreal version has might.

If we leave now, we can still make the 7:40. → If we left now, we could still make the 7:40. If the check clears, I may buy the car. → If the check cleared, I might buy the car. If you ask him, he might help. → If you asked him, he might help. (already at the floor — nothing moves)

There is no third step. Rung 4 is the bottom. Once a clause is at had gone / would have gone, English has no further past to push it into, which is why an unreal past is the end of the line and why it can express only one degree of unreality. The consequences of that floor turn up twice more in this section — in conditionals inside reported speech and in the common mistakes that try to squeeze out a fourth rung (✗ if I hadn't have known).

What the shift does not mean#

It is worth saying flatly, because almost every learner assumes the opposite at first: the shifted form carries no past time at all.

If I had the money, I'd buy it. — about right now If I knew, I'd tell you. — about right now If it rained tomorrow, we'd cancel. — about tomorrow If she left next week, nobody would notice. — about next week

Had, knew, rained, and left are past-tense forms doing no past-tense work whatever. Three of the four sentences are about the future. That is the fact the rest of this section is built on.

The past tense as distance, not time#

One form, three jobs#

The English past tense marks distance. What it is distant from depends on the frame it sits in, and there are three frames.

JobWhat it distances the event fromExampleIs it about past time?
Past timethe present momentI worked there for six years.Yes
Unrealitythe actual worldIf I worked there, I'd know the answer.No
Politeness, tentativenessthe hearer, and the force of the actI wondered whether you could help.No

The three are not three coincidental uses of a homonym. They are one meaning — not here, not now, not quite — pointed at three different things. Time is the literal case; unreality and politeness are the figurative ones. English is not unusual in this; Russian does something comparable with бы, and the Russian section below pulls the parallel apart.

Once you see the past tense as a distance marker, a whole set of unrelated-looking structures turn out to be the same structure.

The distance frames#

These frames all take a past form with no past meaning. Each one is worked through in Wishes and regrets; what matters here is that they are all running the same machine as the if-clause.

After wish and if only — the past form marks the wish as counter to fact:

I wish I knew the answer. (I don't know it — now) I wish I had a car. (I don't have one — now) I wish she were here. (she isn't — now) If only he listened to me. (he doesn't — generally) I wish I hadn't said that. (a step further back: regret about the past)

After would rather and would sooner — when the subject of the second clause is a different person:

I'd rather you didn't smoke in here. (about right now) She'd rather we paid in cash. I'd sooner he heard it from me. I'd rather you hadn't told her. (regret about the past)

After it's time, it's high time, it's about time:

It's time we left. (we haven't left; it is now) It's high time you got a real job. It's about time somebody said something.

After as if and as though:

He talks as if he owned the place. (he doesn't) She looked as though she'd seen a ghost. (she hadn't) It sounds as if they knew all along.

After suppose, supposing, imagine, what if — these introduce a hypothesis, which is an unreal condition with the if rearranged:

Suppose you lost your job — what would you do? Supposing she said no? Imagine you were twenty again. What if we moved the meeting to Thursday?

The same frames also take a plain present when the speaker wants the open reading: What if we move the meeting to Thursday? is a live proposal; What if we moved it? floats an idea. Same one-step mechanism, same effect.

Distance as politeness#

This is where the mechanism earns its keep in everyday speech, and it is invisible to learners who think the past tense means past time. Backing a request one step into the past makes it less direct, and less direct means more polite — you are presenting the request as something you are not quite asking, so the other person is free not to quite refuse.

DirectDistancedWhat the distance buys
Can you help me?Could you help me?a request rather than a demand
Will you sign this?Would you sign this?the same, softer
I want the check.I'd like the check.not an announcement of appetite
Do you want to see me?Did you want to see me?a receptionist's neutral opening
I wonder if you could give me a hand.I wondered if you could give me a hand.nothing is being asked yet
I'm hoping you can fit me in.I was hoping you could fit me in.easier to decline
Do you have a minute?Would you have a minute?formal, unhurried
Is it possible to change the date?Would it be possible to change the date?no presumption
I think that's a mistake.I'd have thought that was a mistake.disagreement without a flat contradiction

None of these is about past time. Did you want to see me? does not ask about a wish you held yesterday; I was hoping you could fit me in does not report a hope that has since expired. The past form is doing the politeness job, and the present form in each left-hand column is grammatical, correct, and blunter.

Layering the markers increases the distance, and English lets you stack quite a few:

Can you call me back? → Could you call me back? → I wondered whether you could call me back. → I was wondering whether you might be able to call me back.

Each step back is a step back from the demand. The last version is what you write to someone who has every right to say no.

Why were is the one surviving special form#

Modern English has one verb that still marks unreality with a shape of its own: be. The unreal form is were for every subject, including I, he, she, and it.

If I were you, I'd take the offer. If he were serious, he'd have put it in writing. If the house were any closer to the highway, you couldn't sleep. I wish it weren't so far. She talks about it as if it were already settled. If I were to tell you the number, you wouldn't believe me.

This is the last visible piece of the old subjunctive, and it survives because be is the only English verb whose past tense has two forms to spare. Everywhere else the unreal form and the ordinary past form are spelled identically, which is why the distinction is invisible.

Two practical notes:

  • In speech, Americans say if I was constantly — if I was taller, if he was here — and it is not stigmatized in conversation. In edited writing, were is the expected form. Write were; do not correct people who say was.
  • If I were you resists the substitution anyway. It is close to a fixed phrase, and ✗ if I was you sounds wrong even to speakers who use was elsewhere. Never ✗ If I am you or ✗ If I would be you.

The negative contracts as weren't: If it weren't for the traffic, we'd be there. See the contraction entry for weren't.

The complete time-reference table#

Two clauses, two clocks#

Here is the fact that makes mixed conditionals stop being a special topic. The condition clause and the result clause each carry their own time reference, and the two are independent. A condition about last year can have a result about right now. A condition about next week can have a result about an hour ago. Every combination is available, and every one of them is built by the same two moves.

So building any conditional is answering three questions in order:

  1. Real or unreal? — decides whether you shift back a step.
  2. What time is the condition about? — decides the form in the if-clause.
  3. What time is the result about? — decides the form in the main clause.

Two notes on the real grid.

The if-clause never takes will for a future condition. If it rains tomorrow — present form, future meaning. This is the single most common error made by Russian speakers, for reasons the next section sets out, and it is the same tenseless-future present that when, as soon as, before, and until require.

The past-condition rows are not third conditionals. If she called, I didn't hear the phone says nothing about whether she called; it says what follows if she did. If she had called, I would have heard the phone says she did not call. Same two events, opposite claims, and the only difference is one step of tense.

Time adverbials are half the signal#

Because the grid's cells share forms, the adverbial is often what tells a reader which cell you are in. Watch the same condition serve three different results:

If I'd left at six, I'd have been there by seven. (past result) If I'd left at six, I'd be halfway to Denver by now. (present result) If I'd left at six, I'd be having dinner with them tomorrow. (future result)

And the same result served by three different conditions:

If you hadn't reminded me, I'd have missed it. (past condition) If I weren't so disorganized, I'd have remembered it myself. (present condition — a standing trait) If I were going to forget anything, it would have been the passport. (future-oriented condition)

Drop the adverbial and some of these go ambiguous. Keep now, by then, tomorrow, back then, and at the time in the sentence when the cell is not obvious — they are doing grammatical work, not decoration.

Why English needs two unreal patterns where Russian has one#

Russian marks unreality once; English marks it with tense#

Russian has a single, clean device for unreality: бы plus the past-tense form of the verb. It appears in both halves of the sentence, and it says nothing at all about time.

Если бы у меня были деньги, я бы купил машину.

That one Russian sentence is two different English sentences, and Russian does not distinguish them. Context does — a сейчас or a тогда, or simply what the conversation has been about.

If I had the money, I'd buy a car. (I don't have it — now) If I had had the money, I'd have bought a car. (I didn't have it — then)

This is the whole difficulty in one line. Russian marks unreality with a particle and leaves time to context. English marks unreality with tense, so it has already spent its tense on unreality and must use a second device — the perfect — to get the time back. That second device is have + past participle, and it is what separates would do from would have done.

The last row is the free gift. Russian uses бы for polite distance too — не могли бы вы, я бы хотел, не подскажете ли — so the idea of marking politeness with the unreality device is already familiar. English simply uses the past tense where Russian uses бы.

The choice, reduced to one question#

When a Russian sentence with бы has to become English, do not ask whether it is "second" or "third." Ask:

Is the situation still open, or is it over and settled?

  • Still open — the world could still be otherwise, or the statement is about a standing state of affairs: use past / would. If I lived closer, I'd see them more often.
  • Over and settled — the moment has passed, the outcome is fixed, nothing can change it: use past perfect / would have. If I'd lived closer, I'd have seen them more often.

And ask it separately for each clause, because that is exactly what produces the mixed patterns in the grid above:

Если бы я выучил испанский, я бы сейчас работал в Мадриде. → The condition is settled (I didn't learn it, and that is done) and the result is about now → If I'd learned Spanish, I'd be working in Madrid now.

What goes wrong when бы transfers directly#

The doubled would. Russian бы appears in both clauses. Learners map бы onto would and put would in both clauses too.

✗ If I would have the money, I would buy it. → ✓ If I had the money, I would buy it. ✗ If I would have known, I would have told you. → ✓ If I had known, I would have told you. ✗ If she wouldn't have called, we would have missed it. → ✓ If she hadn't called, we would have missed it. ✗ If I would be you, I'd say nothing. → ✓ If I were you, I'd say nothing.

The rule is short: the if-clause never contains would in an unreal conditional. Unreality is already marked there by the tense; would is what marks it in the other clause. Russian marks it twice because бы is a particle and particles are cheap; English marks it twice too, but with two different devices — the past tense on one side and would on the other.

(The one genuine would inside an if-clause is the volitional one of polite requests — if you would just sign here, if you would wait a moment — which means be willing to and is not part of this system. American English also allows a result-based will: If it will make you feel better, I'll call him.)

The future in the if-clause. Russian puts the future tense in the если-clause, and English refuses it:

Если будет дождь, мы отменим. → ✗ If it will rain, we'll cancel. → ✓ If it rains, we'll cancel. Если ты увидишь его, скажи ему. → ✗ If you will see him, tell him. → ✓ If you see him, tell him. Когда он придёт, я позвоню. → ✗ When he will come, I'll call. → ✓ When he comes, I'll call.

Picking the wrong pattern for the time. Because бы gives no tense signal, a learner who has memorized one pattern uses it for everything:

(about a job offer that closed last month) ✗ If they offered me more, I would take it. → ✓ If they had offered me more, I would have taken it. (about an offer still on the table) ✗ If they had offered me more, I would have taken it. → ✓ If they offered me more, I would take it.

Both sentences are perfect English; each is wrong for the situation it was used in. This is the error that survives longest in otherwise fluent speech, precisely because nothing in the sentence is ungrammatical.

Over-marking the past. The mirror-image error is reaching for the past perfect whenever the conversation is about the past, even when the condition is a standing trait:

✗ If I had been taller, I would have made the team. (only if your height at the time is the point) ✓ If I were taller, I would have made the team. (you are still short; the tryout is over — a present condition with a past result, row 5 of the grid)

Degrees of likelihood#

Real and unreal are the two ends of a scale, not a binary. English has a graded set of if-clause forms between them, and the choice tells the listener how seriously the speaker is entertaining the condition.

FormLabelWhat it signalsRegister
If it rains…openno position at allneutral, everyday
If it happens to rain…chanceslightly offhand; "as it may turn out"conversational
If it should rain…remote but real"in the unlikely event"; still worth planning forslightly formal
Should it rain…remote but realthe same, invertedformal, written

If it were to rain…purely hypotheticalentertaining a supposition, with no claim at allneutral to formal
If it had rained…counterfactualit did notneutral

If it rains — the open form#

The baseline. It makes no probability claim, which is why it fits things that are near-certain and things that are long shots alike:

If I get the job, I'll call you right away. If the package arrives before noon, leave it with the neighbor. If anyone asks, you haven't seen me. If that is true, we have a serious problem.

If it should rain — the remote form#

The should here is not obligation and not advice. Grammars call it the putative should, and its job is to flag the condition as unlikely while still taking it seriously enough to plan for. It is the form of instructions, safety notices, and hospitality.

If you should need anything, the front desk is open all night. If the alarm should sound, use the stairs. If he should call while I'm out, take a message. If anything should happen to the file, we have a backup.

The inverted version drops if entirely and is one degree more formal. It is common in written American English and rare in speech:

Should you need anything, the front desk is open all night. Should the alarm sound, use the stairs. Should you wish to cancel, written notice is required.

Do not confuse it with obligation should: ✗ If you should call me, I'd appreciate it reads oddly, because a request is not an unlikely event. And note that would cannot substitute: ✗ If you would need anything → ✓ If you should need anything.

If it rained — the unlikely form#

The ordinary unreal present-or-future. It is worth stressing that this form measures the speaker's stance, not the world's odds:

If I won the lottery, I'd pay off the house. (a real possibility; the speaker declines to count on it) If you took the eight o'clock, you'd be there by ten. (perfectly feasible advice, offered without pressure) If somebody offered you the job tomorrow, would you take it? (a question the asker may know the answer to)

That last use is the everyday one: the distanced form makes a question easier to answer honestly, because it has been framed as hypothetical.

If it were to rain — the pure hypothesis#

Were to + base form makes the condition explicitly a supposition. It suspends the question of likelihood rather than answering it, which makes it the form of negotiation, planning, and thought experiments.

If we were to offer you a raise, would you stay? If the bridge were to fail, the whole corridor would be cut off. If she were to resign tomorrow, who would take over? If interest rates were to double, half these loans would default.

Were to and putative should both have fuller entries in Mixed conditionals and variations, and their inverted forms in Inversion and formal conditionals.

Two limits. Were to wants an event, not a state — If I were to be taller is odd where If I were taller is natural. And it points forward: it works for a hypothetical future, not for a general truth. Americans also use was to in casual speech (if it was to rain), but were to is the written form.

Stacking and the edges of the scale#

The forms combine, and the combinations sit exactly where you would expect:

If it should happen to rain, we'll move indoors. (remote + offhand) If you should ever need a reference, ask me. (remote + open-ended) If by any chance he were to call, don't mention the offer. (maximally hedged)

Related forms from the neighboring vocabulary — unless, provided that, as long as, on the off chance that, in the event that, supposing — have their own entries in the subordinators of condition. One of them is a standing trap, set out in full in In case: precaution, not condition: in case is not if. Take an umbrella in case it rains means take it because it might rain — the taking happens first and happens regardless. Take an umbrella if it rains means take it only when rain has arrived.

Conditionals inside reported speech#

Reporting a conditional tests everything above, because a report applies a second backward shift on top of whatever shift the conditional already carries — and the ladder only has so many rungs.

TypeDirectReported after a past verbWhat moved

Real, future"If it rains, we'll cancel."She said that if it rained, they would cancel.both clauses
Real, future, still live"If it rains, we'll cancel."She said that if it rains, they'll cancel.nothing — the condition is still open
Unreal present"If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish."He said that if he had more time, he'd learn Spanish.pronouns only
Unreal past"If I'd left earlier, I'd have caught it."He said that if he'd left earlier, he'd have caught it.pronouns only

A real conditional shifts; an unreal one cannot. The reason is the floor. An unreal present conditional has already spent the present-to-past step on marking unreality, and pushing it down another rung does not report it more accurately — it changes what was said:

✗ He said that if he had had more time, he would have learned Spanish.

That reports a regret about a past opportunity. The speaker expressed a wish about the present. The shift is not a stylistic upgrade; it is a different sentence.

An unreal past conditional is at the bottom of the ladder in both clauses — had + participle and would have + participle — so the report copies it unchanged. There is no ✗ had had known and no ✗ would have had called.

The report neutralizes the real / unreal distinction. This follows directly from "unreal = one step back," and it is a genuine loss of information:

The original wordsThe report
"If it rains, we'll cancel." (open)She said that if it rained, they would cancel.
"If it rained, we'd cancel." (unreal)She said that if it rained, they would cancel.

Once the real version takes its step back, it lands exactly where the unreal version already was. If the difference matters, a reporter fixes it outside the tense system — She said they would cancel if the forecast turned out to be right against She said they would cancel, though she doubted it would come to that.

Three further points, all with fuller treatments elsewhere:

  • Never put would in a reported if-clause. ✗ She said that if it would rain, they would cancel. The condition takes a past form, exactly as it does in the direct version.
  • Modals in the result clause shift with everything else — can → could, may → might, must → had to — while would, could, might, and should are already at the floor and do not move. The complete inventory is in Modal verbs in reported speech.
  • The mechanism itself — the one-step-back ladder, the past-perfect floor, and why an unreal past is invisible to backshift — is Backshift, and the conditional-by-conditional breakdown is in Special cases.

Conditional meaning without a conditional form#

A conditional is a meaning, not a word. English has half a dozen ways to build one without if, and most of them are commoner in speech than the full clause is. The conjunctions that replace if — unless, provided that, as long as, even if, whether, suppose — are a separate matter, handled in Alternatives to if; what follows is the set of structures that carry a condition with no conditional connector at all.

Imperative or noun phrase + and / or#

The most compressed conditional in the language. The first element states the condition; and delivers the consequence, or delivers the alternative.

One more word and you're out. (= if you say one more word) Touch that wire and you'll get a shock. Say that again and I'm leaving. Give him a week and he'll have it running. Hurry up, or we'll miss the opening. Sign here, or the policy lapses tomorrow.

The pattern carries unreality perfectly well, and the tense in the second half is the whole signal:

Another drink and I'd have fallen over. (= if I'd had another drink — unreal past) Another drink and I'll fall over. (= if I have another — open) Two minutes earlier and I'd have caught him. A couple of inches to the left and it would have hit the window. Any later and we'd have found the place locked.

Note what carries the whole conditional meaning in Another drink and I'd have fallen over: there is no if, no clause, and no verb in the first half. The would have in the second half is doing all of it.

Participle and verbless clauses#

A participle clause states the condition in three or four words. The main clause supplies the tense, and therefore the reality status.

Given more time, we'd have finished. (= if we'd been given more time) Given the choice, I'd take the aisle seat. Left to himself, he'd have sold the place years ago. Handled carefully, it'll last twenty years. Taken alone, the number means nothing. Properly trained, a dog that size is no danger to anyone. Judging by the sky, we're going to get soaked. Weather permitting, the ceremony will be outdoors. All else being equal, I'd rather drive.

Verbless clauses work the same way, and several are fixed phrases:

If possible, call ahead. If necessary, we'll postpone. If in doubt, leave it out. If so, let me know; if not, I'll assume we're on. When in Rome, do as the Romans do.

Prepositional phrases#

With a bit of luck, we'll be there by six. With a decent lawyer, he'd have walked. Without your help, I'd have failed the course. But for the rain, we'd have won. (formal; = if it hadn't been for the rain) In your position, I'd say nothing at all. In that case, we'll take the early flight. Under different circumstances, I'd have agreed. Given the numbers, the merger doesn't make sense.

The it frames are worth memorizing as units, since they are how spoken English says but for:

If it weren't for the traffic, we'd be there by now. (present) If it hadn't been for Dana, nobody would have noticed. (past) If not for that one mistake, she'd have won. (American, informal)

Otherwise, or else, failing that#

These carry a negative condition backward to the previous sentence:

Take the map. Otherwise you'll end up on the wrong side of the river. Back it up tonight — or else you'll be doing all of it twice. We'll try the Thursday flight; failing that, we drive. I had a deadline. Otherwise I'd have come.

That last pair shows the unreal version: otherwise + would have is a full third conditional with the condition left in the sentence before.

Inversion instead of if#

Three auxiliaries — had, were, and should — can move in front of the subject and take over the work of if. It is a formal register, common in writing and in careful speech, and it is very useful for avoiding a pile-up of ifs. The full treatment, including the negative-inversion relatives, is Inversion and formal conditionals.

Had I known, I'd have said something. (= if I had known) Had it not been for the fog, we'd have landed on time. Were I in your position, I'd wait. Were the company to relocate, half the staff would quit. Should you change your mind, the offer stands. Should anything go wrong, call this number.

Two restrictions. Only those three auxiliaries invert — there is no ✗ Did I know and no ✗ Knew I. And the negative cannot contract: write Had I not known, never ✗ Hadn't I known, which is a question.

Implied conditionals in polite and tentative language#

A would in a sentence with no if-clause anywhere is almost always an unreal result clause whose condition has been left unsaid. Recovering the missing condition is the fastest way to understand why the would is there at all.

The unstated if#

What is saidThe condition it implies
That would be lovely.if you did that / if that happened

I'd say about thirty.if I had to put a number on it
I wouldn't know.if you asked me, I couldn't tell you

I wouldn't do that.if I were you
A kinder person would have apologized.if a kinder person had been in that position
Who would have thought?if you'd asked any of us beforehand

Once you can hear the missing condition, the American habit of hedging with would stops being mysterious. These are not vague; they are precise, and the precision is in the part that was not said.

Tentative estimates and opinions#

Would converts a flat assertion into an offered one. Compare each pair:

It's about thirty miles. → It'd be about thirty miles. That's Dana at the door. → That would be Dana at the door. I think he's already left. → I'd think he's already left. / I'd have thought he'd already left. The repair costs around four hundred. → The repair would run you around four hundred. I guess we could try Thursday. → I'd guess Thursday would work. That doesn't surprise me. → That wouldn't surprise me.

And for a guessed past, would have does the same job to a past assertion. This is not a third conditional; it is a hedged deduction:

That would have been around 1998, I think. He would have left before the storm hit. She wouldn't have heard about it yet. They'd have been about six or seven at the time.

Requests built out of would#

The tentative request is a negative question with would, and it is designed to make refusal effortless — it half-answers itself in the negative, so the other person only has to agree with it.

You wouldn't have a pen, would you? You wouldn't know where the copy room is, would you? You wouldn't happen to have change for a twenty? I don't suppose you'd be free on Thursday? I don't suppose there's any coffee left? You wouldn't mind waiting a couple of minutes, would you?

Alongside them, the ordinary polite frames, all of which are unreal result clauses with no condition:

Would you like some coffee? (against the blunter Do you want) I'd like to change my reservation. Would it be possible to move the meeting? I'd rather not say. I'd appreciate it if you called first. (the condition surfaces here, and the if-clause holds an open verb, not would) That would work for me. Would you mind closing the window?

Russian speakers have a running start here. Every one of those is a бы sentence in Russian — я бы хотел, не могли бы вы, было бы здорово, я бы не сказал — so the pragmatics transfer intact. All that has to change is the marker: where Russian inserts бы, English shifts the verb back one step.

Common mistakes#

The errors below are the ones that come straight out of the mechanism described in this section. The diagnostic list, with the repairs sorted by symptom, is Common mistakes.

WrongRightWhy
If it will rain, we'll cancel.If it rains, we'll cancel.A future if-clause takes the present form. Russian если будет is the source of this one.
If I would have the money, I'd buy it.If I had the money, I'd buy it.Would never appears in an unreal if-clause; the past tense already marks the unreality.
If I would have known, I would have called.If I had known, I would have called.The doubled бы transferred. One clause takes had + participle, the other takes would have + participle.
If she wouldn't have called…If she hadn't called…Same error in the negative.
If I hadn't have known…If I hadn't known…An attempt at a rung below the floor. Common in speech, never in writing.
Had I have known…Had I known…The inverted version of the same error.
If I would of known…If I had known…Would of is never correct anywhere; see would've.
If I would be you… / If I am you…If I were you…Fixed phrase. The unreal form of be is were for every subject.
If I had the money, I'd have bought it last week.If I'd had the money, I'd have bought it last week.The condition is about a settled past moment, so it needs the past perfect — unless you mean a standing trait, which is a different sentence.
If they offered me more, I would have taken it. (about a closed offer)If they had offered me more, I would have taken it.Choose the pattern by the time, not by habit. Both sentences are grammatical; only one fits.
Unless you don't hurry, we'll be late.Unless you hurry, we'll be late.Unless already means if not; the extra negative reverses the sense.

If he would be serious, he'd put it in writing.If he were serious, he'd put it in writing.Same doubled-would error, with be.

Quick reference#

Build any conditional in three decisions.

  1. Real or unreal? Real → leave the tenses alone. Unreal → shift each clause one step back: will/can/may → would/could/might, present → past, past or present perfect → past perfect.
  2. What time is the condition about? Past → had + participle (unreal) or past simple (real). Present, general, or future → past simple (unreal) or present simple (real).
  3. What time is the result about? Past → add have + participle after the modal. Present or future → bare infinitive after the modal.

The four forms, compressed.

if-clauseresult clause
Real, generalpresentpresent
Real, futurepresentwill / can / may / imperative
Unreal, present or futurepast (were for be)would / could / might + base
Unreal, pasthad + participlewould / could / might + have + participle

Mix rows 3 and 4 freely across the two columns. That is the entire mixed-conditional system.

The three jobs of the past tense.

I worked there for six years. — distance in time If I worked there, I'd know. — distance from reality I wondered if you could help. — distance from the hearer

The scale, from open to impossible.

If it rains → If it should rain → If it rained → If it were to rain → If it had rained

The two things that never happen.

✗ will in an if-clause of condition. ✗ would in an unreal if-clause.

Practice#

For each item, write the English sentence the situation calls for. Some of them have no if in the answer.

  1. (You are broke, right now.) Если бы у меня были деньги, я бы купил эту машину.
  2. (Last year, when the car was for sale and you were broke.) Если бы у меня были деньги, я бы купил ту машину.
  3. (You didn't study medicine; you are an accountant today.) Build a conditional about your present job.
  4. Report this: "If it rains, we'll cancel," she said — and the picnic is over.
  5. Report this: "If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish," he said.
  6. Report this: "If I'd known, I would have called," he said.
  7. Rewrite without if: If you had given me more time, I would have finished.
  8. Rewrite without if: If I had known, I'd have said something. (inversion)
  9. Rewrite without if: If I'd had one more drink, I'd have fallen over.
  10. Make this remote rather than open: If you need anything, call the desk.
  11. Make this a pure hypothesis: If we offer you a raise, will you stay?
  12. Soften into a request: Can you call me back? (three degrees)
  13. What is the unstated condition in That would be lovely?
  14. What is the unstated condition in I'd say about thirty?
  15. Fix: If I will see him, I'll tell him.
  16. Fix: If I would have known, I would have told you.
  17. Fix: Unless you don't hurry, we'll miss it.
  18. Fix: It's time we go.
  19. Which grid cell is If I weren't so disorganized, I wouldn't have missed the deadline?
  20. Which grid cell is If he were going to resign, he'd have said something by now?
