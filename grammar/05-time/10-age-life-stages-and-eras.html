Age, life stages, and eras#

Time measured on a person and on a civilization — I'm thirty and never ✗ I have thirty years, the life-stage words Americans now avoid, the school ladder from kindergarten to senior year, the generation labels, and the named stretches of the past behind antebellum and the Roaring Twenties.

← Time · All grammar topics

Every other section in this topic places an event on a clock or a calendar. This one applies time to people and to history: how long somebody has been alive, what stage of life that puts them in, and what to call the piece of the past you are pointing at.

Two rules carry most of the grammar, and both are mechanical.

Age is something you are, not something you have. English says I am thirty. It does not say ✗ I have thirty years — which is what Russian, French, Spanish, Italian and Portuguese each say in their own way, and which is the most instantly recognizable age error a learner can make.

A measurement used as a modifier goes singular and takes hyphens. Thirty years is a quantity; a thirty-year-old woman is a modifier, and the plural -s disappears along with the spaces. The same rule produces a two-year contract, a six-month lease and a ten-minute walk. One rule, four hundred phrases.

The rest of this section is vocabulary — but vocabulary with a social edge on it. Elderly, senior, kid, boomer, young lady and middle-aged all carry judgments the dictionary gloss does not show, and the notes below say which ones and in which direction.

Stating age#

The verb is be#

There is one pattern, and it has two acceptable lengths.

I'm thirty. I'm thirty years old. She's six. My daughter is eighteen months old. The building is over a hundred years old.

What you cannot do is stop halfway. ✗ I'm thirty years is not English: either drop years entirely or finish the phrase with old.

✗ I have thirty years. → I am thirty. ✗ I have thirty years old. → I'm thirty years old. ✗ I'm thirty years. → I'm thirty. / I'm thirty years old. ✗ To me is thirty years. → I'm thirty.

Two formal variants exist and are worth recognizing rather than using. Years of age belongs to law, medicine and government forms — No person under 21 years of age may purchase alcohol. Of + number is literary and slightly old-fashioned — a woman of thirty, a man of about fifty, children of five and seven.

The negative and the question follow be, so they need no do:

She isn't eighteen yet. Are you over 21? How old is he? — ✗ How old does he have?

Comparing ages#

He's two years older than I am. — or, conversationally, than me She's three years younger than her brother. We're the same age. · She's my age. · He's about my age. He's two years my senior. — formal; my junior is the mirror

Older and younger are the ordinary comparatives; elder and eldest survive only inside a family and only before a noun (my elder sister, the eldest of four), so ✗ She is elder than me is impossible. Note also the idioms that measure behavior instead of years: He's tall for his age, She's very mature for her age, Act your age.

A thirty-year-old woman: the compound#

When an age modifies a noun, it becomes one hyphenated block and the plural disappears.

As a statementAs a modifierAs a noun
She is thirty years old.a thirty-year-old womana thirty-year-old
He is six months old.a six-month-old babya six-month-old
The car is ten years old.a ten-year-old car—
They are eighteen.an eighteen-year-old freshmaneighteen-year-olds

Three details do all the damage:

  1. Singular year. ✗ a thirty-years-old woman, ✗ a two-years-old child.
  2. Both hyphens. ✗ a thirty year old woman, ✗ a thirty-year old woman. The whole thing is one adjective.
  3. The noun form pluralizes at the end. Two thirty-year-olds, ✗ two thirty-years-old.

American publications write the number as a figure — a 30-year-old woman, a 6-month-old — even where they would spell out other small numbers. In running prose either is fine; be consistent.

A 34-year-old man from Tulsa won the contract. The class is for four- and five-year-olds. — note the suspended hyphen It's a twelve-year-old scotch. She's dating a thirty-year-old.

In her thirties: the decade words#

To place somebody inside a decade of life, English uses a plural with a possessive determiner.

He's in his forties. She's in her early twenties. My grandmother lived into her nineties. He didn't finish college until his thirties.

SliceCoversExample
early + decadethe first three or four yearsin her early thirties = 30–33
mid- + decadethe middlein his mid-forties = 44–46
late + decadethe last three or fourin their late sixties = 66–69

Mid- takes a hyphen (mid-thirties); early and late do not. The determiner is obligatory and must match the person: ✗ He is in the forties describes the 1940s, not a man. And there is no ✗ in his tens — for that decade English says in his teens, which is why the teenage years feel like a named stage and the others do not.

Aged is the one to be careful with. British journalism writes a man aged 34; American journalism writes a man, 34, or a 34-year-old man. In American English aged survives mostly in research, medicine and bureaucratic prose — adults aged 65 and older — and it is pronounced with one syllable there, /eɪdʒd/, against the two-syllable adjective in an aged oak /ˈeɪdʒɪd/.

Note also the plural in a range: between the ages of five and twelve, never ✗ between the age of five and twelve.

Asking about age#

How old are you?#

The sentence is perfect English and socially loaded. It is the normal question to and about children, on medical forms, in a doctor's office, and at a ticket window. Between adults who are not close it is intrusive, and in an American workplace it is close to unaskable: federal law protects workers over 40, so interviewers are trained never to raise the subject.

How old are you? — to a child: completely ordinary How old is she? — about a child: ordinary small talk Do you mind if I ask how old you are? — to an adult: acceptable if there is a reason May I ask your age? — formal, and clearly flagged as a request What's your age? — a form, not a conversation

What's your age? is not rude, it is clinical. Said aloud it sounds like an intake form, and native speakers reach for How old are you? or Can I ask how old you are? instead. In writing, on a form, Age with a blank after it is the normal thing.

Americans treat age as one of a small cluster of private questions — age, salary, weight, politics, religion — and the polite move is to let people volunteer it. The stock deflections are worth recognizing:

"Old enough." "A lady never tells." "Twenty-nine. Again."

Asking about children#

With children the questions multiply, because age, school year and size are all fair game.

How old is she? — "She'll be three in March." How old is he now? — now softens it into catching up rather than interrogating What grade is she in? — the American default once a child is in school What year is he in? — college, or a British speaker How far along are you? — to a pregnant woman: weeks or months, not the baby's age

For babies, Americans count in months until roughly two and in half-years after that: She's eight months, He's eighteen months, She's two and a half. Saying ✗ She's zero or She's one year and six months marks you as a non-native speaker instantly.

The nouns themselves — plurals, pronunciation, Russian glosses — are in the people vocabulary and childcare and parenting.

The boundaries are not sharp, and that is the point#

Only three of those words have a fixed edge: teenager (13 through 19, by arithmetic), minor (under 18, by law) and newborn (a medical window). Everything else is a judgment that shifts with the speaker's own age — a 60-year-old calls a 45-year-old young, a 25-year-old calls the same person middle-aged. So: do not argue about the categories, and do not apply the unflattering ones to people in front of you. Middle-aged and elderly are third-person descriptions of strangers, not labels you hand to somebody at a party.

The words to handle carefully#

Elderly has moved. It used to be the polite alternative to old; it now reads as a euphemism that implies frailty and dependence, and most American newsrooms and style guides discourage it. Prefer older:

✗ an elderly man → an older man ✗ elderly people → older people · older adults ✗ the elderly → older Americans · people over 75

Use elderly only when frailty is genuinely the point (an elderly relative who needs daily care), and never as a form of address.

Old is blunt but not forbidden. My old man is slang for a father or a husband; an old friend is about the friendship's length, not the friend's age; the old days is affectionate. But You're old is an insult and an old woman in a news report would be rewritten.

Senior is genuinely ambiguous. A senior is a twelfth-grader, a fourth-year college student, a person over 65, or a job rank — and only context sorts it out. Senior citizen is unambiguous and slightly bureaucratic; seniors on a store sign means over-65s, while seniors in a school newsletter means the graduating class.

Kid is not disrespectful in American English. How are the kids? is the normal question about somebody's children, and kids is used of college students, new employees and anyone visibly younger than the speaker. What is disrespectful is boy or girl for an adult — a history that makes girl for a grown woman and boy for a Black man offensive in different but serious ways.

Age-related constructions#

At, by, and from#

PatternMeaningExample

Two traps ride along with these.

English has no ✗ in thirty years meaning "at thirty." Russian «в тридцать лет» goes to at thirty; in thirty years means thirty years from now. The two sentences are about opposite ends of a life:

She got her license at sixteen. — when she was sixteen She'll get her license in sixteen years. — sixteen years from today

Do not double the preposition. At the age of thirty and at thirty are both complete; ✗ at the age of thirty years old is not.

American style guides prefer older to over for people, precisely because over 65 is ambiguous about whether it includes 65. For a rule, write 65 and older; for conversation, over sixty-five is what everybody says.

Of age, underage, coming of age#

Of age means having reached the legal threshold — which one depends on the activity and, in the United States, sometimes on the state.

He isn't of age yet. anyone of voting age — 18 nationwide anyone of drinking age — 21 nationwide, which surprises visitors

Underage is one word and an adjective: underage drinking, an underage driver, She's underage. ✗ under age as a modifier is a misspelling, though under the age of 21 is correct as a phrase.

Come of age is the verb — literally reaching adulthood, figuratively a person, a technology or an art form arriving at maturity. Hyphenated before a noun, it becomes the name of an entire American genre:

She came of age in the 1970s. Streaming came of age during the pandemic. a coming-of-age novel · a coming-of-age story · a coming-of-age film

Born in, and a child of the nineties#

I was born in 1994. — year I was born in July. · I was born on July 4th. — month versus date I'm a child of the nineties. · I'm a nineties kid. a lifelong New Yorker · a native Texan

A child of the nineties claims the decade's culture, not just its birth certificate — it means cartoons, dial-up and a particular kind of music, and somebody born in 1988 can say it honestly. The decade forms themselves (the 1990s, the '90s, the nineties, and the apostrophe that does not go before the s) are in Dates, days and years.

School and career time#

The American ladder#

American schooling is counted in grades, numbered 1 to 12, with kindergarten in front of them. The whole span is written K–12 and said "kay through twelve."

   age 5        kindergarten
   ages 6–10    grades 1–5      elementary school  (also: grade school, grammar school)
   ages 11–13   grades 6–8      middle school      (also: junior high, grades 7–9)
   ages 14–18   grades 9–12     high school
   ages 18–22   years 1–4       college            (undergrad)
   after that   —               grad school, law school, med school

Districts draw the middle lines differently — some run elementary through grade 6, some call grades 7–9 junior high — but the numbering of the grades themselves never varies.

She's in third grade. — ✗ in the third class, ✗ in three class He's a third-grader. My son starts kindergarten in the fall. He's a senior in high school, and his sister's a college freshman.

Freshman, sophomore, junior, senior#

The same four words name the four years of high school and the four years of college. They are nouns and adjectives at once, and they have no Russian equivalent at all.

WordHigh schoolCollegeAs an adjective
freshman9th grade1st yearfreshman year, a freshman seminar
sophomore10th grade2nd yearthe sophomore slump
junior11th grade3rd yearjunior year, junior prom
senior12th grade4th yearsenior year, senior pictures

I was a sophomore when the plant closed. Junior year is when the college applications start. He's a freshman at Ohio State.

Two live notes. The years extend metaphorically: a sophomore album is a band's second record, and the sophomore slump is the disappointing follow-up to anything. And freshman is being replaced on many campuses by first-year (a first-year student, first-year housing) on the grounds that -man is not gender-neutral. Freshman is still overwhelmingly what people say; first-year is what the admissions office writes.

Class of 2026#

The class is the graduating year, not the year of entry, and it takes the only sometimes.

She's class of 2026. a proud member of the class of 2014

Related American school vocabulary that does not map onto Russian: alumnus / alumna / alumni (graduates), alma mater (the school you attended), commencement (the graduation ceremony), valedictorian (the top of the class, who gives the speech).

The verb graduate, and the word school#

I graduated from Michigan in 2011. — the American standard I graduated in 2011. — fine, the school understood ✗ I graduated Michigan. — heard in speech, still flagged in writing ✗ I finished the university. → I graduated from college.

And a word that trips up every Russian speaker: in American English school includes college. Where did you go to school? usually means "which university," I'm still in school can be said by a 26-year-old in a PhD program, and back to school covers everything from kindergarten to grad school. When you mean the years before college, say high school or grade school.

Gap years and time off#

She took a gap year before college. a nontraditional student — someone starting or returning later than usual

The gap year is a recognized thing in the United States but much less routine than in Britain; took a year off is the commoner phrasing.

How Americans use them#

She's a Millennial. · He's Gen X. · They're Boomers. That's such a Gen Z thing to say. Millennials killed the department store, apparently. My parents are Baby Boomers — they bought that house for forty thousand dollars. OK boomer. — a 2019 dismissal, aimed at an attitude rather than an age

Four usage facts:

  • They are capitalized — a Millennial, Gen Z, Baby Boomers — because they are names, not descriptions.
  • The attaches to some and not others. The Greatest Generation, the Silent Generation, the Baby Boomers take an article; Gen X, Gen Z, Millennials usually do not.
  • The labels mean taste, not arithmetic. Very Millennial points at avocado toast and a particular kind of earnest humor, not at a birth certificate; a person born in 1980 or 1996 picks whichever side they prefer, and Xennial exists for the 1977–1983 cusp.
  • They are pop sociology. No agency defines them, and a careful sentence uses real ages: adults under 30, not young people these days.

The one to be careful with is epoch. In American English it is a technical word for a slice of geological time, plus a fossil literary use meaning a decisive turning point. It is not the ordinary word for a historical stretch — that is era or period. ✗ the Soviet epoch → the Soviet era.

Antebellum is worth a line of its own, because it is a word Americans use casually and foreigners meet only in textbooks. It is an adjective, it never stands alone, and it refers to the American South before 1861: an antebellum mansion, the antebellum South, antebellum politics. Its opposite is postbellum, which almost nobody says.

BC, AD, BCE, CE#

NotationStands forPositionExample
BCBefore Christafter the year44 BC
ADanno Domini, "in the year of the Lord"before the year, traditionallyAD 1066

Five practical points:

  1. BCE/CE are the academic default in American museums, textbooks and scholarship. They map exactly onto BC/AD — same years, no religious claim.
  2. Never mix the pairs. ✗ 44 BC to 1066 CE. Pick one system per document.
  3. AD goes first in strict style — AD 1066 — but 1066 AD is now common and no longer worth fighting; with a century, only the trailing position works (the fourth century AD). Modern American style drops the periods: BC, not B.C.
  4. There is no year zero. 1 BC is followed immediately by AD 1, which is why a span crossing the line is always one year shorter than the arithmetic suggests.

Circa, and the approximate date#

Circa is Latin for "around," abbreviated c. or ca., and it belongs to dates only.

The house dates from circa 1840. a portrait, c. 1650

It modifies a date, not a quantity: ✗ circa two hundred people is a joke in English, where about or roughly is the word. The everyday alternatives are around 1840, about 1840, sometime in the 1840s, the early 1840s, the mid-nineteenth century, the turn of the century.

Two pairs decide a lot of sentences.

Modern versus contemporary. In everyday use they overlap. In art, architecture and design they do not: Modern art is a historical movement running roughly from the 1860s to the 1970s, and contemporary art is what is being made now. So a modern building can be eighty years old, and a contemporary one cannot.

Historic versus historical. Historic means important enough to be remembered; historical means having to do with the past.

a historic vote — one that mattered a historical novel — one set in the past the historic district — protected because it matters historical records — records of the past

And contemporary has a second sense that catches learners out: a contemporary of Lincoln means somebody who lived at the same time as Lincoln, not somebody alive today.

Duration of things, not people#

The compound rule#

The same rule that produced a thirty-year-old woman runs the whole vocabulary of contracts, leases and journeys: a noun used as a modifier goes singular and takes a hyphen.

Predicate — pluralModifier — singular and hyphenated
The contract runs for two years.a two-year contract
The lease is for six months.a six-month lease
The study ran for ten years.a ten-year study · a decade-long study
The walk takes ten minutes.a ten-minute walk
The shift is eight hours.an eight-hour shift
The warranty lasts 90 days.a 90-day warranty

✗ a two-years contract → a two-year contract ✗ a six-months lease → a six-month lease ✗ a ten minutes walk → a ten-minute walk ✗ a 90 days warranty → a 90-day warranty

The -long family and the apostrophe alternative#

-long compounds do the same job with a different shape. AP style closes up the short ones and hyphenates the long ones: yearlong, daylong, weeklong, monthlong, but decade-long, hourlong (also closed), season-long.

a yearlong renovation · a weeklong conference · a decade-long study · a lifelong friend

The other alternative uses a possessive apostrophe, and here the plural comes back:

two weeks' notice · a week's vacation · ten years' experience · a day's work

All three are available for the same idea: a ten-year sentence, ten years' imprisonment, ten years of experience. What is not available is the bare plural with no apostrophe and no hyphen — ✗ ten years experience is extremely common on résumés and still gets corrected.

For Russian speakers#

Six interference points, in order of how visible the error is.

1. Age takes be, and the subject is the person.

Russian puts the person in the dative and the years in the genitive plural: «Мне тридцать лет», «Ей два года», «Ему двадцать один год». There is no verb at all in the present. English needs a subject, a verb, and no case at all:

RussianEnglishNot
«Мне тридцать лет»I'm thirty. / I'm thirty years old.✗ I have thirty years. ✗ To me thirty years.
«Сколько тебе лет?»How old are you?✗ How many years to you? ✗ How much are you?
«Ей два года»She's two.✗ To her two years.
«Ему за сорок»He's over forty. / He's in his forties.✗ He's after forty.
«Ей около тридцати»She's about thirty.✗ She's around of thirty.

The ✗ I have thirty years version usually arrives through a second foreign language — French j'ai trente ans, Spanish tengo treinta años — and it is the most conspicuous of all age errors, because no native speaker ever produces it.

2. Russian «лет» is genitive plural; English compounds are singular.

The two languages agree more than they look. Russian builds the same compounds with a fused, numeral-stem form — «тридцатилетняя женщина», «двухлетний контракт», «шестимесячная аренда» — where the word for the unit is not plural either. English does the same thing with hyphens:

RussianEnglish
«тридцатилетняя женщина»a thirty-year-old woman
«двухлетний контракт»a two-year contract
«шестимесячная аренда»a six-month lease
«Контракт на два года»The contract is for two years.

So the rule to carry across is: when Russian fuses the number into the adjective, English hyphenates it and drops the plural. When Russian uses a separate phrase («на два года»), English uses a separate phrase too (for two years).

3. «Пожилой» is older, not elderly.

This is a register trap rather than a grammar one. Russian «пожилой» is the polite word — it is what you say instead of «старый». Its dictionary translation, elderly, has drifted in American English and now implies frailty; the current polite word is older.

RussianSayAvoid
«пожилой человек»an older man · an older adultan elderly man
«пожилые люди»older people · older adultsthe elderly
«бабушка» (a stranger)an older woman✗ a grandmother — that is a relationship, not an age
«молодой человек» (address)—there is no English equivalent; use excuse me
«девушка» (to a waitress)—✗ girl, ✗ young lady; use excuse me

The last two rows matter more than they look. Russian has polite age-based forms of address; American English has none, and substituting girl or young man for a stranger's name is at best odd and at worst offensive.

4. The American school ladder has no Russian equivalent.

Russia counts eleven classes and calls them «класс»; a university student is in a «курс» and is a «студент», while a schoolchild is a «школьник» or «ученик». American English draws all these lines elsewhere.

RussianAmerican English
«Она в третьем классе»She's in third grade. ✗ in the third class
«третьеклассник»a third-grader
«одиннадцатый класс»twelfth grade · senior year of high school
«студент второго курса»a sophomore · a second-year student
«школьник»a student — pupil is British and sounds foreign here
«Я окончил университет»I graduated from college. ✗ I finished the university.
«школа»school — but school in American English also means college

There are also twelve American grades to Russia's eleven, and a kindergarten year in front of them, so eleventh grade is not «одиннадцатый класс» — it is the year before the last one.

5. «Эпоха» is era, and «век» is two words.

✗ the Soviet epoch → the Soviet era ✗ the epoch of the Internet → the age of the Internet · the Internet era «двадцатый век» → the twentieth century «золотой век» → the golden age «Средние века» → the Middle Ages — plural in both languages «до нашей эры» → BC / BCE · «нашей эры» → AD / CE

Epoch in American English is a geological term. Russian «эпоха» covers everything English splits between era, age and period, and era is the safe default.

6. «В тридцать лет» is at thirty, not in thirty years.

Russian «в» plus an age means the age reached; English in plus a period means the future. The mapping is:

RussianEnglishMeans
«в тридцать лет»at thirty · at the age of thirtywhen she was thirty
«через тридцать лет»in thirty years · thirty years from nowthe future
«тридцать лет назад»thirty years agothe past
«к тридцати годам»by thirty · by the age of thirtyat or before
«с шести лет»from the age of sixstarting then

The in / ago / at split is the same one worked out in full in Duration and frequency and Prepositions of time; what is specific here is that an age takes at, while a span from now takes in.

What transfers for free. The compound rule, as noted in point 2. And the tense pairings in the change-over-time table, because Russian also demands a past for «тогда» and a present for «сейчас» — the only one with no Russian counterpart is the present perfect after since then, which is treated in the present perfect.

Quick reference#

Age, in five lines.

   statement     I'm 30.        I'm 30 years old.      (never: I have 30 years)
   modifier      a 30-year-old woman                   (singular year, two hyphens)
   noun          a 30-year-old  /  two 30-year-olds
   decade        in her thirties · in her mid-thirties (her, not the)
   at a point    at 30 · at the age of 30 · by 30 · from the age of 6

The one rule behind half this page. A measurement in front of a noun loses its plural and gains hyphens: two years → a two-year contract; thirty years → a thirty-year-old woman; ten minutes → a ten-minute walk.
