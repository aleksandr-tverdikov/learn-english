## Clock times

English has two readings for any time. The **digital reading** says the numbers and nothing else. The **analog reading** counts minutes forward from the hour just past, or backward to the hour coming up. Americans use both, and the digital reading is quietly winning: it is what every screen shows, it never has to choose between *after* and *past*, and it cannot be misheard. The analog reading survives strongly at the quarter hours and on round fives.

One full hour, in five-minute steps, with every reading an American might actually produce.

| Clock | Digital reading | Analog reading (most common) | Also standard | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **3:00** | *three* | *three o'clock* | *three sharp*, *three on the dot*, *three exactly* | *o'clock* attaches only to a whole hour |
| **3:05** | *three oh five* | *five after three* | *five past three* | the zero is the letter **oh**, never *zero* |
| **3:10** | *three ten* | *ten after three* | *ten past three* | — |
| **3:15** | *three fifteen* | *a quarter after three* | *a quarter past three*, *fifteen after three* | *a quarter* keeps its article in careful speech |
| **3:20** | *three twenty* | *twenty after three* | *twenty past three* | — |
| **3:25** | *three twenty-five* | *twenty-five after three* | *twenty-five past three* | the last slot that counts **forward** |
| **3:30** | *three thirty* | *half past three* | *half after three* (dated) | ✗ *half three* means 3:30 in Britain and nothing in America |
| **3:35** | *three thirty-five* | *twenty-five to four* | *twenty-five of four*, *twenty-five till four* | the count flips to the **next** hour |
| **3:40** | *three forty* | *twenty to four* | *twenty of four*, *twenty till four* | ✗ *forty after three* is not said |
| **3:45** | *three forty-five* | *a quarter to four* | *a quarter of four*, *a quarter till four* | — |
| **3:50** | *three fifty* | *ten to four* | *ten of four*, *ten till four* | — |
| **3:55** | *three fifty-five* | *five to four* | *five of four*, *five till four* | — |

Four things the table cannot say in a cell.

**The direction flips at the half hour.** Through 3:30 you count up from three; from 3:31 on you count down to four. There is no American who says ✗ *forty after three*, and no hour in which *twenty to* could mean anything but 3:40, 4:40, and so on.

**Three regional words fill one slot.** *Twenty **to** four* is national and always safe. *Twenty **of** four* is Northeastern — New England, New York, Philadelphia — and sounds slightly formal elsewhere. *Twenty **till** four* is Southern and Midland. All three are standard American English; none is a mistake.

**Say *noon* and *midnight*.** 12:00 p.m. is noon and 12:00 a.m. is midnight, which is the opposite of what most people guess, so American offices write *12 noon* and *12 midnight* on anything that matters. A deadline of *midnight Friday* means the instant Friday ends in some offices and the instant it begins in others; ask.

**The 24-hour clock is military time.** *0600* is *oh six hundred*, *1400* is *fourteen hundred*, *2330* is *twenty-three thirty*. The armed forces, hospitals, police, and aviation use it; ordinary American civilian life does not, and writing *the meeting is at 14:00* in a normal office email reads as European.

## The preposition decision table

Three prepositions and one empty slot cover almost every time expression in English. What decides is the **kind of thing that follows**, not the meaning of the sentence, which is why this can be a lookup table instead of an explanation.

| What follows the preposition | Use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| a clock time | **at** | *at 6:15* &middot; *at a quarter to nine* &middot; *at 3 a.m.* |
| *noon, midnight, dawn, dusk, sunrise, sunset* | **at** | *at noon* &middot; *at sunset* |
| *night* | **at** | *at night* — but *in the morning / afternoon / evening* |
| *the moment, present, the time, once, first, last* | **at** | *at the moment* &middot; *at present* &middot; *at once* &middot; *at last* |
| a mealtime as a point | **at** | *at lunch* &middot; *at breakfast* &middot; *at dinner* |
| an age | **at** | *at seventeen* &middot; *at the age of sixty-two* |
| *the end / the beginning* + *of* | **at** | *at the end of the month* &middot; *at the start of the year* |
| *the same time, the latest, the earliest* | **at** | *at the same time* &middot; *Friday at the latest* |
| *Christmas, Easter, Thanksgiving* as a **season** | **at** | *at Christmas we go north* (the whole stretch) |
| a day of the week | **on** | *on Monday* &middot; *on Tuesdays* &middot; *on Sundays* |
| a calendar date | **on** | *on July 4* &middot; *on the fourth* &middot; *on March 3, 2026* |
| a day plus a part of it | **on** | *on Monday morning* &middot; *on the night of the twelfth* |
| a named single day | **on** | *on my birthday* &middot; *on New Year's Day* &middot; *on Christmas Day* |
| *the weekend* | **on** (American) | *on the weekend* — British: *at the weekend* |
| an anniversary or occasion | **on** | *on our anniversary* &middot; *on that occasion* |
| a month | **in** | *in June* &middot; *in early September* |
| a season | **in** | *in the summer* &middot; *in winter* (both articles are fine) |
| a year, decade, or century | **in** | *in 1999* &middot; *in the nineties* &middot; *in the 21st century* |
| a part of the day, not *night* | **in** | *in the morning* &middot; *in the afternoon* &middot; *in the evening* |
| a length counted forward from now | **in** | *in an hour* &middot; *in three days* &middot; *in a week* |
| the time something took to finish | **in** | *She ran it in four hours.* |
| a long era | **in** | *in the Middle Ages* &middot; *in her childhood* |
| *last, next, this, that, every, each, all, some, one, any* | **none** | *last June* &middot; *next Friday* &middot; *this morning* &middot; *every day* &middot; *all week* |
| *today, tomorrow, yesterday, tonight* | **none** | *See you tomorrow.* &middot; *I called yesterday.* |
| *the day before yesterday, the day after tomorrow* | **none** | *He left the day before yesterday.* |
| *ago* | **none** — it follows the phrase | *two weeks ago* &middot; *a long time ago* |
| a bare day in casual American speech | **none** (optional) | *I'll call you Tuesday.* — British keeps *on* |
| a length of time, answering *how long* | **for** | *for three hours* &middot; *for two years* |
| a starting point of a stretch still running | **since** | *since Monday* &middot; *since 2019* |
| a named event you were inside | **during** | *during the movie* &middot; *during the war* |
| a deadline | **by** | *by Friday* &middot; *by the time you read this* |
| a continuous stretch ending at a point | **until**, *till* | *until nine* &middot; *till Monday* |
| a span with both ends named | **from … to / through** | *from nine to five* &middot; *Monday through Friday* (American) |

**The zero row is the one Russian speakers lose.** *Last, next, this, every* and the bare day-names already carry the time relation, so English refuses a preposition on top of it: ✗ *on last week*, ✗ *in next Friday*, ✗ *in this morning*, ✗ *in every day*. The rule is easier to keep if you read it as a ban rather than an option — a determiner in the phrase means no preposition in front of it.

**The one real overlap is *at* and *on* with holidays.** *At Christmas* means the season, roughly the week around it; *on Christmas* or *on Christmas Day* means December 25 itself. *Over Christmas*, *over the weekend*, *over the summer* is a third option, very American, and means "across that whole stretch."

## The confusable pairs

Each of these is two English expressions that map onto one Russian word or construction, which is why they come out wrong even when the grammar is otherwise good. The test column is the question that settles it without thinking about the meaning.

| Pair | Which one | Test | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***by*** vs. ***until*** | **by** = not later than that point, one action &middot; **until** = continuously up to that point | Does the action **happen once** or **keep going**? | *Send it **by** Friday.* &middot; *The office is open **until** six.* | ✗ *Send it until Friday.* |
| ***for*** vs. ***since*** | **for** + a **length** &middot; **since** + a **starting point** | Does the phrase answer *how long* or *starting when*? | *for three years* &middot; *since 2022* | ✗ *since three years* &middot; ✗ *for 2022* |
| ***during*** vs. ***for*** | **during** + a **named event or period** &middot; **for** + a **measured length** | Can you count it in units? Then **for**. | *during the flight* &middot; *for six hours* | ✗ *during six hours* |
| ***in*** vs. ***within*** | **in** = after that much time has passed &middot; **within** = at some point before that limit is reached | Does it happen **at the far end** or **anywhere inside**? | *I'll be back **in** an hour.* &middot; *Reply **within** 24 hours.* | ✗ *Reply in 24 hours at the latest* (means "in exactly 24") |
| ***on time*** vs. ***in time*** | **on time** = punctual, at the scheduled moment &middot; **in time** = early enough, with margin | Substitute *punctually* → **on time**. Substitute *early enough to* → **in time**. | *The train left **on time**.* &middot; *We got there **in time** to eat.* | ✗ *We arrived on time to save him.* |
| ***at the end*** vs. ***in the end*** | **at the end (of X)** = at the final point of a thing &middot; **in the end** = finally, after everything | Can you add *of something*? Then **at**. Can you swap in *eventually*? Then **in**. | *at the end of the movie* &middot; ***In the end** we drove.* | ✗ *At the end we drove.* |
| ***at last*** vs. ***lastly*** vs. ***at the last minute*** | **at last** = relief after a wait &middot; **lastly** = the final item in a list &middot; **at the last minute** = with no margin | Is it emotion, enumeration, or timing? | ***At last**, a table.* &middot; ***Lastly**, the budget.* &middot; *booked **at the last minute*** | ✗ *At last, the budget* (in a list) |
| ***in the beginning*** vs. ***at the beginning*** | **at the beginning (of X)** = the opening point &middot; **in the beginning** = originally, at first | Same test as *at / in the end*. | *at the beginning of July* &middot; ***In the beginning** we used paper.* | ✗ *In the beginning of July* |
| ***ago*** vs. ***before*** | **ago** counts back from **now** &middot; **before** counts back from a **past point** | Is the anchor *now* or a moment in the story? | *two years **ago*** &middot; *two years **before** the war* | ✗ *two years before* (meaning "from now") |
| ***after*** vs. ***in*** (future) | **in an hour** = one hour from now &middot; **after an hour** = once an hour had elapsed, inside a narrative | Is the anchor *now*? Then **in**. | *I'll call **in** an hour.* &middot; ***After** an hour he gave up.* | ✗ *I'll call after an hour.* |
| ***once*** vs. ***one time*** | **once** = one occurrence, and also "formerly" &middot; **one time** is informal | Counting? **once, twice, three times**. | *I've been there **once**.* | ✗ *two times a week* (say *twice a week*) |
| ***then*** vs. ***than*** | **then** = time &middot; **than** = comparison | Homophones in fast speech only; they are unrelated words. | *back **then*** &middot; *later **than** usual* | ✗ *earlier then usual* |

### *Already*, *yet*, *still*, *anymore*

Four words, one Russian cluster, and a position rule each.

| Word | Meaning | Sentence type | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **already** | sooner than expected; it has happened | affirmative statements; surprised questions | mid-position, or end for emphasis | *I've **already** sent it.* &middot; *Is it noon **already**?* |
| **yet** | up to now, and it has not happened | negatives and questions | end of the clause | *She hasn't called **yet**.* &middot; *Have you eaten **yet**?* |
| **still** | it was true and it continues | affirmative, negative, questions | before the main verb, after *be* | *He**'s still** asleep.* &middot; *I **still** haven't heard.* |
| **anymore**, *any longer* | it was true and it stopped | negatives and questions | end of the clause | *They don't make them **anymore**.* |
| **no longer** | the formal *anymore* | affirmative in form, negative in sense | mid-position | *He **no longer** works here.* |
| **just** | a moment ago | present perfect; in American English the past simple too | mid-position | *I**'ve just** finished.* &middot; *I **just** finished.* |

*Still* and *yet* both translate the Russian *ещё*, and the split is clean: **still** is for something that is happening, **yet** is for something that is not. *I still haven't heard* and *I haven't heard yet* both exist and both are correct, but the first is impatient and the second is neutral.

## Dates, years, decades, centuries

American date order is **month, day, year** in both writing and speech, and in the all-numeral form too — which means 3/8/2026 is March 8 in the United States and August 3 in Britain and Russia. On anything that crosses a border, spell the month.

| Written | Spoken | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| July 4, 2026 | *July fourth, twenty twenty-six* | the standard American form; comma after the day |
| July 2026 | *July twenty twenty-six* | no comma when there is no day |
| 7/4/2026 &middot; 7/4/26 | *July fourth* | US order is M/D/Y |
| 4 July 2026 | *the fourth of July, twenty twenty-six* | British form; no comma |
| 2026-07-04 | — | ISO 8601; software, filenames, databases |
| Jul. 4 &middot; Sept. 12 | *July fourth* &middot; *September twelfth* | American abbreviations take a period; *May, June, July* are not abbreviated |
| Mon., Jan. 1, 2024 | *Monday, January first, twenty twenty-four* | the weekday is set off by a comma |
| the 4th &middot; the 21st &middot; the 3rd | *the fourth* &middot; *the twenty-first* &middot; *the third* | speech always uses the ordinal, even when writing shows *July 4* |
| 1066 | *ten sixty-six* | four-digit years split into two pairs |
| 1900 | *nineteen hundred* | — |
| 1905 | *nineteen **oh** five* | the letter O again, never *zero* |
| 1999 | *nineteen ninety-nine* | — |
| 2000 | *two thousand* | ✗ *twenty hundred* |
| 2005 | *two thousand five* | American drops *and*; British says *two thousand and five* |
| 2010 | *twenty ten* &middot; *two thousand ten* | both are current; *twenty ten* has been winning since about 2011 |
| 2026 | *twenty twenty-six* | — |
| the 1980s &middot; the '80s | *the nineteen eighties* &middot; *the eighties* | no apostrophe before the *s*; the apostrophe replaces *19* |
| the 1800s | *the eighteen hundreds* | = the nineteenth century, 1800–1899 |
| the 19th century | *the nineteenth century* | = 1801–1900; it is always one ahead of its digits |
| the turn of the century | — | ambiguous now; say *around 1900* or *around 2000* |
| 44 BC &middot; AD 1066 | *forty-four BC* &middot; *AD ten sixty-six* | **BC follows** the year, **AD precedes** it |
| 44 BCE &middot; 1066 CE | *BCE* &middot; *CE* | both follow the year; standard in academic writing |
| c. 1450 &middot; ca. 1450 | *circa fourteen fifty* | "approximately" |
| 1914–18 | *nineteen fourteen to nineteen eighteen* | en dash, no spaces |
| FY2026 &middot; Q3 FY26 | *fiscal year twenty twenty-six* &middot; *Q three* | the US federal fiscal year runs October 1 to September 30 |
| 7/4–7/6 | *July fourth through the sixth* | American *through* includes the last day; *to* is ambiguous |

Two conventions that are pure formatting and still get marked wrong. **No ordinal suffix in the written American date**: *July 4, 2026*, not ✗ *July 4th, 2026* — even though you say *July fourth*. And **the decade takes no apostrophe**: *the 1990s*, *the '90s*, never ✗ *the 1990's*, which is a possessive.

## Frequency: the full scale

| Adverb | Roughly | Sentence type | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **always** | 100% | affirmative | mid only | *She**'s always** early.* |
| **constantly**, *continually* | ~95% | affirmative | mid or end | *He**'s constantly** on his phone.* |
| **almost always**, *nearly always* | 95% | affirmative | mid only | *I **almost always** walk.* |
| **usually**, *normally*, *generally*, *as a rule* | 80% | affirmative | mid, front, or end | ***Usually** I walk.* &middot; *I **usually** walk.* |
| **often**, *frequently* | 70% | affirmative | mid, front, or end | *We **often** eat late.* |
| **regularly** | 65% | affirmative | mid or end | *He calls **regularly**.* |
| **sometimes** | 50% | affirmative | mid, front, or end | ***Sometimes** it rains.* |
| **occasionally**, *now and then*, *from time to time* | 30% | affirmative | mid, front, or end | *We meet **occasionally**.* |
| **once in a while**, *every so often* | 20% | affirmative | front or end | *I see him **every so often**.* |
| **seldom**, *rarely* | 10% | already negative in sense | mid only | *He **rarely** complains.* |
| **hardly ever**, *scarcely ever* | 5% | already negative | mid only | *She **hardly ever** drives.* |
| **almost never** | 3% | already negative | mid only | *They **almost never** call.* |
| **never** | 0% | already negative | mid only | *I**'ve never** been.* |
| **ever** | — | questions, negatives, conditionals, superlatives | mid | *Have you **ever** been?* |

**Mid-position is one rule with three cases.** After *be* when *be* is the only verb (*She **is always** late*); after the first auxiliary (*She **has always** been late*); before a one-word main verb (*She **always** arrives late*). Getting this wrong produces the single most recognizable learner sentence in English: ✗ *She is late always*, ✗ *I go always by bus*.

Three more constraints. **The negative-in-sense adverbs cannot move to the front without inversion** — *Rarely **have I seen** anything like it* is correct and formal, ✗ *Rarely I have seen* is not. **Never is already a negative**, so ✗ *I don't never go* is a double negative and nonstandard. And **always in the continuous is a complaint, not a frequency** — *He's always losing his keys* means it is irritating, not that he loses them 100% of the time.

### Definite frequency

These are noun phrases, not adverbs, and they go at the **end** of the clause, or at the front for emphasis. They never take mid-position: ✗ *I every day go*.

| Expression | Means | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *once / twice / three times* + *a day, a week, a month, a year* | a count per period | ✗ *two times a week* is childish English; say *twice* |
| *every day, every week, every year* | each one without a gap | *everyday* as one word is an adjective: *everyday problems* |
| *every other day, every other week* | alternating; skipping one each time | American; British also says *every second day* |
| *every two days, every few minutes* | at that interval | — |
| *daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly, annually* | the adjective-adverb pair | *a daily paper* &middot; *it updates daily* |
| *hourly, nightly* | — | — |
| *biweekly, bimonthly* | **ambiguous** | can mean twice a week or every two weeks; write *twice a week* or *every two weeks* |
| *semiweekly, semimonthly, semiannually* | twice per period | unambiguous, which is why careful writing prefers them |
| *biannual* vs. *biennial* | twice a year vs. every two years | the most reliably confused pair in business English |
| *per day, per week* (formal) | — | *twice per week* is technical register |
| *on and off, off and on* | intermittently over a long stretch | informal |
| *round the clock, 24/7* | continuously | informal; *24/7* is American and now global |

## Phrasal verbs of time

**Separability, in one line.** *Sep* = the object can go in either slot, and a **pronoun must** go in the middle: *put the meeting off* &middot; *put off the meeting* &middot; *put it off*, never ✗ *put off it*. *Insep* = the parts never split: *get over it*, never ✗ *get it over*. *Intr* = no object at all.

| Phrasal verb | Means | Type | Example |

**Daylight saving time** starts the second Sunday in March, when clocks jump 2:00 a.m. to 3:00 a.m. — *spring forward* — and ends the first Sunday in November, when they fall back. Arizona (except the Navajo Nation) and Hawaii do not observe it, and neither do the territories.

**Write ET, CT, MT, PT** when you are scheduling. *EST* is only correct from November to March; using it in July is the most common time-zone error in American email, and *ET* is right all year. *9 a.m. PT / noon ET* is the standard way to write a cross-country meeting time.

## Quick reference

**Five rules that fix most of the errors above.**

| Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| A determiner in the phrase means no preposition | *last week, next Friday, this morning, every day* | ✗ *on last week* |
| *For* takes a length, *since* takes a point | *for three years* &middot; *since 2022* | ✗ *since three years* |
| *By* is a deadline, *until* is a stretch | *finish **by** six* &middot; *stay **until** six* | ✗ *finish until six* |
| Age is *be*, not *have* | *I'm thirty.* | ✗ *I have thirty years.* |
| Frequency adverbs sit in mid-position | *She **is always** late.* &middot; *I **always** walk.* | ✗ *She is late always.* |

```
  at  ->  a POINT      at 6:15, at noon, at night, at seventeen
  on  ->  a DAY        on Monday, on July 4, on my birthday
  in  ->  a CONTAINER  in June, in 2019, in the morning, in an hour
  --  ->  a DETERMINER last week, next Friday, this morning, every day
```

## Answers

### A. Write the time in words

1. **seven o'clock** &middot; *seven* — *o'clock* attaches only to a whole hour, and the bare number is just as normal.
2. **nine fifteen** &middot; **a quarter after nine** — American prefers *after*; *a quarter past nine* is also standard.
3. **two thirty** &middot; **half past two** — ✗ *half two* is British and means 2:30 there, nothing here.
4. **five forty-five** &middot; **a quarter to six** — past the half hour you count to the next hour; *a quarter of six* is the Northeastern variant.
5. **eleven oh five** &middot; **five after eleven** — the zero is said as the letter *oh*, never *zero*.
6. **four fifty** &middot; **ten to five** — ✗ *fifty after four* does not exist.
7. **noon** &middot; *twelve noon* — write *noon*, because 12:00 p.m. confuses half the people who read it.
8. **midnight** &middot; *twelve midnight* — the same reason in reverse: 12:00 a.m. is midnight.
9. **six forty** &middot; **twenty to seven** — also *twenty of seven*, *twenty till seven*, both regional and both standard.
10. **ten twenty-five** &middot; **twenty-five after ten** — the last slot that still counts forward from the hour.
11. **one thirty-five** &middot; **twenty-five to two** — five minutes past the half hour, so the direction has already flipped.
12. **eight ten** &middot; **ten after eight** — still counting forward, since 8:10 is well short of the half hour.
13. **three fifty-five** &middot; **five to four** — also *five of four*, *five till four*.
14. **two twenty in the afternoon** &middot; *2:20 p.m.* — 14:20 minus 12 is 2:20; Americans do not say *fourteen twenty* outside the military and hospitals.
15. **twelve forty-five a.m.** &middot; **a quarter to one in the morning** — the hour after midnight is the 12 a.m. hour, not a thirteenth or a twenty-fourth, which is why 00:45 comes out as *twelve*, not *zero*.

### B. Say the date aloud

1. **July fourth, seventeen seventy-six** — speech uses the ordinal even though the writing shows *4*.
2. **September eleventh, two thousand one** — the event itself is *nine eleven*, which is the one date Americans say as digits.
3. **January first, two thousand** — ✗ *twenty hundred* is not a possible reading of 2000.
4. **nineteen oh five** — the same *oh* as in clock times.
5. **the nineteen eighties** &middot; **the eighties** — no apostrophe before the *s*; *the '80s* puts one where *19* was removed.
6. **twenty twenty-six** — the two-pair reading came back for years from 2010 onward.
7. **the nineteenth century** — that is 1801 to 1900, one ahead of its digits; *the eighteen hundreds* means the same span.
8. **forty-four BC** and **AD ten sixty-six** — BC follows the year, AD precedes it; *44 BCE* and *1066 CE* both follow.
9. **March eighth, twenty twenty-six** — but a British reader sees *the third of August, twenty twenty-six*, because the British numeral order is day-month-year. This is exactly why a date that crosses a border spells the month out.
10. **circa fourteen fifty** &middot; *about fourteen fifty* — *c.* and *ca.* are the same abbreviation.

### C. Choose the preposition, or none

1. **at** — a clock time is a point.
2. **on** — days of the week take *on*.
3. **in** — months are containers.
4. **—** — *next* is a determiner, and a determiner blocks the preposition.
5. **at** — *night* is the one part of the day that takes *at*.
6. **in** — years take *in*.
7. **in** — *morning, afternoon, evening* take *in*; only *night* breaks the pattern.
8. **at** — *midnight*, like *noon* and *dawn*, is a point.
9. **on** — American *on the weekend*; British *at the weekend*.
10. **in** — decades take *in*, like years and centuries.
11. **—** — *every* is a determiner.
12. **on** — a named single day, so *on*, exactly like a date.
13. **at** — age takes *at*: *at sixty-two*, *at the age of sixty-two*.
14. **in** — a length counted forward from now.
15. **in** — seasons take *in*, with or without *the*.
16. **on** — a day plus a part of it stays with the day's preposition.
17. **At** — *at the moment*, *at present*, *at once* are fixed.
18. **at** — *at the end of* is a point; *in the end* would mean "finally."
19. **at** — a mealtime treated as a point. *Over lunch* is also idiomatic.
20. **on** — *the night of the twelfth* is anchored to a date, so it follows the date's preposition, not *night*'s.

### D. Choose between the confusable pairs

1. **by** — a deadline for a single action.
2. **until** — a state continuing up to a point.
3. **until** — waiting is continuous, so it takes *until*.
4. **By** — *by the time* is the fixed conjunction; ✗ *until the time* is not English.
5. **since** — 2019 is a starting point.
6. **for** — seven years is a length.
7. **since** — *since* can take a whole clause naming the starting event.
8. **for** — *ages* is a length, however vague.
9. **on time** — punctual, at the scheduled moment.
10. **in time** — early enough for something, with margin to spare.
11. **in time** — *in time for X* is the fixed frame; *on time* would need a schedule the package is on.
12. **on time** — punctuality again, and *for meetings* does not change that.
13. **In the end** — an outcome after deliberation; swap in *eventually* and it still works.
14. **at the end** — there is an *of*, so it is a position inside a thing.
15. **At the end** — also an *of*, and here it is literal: the final days of each month.
16. **in the end** — the outcome of the argument, not a position in it.
17. **yet** — a question about something that has not happened.
18. **already** — affirmative, and sooner than the other person expected.
19. **still** — the rain was happening and is continuing.
20. **yet** — a negative about something that has not happened.

### E. Pick the right phrasal verb

1. **dragged on** — it continued too long, which is the whole meaning of *drag on*.
2. **push back** — later. The unready demo is the reason, so the date moves away from you.
3. **moved up** — earlier. This is the American trap: *up* means sooner, not later.
4. **called off** — cancelled entirely, not merely postponed.
5. **wrap up** — finish neatly, on purpose, before a limit.
6. **slept in** — deliberately sleeping late; *overslept* would mean you missed something.
7. **stayed up** — did not go to bed.
8. **showed up** — arrived, with a hint that more were expected.
9. **catch up on** — get back to the level you should already be at; *on* is required before the thing.
10. **ran out of** — had none left. *Run out* takes *of* before its object.

### F. Complete the idiom

1. **hour** — *at the eleventh hour*, the last possible moment before a deadline.
2. **nick** — *in the nick of time*, with almost no margin at all.
3. **moon** — *once in a blue moon*, very rarely.
4. **call** — *call it a day*, stop working for now; *call it a night* after dark.
5. **clock** — *around the clock*, continuously through day and night.
6. **second** &middot; *sec* &middot; *minute* — *give me a second* means very soon, not one second.
7. **time** — *it's high time* + a past-tense verb: *it's high time you learned*.
8. **time** — *ahead of her time*, too advanced for the era.
9. **oil** — *burning the midnight oil*, working very late.
10. **For** — *for the time being*, temporarily, until something changes.

### G. Fix the error

1. **I'm twenty-five.** &middot; *I'm twenty-five years old.* — English states age with *be*, not *have*; Russian *мне двадцать пять лет* has no verb to copy.
2. **Let's meet at five thirty** &middot; *at half past five*. — *Half six* means 6:30 in Britain and is not said in America; Russian *полшестого* means 5:30, so the calque lands an hour off.
3. **See you Monday.** &middot; *See you on Monday.* — Days take *on*, never *in*, and American speech often drops it entirely.
4. **I'll be back in an hour.** — *In* counts forward from now; *after an hour* belongs inside a past narrative.
5. **I've lived here since 2020.** — An open stretch takes the present perfect, and *since* takes a point.
6. **We talked for two hours.** — *During* takes a named event; a measured length takes *for*.
7. **Let's meet next Friday.** — *Next* is a determiner, so no preposition and no article.
8. **In the end, we took the train instead.** — The sentence reports an outcome. *At the end* needs an *of*-phrase.
9. **The conference is on May 15.** — Dates take *on*, and American order puts the month first.
10. **I'm ten minutes late.** — The amount goes in front of *late*, bare; *for ten minutes* would mean the lateness lasted that long.
11. **He comes here twice a week.** — *Twice*, not *two times*, and *a week*, not *in a week*.
12. **Please send me the file by Friday.** — Sending is one action against a deadline, so *by*, not *till*.
13. **Call me on Saturday evening.** — A day plus a part of it keeps the day's preposition, and there is only one.
14. **Last year we were in Boston.** — *Last* is a determiner, so no *in* and no *the*. *In the last year* exists but means "during the twelve months just past."
15. **In the morning I drink coffee, and at night I read.** — *Morning* takes *in the*, and *night* is the single part of the day that takes *at* with no article.
