Prepositions of time#

Every word that puts an event on the clock or the calendar — the at / on / in ladder, the phrases that take no preposition at all, and the one test that separates by from until.

← Time · All grammar topics

English has about sixty prepositions that place something in time, and the standard advice — memorize at for clock times, on for days, in for months — covers maybe a fifth of them. It is not wrong. It is just the smallest part of the problem, and it is the part that fixes itself after a few weeks of reading.

What keeps going wrong is everything on either side of that rule. A learner who has drilled at six, on Monday, in March still writes ✗ in last week, because nothing in the drill said that last cancels the preposition outright. The same learner writes ✗ during three hours for for three hours, ✗ since three years for for three years, and — the expensive one — Finish it until Friday when the sentence needed by Friday.

So this section is built as a decision procedure rather than a list. Four questions, asked in order, and the first one that applies decides:

   1. Does it start with last / next / this / that / every / each / all /
      some / one — or is it yesterday, today, tomorrow, tonight?
      YES ──►  NO PREPOSITION.  last week, next Monday, every day. Stop.

   2. Am I naming WHEN — a clock point, a calendar square, a stretch?
      YES ──►  at 6:15 (point) · on Tuesday (day) · in March (period)

   3. Am I measuring HOW LONG, or naming the period it sits inside?
      YES ──►  for / since · during / over / throughout · within / in

   4. Am I naming a BOUNDARY — a last acceptable moment, or a stopping point?
      YES ──►  by (one act) vs. until (a continuing state)

Everything below is one of those four questions worked out in detail, plus the smaller families — sequence, approximation, frequency, and the multi-word phrases — that hang off them.

The prepositions themselves — pronunciation, register, what complement each one takes, Russian glosses, and a dozen examples apiece — are in the dictionary: 1,297 prepositions of time, with at, on and in in the core three. This section is about choosing between them.

at / on / in: the size hierarchy#

The principle#

Points get at. Days get on. Containers get in. Zoom out from a clock time to a day to a month and the preposition climbs the ladder with you; zoom back in and it climbs down.

   in    2027        a year          ── the widest container
   in    March       a month
   on    Tuesday     a day           ── the calendar square
   on    Tuesday morning              ── still a day: the date outranks the part
   at    6:15        a clock time    ── the point

That is the whole rule, and it predicts the right answer perhaps nine times in ten. The remaining tenth is a short list of fixed phrases that have to be memorized, and they are below.

One thing the ladder does not do is tell you the preposition from the size of the thing. It tells you the preposition from the size of the unit you named. The same Tuesday is on Tuesday, at 3 p.m. on Tuesday, and in the first week of the month — three prepositions for one afternoon, because three different units got named.

The fixed phrases that break the pattern#

There are eleven of them, and between them they account for most of the errors that survive into advanced English.

CaseWhat to writeWhy it breaks the ladder
at night vs. in the morning / afternoon / eveningI work at night. / I work in the morning.The one genuine hole. Night is treated as a point, the other three as containers, and no principle explains it.
in the nightI woke up twice in the night.Not a variant of at night. With the, it means one particular night, and it is nearly always about something that interrupted sleep.

at the end vs. in the endA twist at the end of the movie. / In the end, we drove.At the end locates a moment inside a sequence; in the end means "finally, after all." The pairs line up: at first with at last, in the beginning with in the end.

Two more that are simply unanalyzable and are learned whole: at once, at last, at the last minute, on the dot, on schedule, on arrival, in a minute, in the nick of time, in advance.

And one American habit worth knowing before you hear it: the on can be dropped in front of a day. I'll call you Monday. We're closed Sundays. The game's Saturday. This is standard in American speech and informal writing and impossible in British English. Keep the on in formal prose.

The zero preposition#

The rule nobody tells you#

This is the single most useful rule on the page, and most textbooks bury it in a footnote.

A time phrase that begins with last, next, this, that, every, each, some, any, all, one, or another takes no preposition at all. Neither do yesterday, today, tomorrow, and tonight. The determiner does the locating, and English refuses to locate the same phrase twice.

I saw her last week. — ✗ in last week We're leaving next Monday. — ✗ on next Monday This morning was a disaster. — ✗ in this morning He calls every Sunday. — ✗ on every Sunday It rained all day. — ✗ during all day Yesterday I forgot my badge. — ✗ at yesterday The results come out tomorrow. — ✗ on tomorrow Let's do it some other time. — ✗ at some other time (grammatical, but heavier than anyone needs)

There is no way to argue with this one. ✗ in last week is not a stylistic slip; it is the mark of a learner, and it is completely invisible to the person making it, because the Russian, the Polish, the Spanish and the French all put a preposition there.

What does not block the preposition#

The blockers are that closed list. Put a definite article in front of the same noun and the preposition comes straight back, because the makes the phrase a period rather than a pointer:

Zero prepositionPreposition requiredDifference
last weekin the last week / over the past weekLast week is the calendar week before this one. In the last week is the rolling seven days just gone.
next monthin the next monthNext month is a named month. In the next month is the thirty days ahead.
last yearin the last year / over the last yearSame split: the calendar year, versus the twelve months just finished.
this weekduring the weekDuring the week in American usage often means "on weekdays," as opposed to the weekend.
every Fridayon FridaysTwo ways to say the same habit.
one morningon the morning of the 12thA date brings on back with it.

A second, narrower exception: nouns that name a position in a sequence rather than a measure of time keep at, even after this or that. Moment, point, stage, time and juncture behave this way.

At this point we may as well wait for the next one. At that moment the lights came back on. The project was canceled at that stage. At the time, nobody thought it was strange.

The test is whether the noun could be replaced by a stretch of calendar. This week can (a week is seven days). This point cannot, because a point in a process is not a length of anything — so the preposition survives.

The mirror-image error#

The block is so counterintuitive that some learners overcorrect and start dropping prepositions where English wants them. The rule cuts one way only: a determiner from the list above blocks the preposition, and nothing else does.

✗ We got back the Friday. → We got back on Friday. ✗ The meeting is 1 p.m. → The meeting is at 1 p.m. ✗ He was born 1994. → He was born in 1994.

Duration: how long, and what it sits inside#

Six prepositions share this ground, and they do not overlap as much as they look. Three of them take a measured length (for, within, in), two take a named period (during, throughout), one takes a starting point (since), and one does both a period and a span (over). Feeding the wrong kind of object to one of them is the commonest error in the whole family.

for, since, and ago#

These three answer three different questions, and each takes a different kind of object.

WordObjectQuestion answeredTense it lives in
fora lengthHow long?any — I lived there for six years / I've lived here for six years
sincea pointStarting when?perfect only — I've lived here since 2019
agoa lengthHow long back?simple past — I moved here six years ago

I've worked here for three years. — ✗ since three years I've worked here since 2022. — ✗ for 2022 I started here three years ago. — ✗ before three years ✗ I am here since Monday. → I've been here since Monday.

Two structural notes. Ago is a postposition — it follows its object instead of preceding it, which makes it the only word in this section that does (two weeks ago, never ✗ ago two weeks). And American English drops for freely in front of a plain measure: I've lived here three years is completely ordinary, where ✗ I've lived here since three years never is.

The tense side of this — why since forces a perfect and ago forbids one — is in The present perfect; ago against adverbial before and beforehand is in Adverbs and sequence.

during versus for#

During names the period. For measures it. During answers when, not how long, which is why a number after it is almost always an error.

✗ We talked during three hours. → We talked for three hours. We talked during the flight. — inside that period, not necessarily the whole of it He fell asleep during the second half. Nothing was decided during the meeting.

During also refuses a gerund: ✗ during eating is not English. The repairs are while eating or in the middle of eating.

One overlap is real: with a named period that also has a length, both work and mean different things. I read it during the flight says the reading happened somewhere inside the flight; I read it for the whole flight says it filled the flight.

over and throughout#

Over spans a period and usually implies that something developed across it or happened repeatedly inside it. Throughout insists on the entire extent with no gaps, and is the emphatic version of during.

We'll be out of town over the weekend. Rates have fallen over the past decade. We talked about it over dinner. — during the meal, and the meal is the occasion The store stays open throughout the holidays. She was in pain throughout the flight. — all of it, continuously

Over the weekend is the American phrase; over Christmas, over the summer, and over the years work the same way. Note that over is the one word here that also takes a bare meal (over coffee, over lunch), where during lunch is possible but colder.

in, within, and inside of#

This is the trio that costs money. All three attach to a measured length, and they mean three different things about the end of it.

PhraseMeansPicture
in an hourat the end of the hour — sixty minutes from nowone point, at the far edge
within an hourat any time before the hour is upthe whole interval, with a hard edge
inside of an hourthe same as within, informal Americanthe whole interval

I'll be there in an hour. — around 4:00 if it's 3:00 now I'll be there within an hour. — any time between now and 4:00 They had the power back on inside of twenty minutes. Payment is due within thirty days of the invoice date. — a contract would never write in thirty days here

Casual American speech lets in do both jobs, and nobody misunderstands I'll call you in five minutes. Formal writing keeps them apart, and for a deadline within is the unambiguous word.

In + length also has a second, unrelated life: after a negative or a superlative it means "for," and it is the American default — I haven't been back in years, the worst storm in a decade.

from … to and between … and#

Two frames, both taking a pair of endpoints.

The office is open from nine to five. The road was closed from Tuesday until Thursday. He was governor from 1998 to 2006. The store is closed between one and two. Somebody called between eight and nine.

The difference: from … to names a run, and something continues across it. Between … and names a window, and something happens at an unspecified point inside it. We drove from noon to six is six hours of driving; We left between noon and six is one departure, time unknown.

And from … to has the American inclusive problem built into it — see through below.

Deadlines and boundaries: by versus until#

This is the most useful pair in the section, and the one where a mistake changes what somebody is expected to do.

The two meanings#

by = the last acceptable moment for a single act. It can happen at any point up to then. until = the point where a continuing state or activity stops. It goes on the whole time up to then.

Finish the report by Friday. — any time before Friday is fine; Friday is the outside limit Stay until Friday. — remain continuously, and leave on Friday

The test#

Look at the verb. A verb that names one completed act — finish, submit, send, pay, arrive, call, decide — takes by. A verb that names a state or an activity stretching out in time — wait, stay, keep, sleep, work, live, be open — takes until.

✗ Finish it until Friday. → Finish it by Friday. ✗ Wait by Friday. → Wait until Friday.

A second test, if the verb is ambiguous: try inserting the paraphrase. By takes "at any time before," and until takes "continuously up to."

SentenceParaphrase that fitsVerdict
Get the numbers to me ___ Thursday.at any time before Thursdayby
The pool is open ___ nine.continuously up to nineuntil
I need an answer ___ noon.at any time before noonby
We're staying ___ Sunday.continuously up to Sundayuntil
He should be home ___ six.at any time before sixby
He'll be home ___ six.continuously up to six — then he leavesuntil

That last pair is worth staring at. He should be home by six means he will arrive no later than six. He'll be home until six means he is there now and leaves at six. One word, two opposite schedules.

More pairs#

Pay the balance by the 15th. / The offer stands until the 15th. Turn it in by Monday. / Don't turn it in until Monday. The lock changes by noon. / The old key works until noon. We should hear by now. / Nothing up until now has worked.

not … until#

The negative frame is where until does something by cannot: it names the point at which something finally happens.

He didn't call until Tuesday. — the call happened on Tuesday She didn't find out until the movers were loading the truck. Nothing was decided until well after midnight.

Fronted for emphasis, it inverts the main clause: Not until Tuesday did he call. That inversion belongs to the same family as fronted only if and rarely; it is worked through in Inversion and formal conditionals.

by the time#

By the time is a complex preposition that takes a clause, and it is the only member of this family that does. Its clause obeys the no-will rule that governs every time clause in English.

By the time we got there, everyone had already eaten. By the time you read this, I'll be in Denver. — ✗ by the time you will read this By the time the permit clears, it'll be October.

Note the tense pairing: a past by the time clause usually pulls a past perfect into the main clause, because the main event was finished first. The rule about will is in The present simple, and the conjunction side of until, before, after and by the time is in 70 subordinators of time.

The inclusion problem is real and it is American English's one genuine contribution here. Open until Friday does not tell a reader whether Friday counts; before Friday clearly excludes it; by Friday leaves it open; through Friday settles it. British English has no through solution, which is why British schedules say Monday to Friday inclusive.

We're open Monday through Friday. — five days The exhibit runs through September. — September included The sale ends Friday. — spoken American, and still ambiguous; a careful writer says through Friday or at midnight Thursday

Sequence: what comes before and what comes after#

The neutral pair#

Before and after order two events on a line and carry nothing else — no cause, no judgment, no claim about the size of the gap.

The pharmacy closes after nine. Sign the lease before the walk-through. Read the whole contract before signing anything. — both take a gerund She quit two weeks before the merger was announced.

Two contrasts do most of the work. Before excludes, by includes: a deadline written before Friday is a day tighter than most writers intend. And before is not ago: ago counts backward from now and takes the simple past (three days ago), while before counts backward from a point already in the past and goes with the past perfect (he had left three days before).

The heavy register#

Every one of these means before or after in a suit. None of them is clearer, and an American editor cuts most of them on sight — but they are everywhere in legal, corporate and journalistic prose, so they have to be readable.

Prior to the hearing, both sides exchanged exhibit lists. Ahead of Tuesday's vote, three members changed their positions. Following the merger, the Dallas office closed. The permit is suspended pending the outcome of the appeal.

Note the complement: prior to, subsequent to, ahead of, in advance of, following and upon all take a gerund as happily as a noun — prior to filing, upon signing, ahead of announcing the cuts. Pending does not.

past#

Past puts a time on the far side of a line, and the line is the whole point. After midnight just names a later time; past midnight says a boundary was crossed, and usually that somebody should not have been up.

It's past midnight. The kids were up way past their bedtime. The milk is a week past its expiration date. By then she was past caring what anyone thought. It's twenty past four. — the clock sense, alive and well in American speech

That evaluative edge is in past and not in after, which is why past the deadline is a failure and after the deadline is a date.

As of deserves its own line, because American business English runs on it. It means "starting from, and still true," and it works forward (The policy takes effect as of July 1) and backward (As of this morning, we had eleven confirmed). Its British twins are as from and with effect from, which read as imported in American copy; the American alternative is effective — effective Monday, effective immediately.

Approximation: roughly when#

Six ways to hedge a time, and in American English two of them cover nearly everything. (For a date rather than a clock time there is a seventh, the historian's circa — a farmhouse built circa 1890.)

WordMeansRegisterExample

Three notes. Around and about are interchangeable in American English for time, and around is the more distinctly American of the two; British round about six is not written here. Toward is directional — it says the time is being approached, so toward evening works and ✗ toward six o'clock sharp does not. And American English writes toward, not towards, along with among over amongst and while over whilst.

For legal hedging there are two fixed phrases — on or about (a date: on or about March 3) and in or about (a month or a year) — which exist precisely so that a document can be wrong about the date without being false.

Frequency: how often#

Four patterns, and the first one catches learners because the preposition is an article.

PatternFormExample
a / an[count] + a / an + [singular unit]twice a week · three times a day · $20 an hour
perper + bare singular unit40 hours per week · twice per day
every / eachno preposition at allevery day · every other Tuesday · every three hours
on + plural dayhabitualon Mondays · on weekends

She goes twice a week. The medication is one tablet a day, or four a day at the start. The lease allows two guests per night. The bus runs every fifteen minutes. I work on Saturdays. — or, in American speech, I work Saturdays.

A and per mean the same thing and differ only in register. Twice a week is what people say; twice per week is what a lab report or a rate card says, and it sounds stiff in conversation. Neither one takes an extra article: ✗ per a week, ✗ twice the week.

Every is not a preposition and never takes one — that is the zero-preposition rule again. It also does something a and per cannot: it counts in multiples (every three hours, every other week, every fourth Tuesday), where ✗ three hours a time is not English.

The one-word frequency adverbs — always, usually, often, rarely, never — are a different system with a different position rule, and they belong to Duration and frequency and Adverbs and sequence.

Two of these hide a trap.

In the course of is not a synonym for during in every position: it prefers a period with internal development (in the course of the investigation), and it sounds wrong pinned to a short fixed event (✗ in the course of the toast).

For Russian speakers#

Five interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

1. Russian «в» splits three ways, and nothing marks the split#

One Russian preposition covers the whole at / on / in ladder, so the choice English forces is invisible from the Russian side. The good news is that the ladder is mechanical once you see it; the bad news is that «в» gives you no clue which rung you are on.

RussianEnglishRung
в пять часовat five o'clockpoint
в полдень / в полночьat noon / at midnightpoint
в понедельникon Mondayday
в мой день рожденияon my birthdayday

в 2019 годуin 2019container
в двадцатом векеin the twentieth centurycontainer
ночьюat nightthe one hole

в выходныеon the weekend (American)day
в конце месяцаat the end of the monthedge
в то времяat that timepoint in a sequence

✗ in Monday → on Monday ✗ at the morning → in the morning ✗ in night → at night ✗ in the weekend → on the weekend

2. «Через час» is in an hour, and never ✗ through an hour#

«Через» is a false friend in two directions at once. The preposition that translates it is in, and the two words that look like it — through and after — are both wrong for the "from now" meaning.

Через час → I'll be there in an hour. — ✗ through an hour, ✗ after an hour Через два дня → The results come in two days. Через неделю после операции → A week after the surgery, she went back to work.

The honest complication: after is correct when the reference point is in the past, not now. After an hour, he gave up is fine, because the hour is measured from a point in the story. ✗ After an hour I'll call you is not, because the hour is measured from now, and that is in.

And «через каждые два дня» is every other day or every two days — with no preposition at all.

3. «На прошлой неделе» loses its preposition entirely#

Russian puts a preposition on nearly every time phrase. English deletes it in front of the whole last / next / this / every family, and that deletion is the single most reliable giveaway of a Russian-speaking writer.

RussianEnglishNot
на прошлой неделеlast week✗ in last week, ✗ on last week
в прошлом годуlast year✗ in last year
в следующий понедельникnext Monday✗ on next Monday
на этой неделеthis week✗ in this week
сегодня утромthis morning✗ in this morning
вчераyesterday✗ at yesterday
завтраtomorrow✗ on tomorrow
каждый деньevery day✗ in every day
весь деньall day✗ during all day
на следующий деньthe next day✗ on the next day in narrative

The one free ride in the whole section: «каждый день» takes no preposition in Russian either, so every day transfers without thinking about it. Use it as the anchor and extend the feeling to the rest of the list.

4. «До пяти» is two different English sentences#

Russian «до» covers both the deadline and the duration, and Russian has no way to mark which one is meant — the verb does it, or nothing does. English has two words and forces the choice.

RussianMeaningEnglish
Закончи до пяти.one act, deadlineFinish it by five.
Жди до пяти.continuing stateWait until five.
Магазин работает до девяти.continuing stateThe store is open until nine.
Сдай отчёт до пятницы.one act, deadlineTurn in the report by Friday.
Я буду дома до шести.continuing — I'm there, then I leaveI'll be home until six.
Я буду дома к шести.deadline — I arriveI'll be home by six.

Russian «к» maps cleanly onto by, and that is the pair to memorize: «к» → by, «до» → until or by depending on the verb. The test is the one above — one completed act takes by, a continuing state takes until.

5. «За два дня» and «два дня назад» are not the same shape in English#

Three Russian phrases that all involve a measured length, and three different English structures.

RussianEnglishStructure
два дня назадtwo days agolength + postposition, simple past
за два дня (до)two days beforelength + before + past perfect
за два дня (успеть)in two dayscompletion inside a span
в течение двух днейfor two days (duration) · within two days (deadline) · during those two days (when)three different words
на две неделиfor two weeks✗ on two weeks
с 2019 годаsince 2019requires a perfect tense
по пятницамon Fridayshabitual plural
раз в неделюonce a weekthe article is the preposition

«В течение» is the worst of these, because a single Russian phrase splits three ways and the split is exactly the for / within / during distinction English cares most about.

«Мы говорили в течение трёх часов» → We talked for three hours. — ✗ during three hours «Ответьте в течение трёх дней» → Reply within three days. «В течение встречи никто не возражал» → During the meeting, nobody objected.

What transfers for free. Russian «около пяти» is around five with no surprises, «после обеда» is after lunch, and «перед встречей» is before the meeting. The sequence prepositions are the easy half of this section for a Russian speaker; the trouble is concentrated in the zero-preposition family, in «до», and in «в течение».

✗ I saw her in last week.I saw her last week.Last blocks the preposition outright.
✗ We're leaving on next Monday.We're leaving next Monday.So does next.
✗ At yesterday I forgot my badge.Yesterday I forgot my badge.Yesterday, today, tomorrow and tonight never take one.
✗ I work in the night.I work at night.At night is the fixed exception; in the night means one particular night.

✗ What are you doing at the weekend?What are you doing on the weekend?At the weekend is British.
✗ We talked during three hours.We talked for three hours.During names a period; only for measures one.
✗ I've lived here since three years.I've lived here for three years.Since takes a starting point, never a length.

Quick reference#

The ladder, compressed.

   at    a point      at 6:15 · at noon · at night · at Christmas · at the age of ten
   on    a day        on Tuesday · on July 4 · on Tuesday morning · on the weekend
   in    a container  in March · in 1998 · in the morning · in an hour · in her thirties
   —     blocked      last week · next Monday · this morning · every day · all night
                      yesterday · today · tomorrow · tonight

The five contrasts to memorize.

Finish it by Friday. ≠ Stay until Friday. — a deadline, versus a duration. I'll be there in an hour. ≠ I'll be there within an hour. — at the end of it, versus anywhere inside it. We talked for three hours. ≠ We talked during the flight. — how long, versus when. at Christmas ≠ on Christmas Day — the season, versus the dated day. We're open through Friday. ≠ We're open before Friday. — Friday included, versus Friday excluded.

What never takes a length of time
since, during, until, by, no later than. All five take a point.
What never takes a gerund
during, until, pending, no later than.
What takes a gerund freely
before, after, since, upon, prior to, subsequent to, ahead of, in advance of, at the time of, in the course of, in the middle of.
What follows its object instead of preceding it
ago, and hence in formal writing (three days hence).
British forms to recognize but not to write
at the weekend → on the weekend · Monday to Friday (inclusive) → Monday through Friday · in future → in the future · as from July 1 → as of July 1 · with effect from → effective · towards → toward · a fortnight → two weeks.

Practice#

Fill in the preposition — or write — if the phrase takes none.

  1. She's been with the company ___ 2018.
  2. We waited ___ two hours and then gave up.
  3. I'll see you ___ next Thursday.
  4. The package should get there ___ Friday at the latest.
  5. Don't open it ___ I get there.
  6. He works ___ night and sleeps ___ the morning.
  7. We're closed ___ Sundays.
  8. The exhibit runs ___ September 30. (Say whether the 30th is included.)
  9. Reply ___ ten business days. (It may be sent any time in that window.)
  10. I'll be ready ___ five minutes.
  11. ___ Christmas Day the whole street is quiet.
  12. They moved to Tulsa three years ___.
  13. He called ___ the middle of dinner.
  14. The policy takes effect ___ January 1.
  15. Take one tablet twice ___ day.
  16. It was ___ midnight when they finally got in.
  17. ___ the time we got there, everyone had left.
  18. Fix each error: ✗ I am working here since two years. ✗ Please finish until Monday. ✗ We met in last summer. ✗ He'll call you after an hour.

Answers#

#AnswerWhy
1She's been with the company since 2018.A starting point, with a perfect tense.
2We waited for two hours.A measured length — ✗ during two hours.
3I'll see you — next Thursday.Next blocks the preposition.
4The package should get there by Friday.A single completed act, with a deadline.
5Don't open it until I get there.A continuing state stopping at a point — and here until is a conjunction.
6He works at night and sleeps in the morning.The one hole in the ladder, next to the rule it breaks.
7We're closed on Sundays.Habitual plural day. In American speech, We're closed Sundays.
8The exhibit runs through September 30 — the 30th is included.The American inclusive endpoint. Until would leave it open.
9Reply within ten business days.Any point before the ten days are up. In ten days would mean on the tenth.
10I'll be ready in five minutes.At the end of the interval — measured from now.
11On Christmas Day the whole street is quiet.The dated day takes on; the season takes at.
12They moved to Tulsa three years ago.Ago follows its object, with the simple past.
13He called in the middle of dinner.The multi-word preposition; during dinner also works.
14The policy takes effect as of January 1.Or effective January 1. Not as from, which is British.
15Take one tablet twice a day.The article is the rate marker. Per day is the clinical register.
16It was past midnight when they finally got in.A boundary crossed, not merely a later time.
17By the time we got there, everyone had left.By the time + clause, with a past perfect in the main clause.
18I've been working here for two years. · Please finish by Monday. · We met — last summer. · He'll call you in an hour.Perfect tense with for + length; deadline takes by; last blocks; «через» measured from now is in.

One more pass#

Say what changes in each pair, in one sentence:

  1. I'll be home by six. / I'll be home until six.
  2. She read it during the flight. / She read it for the whole flight.
  3. The sale ends Friday. / The sale runs through Friday.
  4. I haven't seen him in years. / I didn't see him for years.

Answers: (1) arrival versus departure — by means he gets there no later than six, until means he is there now and leaves at six. (2) somewhere inside it, versus filling it. (3) ambiguous, versus Friday explicitly included. (4) the American present-perfect idiom about now, versus a finished stretch of past time.
