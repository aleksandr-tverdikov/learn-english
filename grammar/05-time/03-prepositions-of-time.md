# Prepositions of time

> Every word that puts an event on the clock or the calendar — the *at / on / in* ladder, the phrases that take no preposition at all, and the one test that separates *by* from *until*.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

English has about sixty prepositions that place something in time, and the standard advice — memorize *at* for clock times, *on* for days, *in* for months — covers maybe a fifth of them. It is not wrong. It is just the smallest part of the problem, and it is the part that fixes itself after a few weeks of reading.

What keeps going wrong is everything on either side of that rule. A learner who has drilled *at six, on Monday, in March* still writes ✗ *in last week*, because nothing in the drill said that *last* cancels the preposition outright. The same learner writes ✗ *during three hours* for *for three hours*, ✗ *since three years* for *for three years*, and — the expensive one — *Finish it until Friday* when the sentence needed *by Friday*.

So this section is built as a **decision procedure** rather than a list. Four questions, asked in order, and the first one that applies decides:

```
   1. Does it start with last / next / this / that / every / each / all /
      some / one — or is it yesterday, today, tomorrow, tonight?
      YES ──►  NO PREPOSITION.  last week, next Monday, every day. Stop.

   2. Am I naming WHEN — a clock point, a calendar square, a stretch?
      YES ──►  at 6:15 (point) · on Tuesday (day) · in March (period)

   3. Am I measuring HOW LONG, or naming the period it sits inside?
      YES ──►  for / since · during / over / throughout · within / in

   4. Am I naming a BOUNDARY — a last acceptable moment, or a stopping point?
      YES ──►  by (one act) vs. until (a continuing state)
```

Everything below is one of those four questions worked out in detail, plus the smaller families — sequence, approximation, frequency, and the multi-word phrases — that hang off them.

The prepositions themselves — pronunciation, register, what complement each one takes, Russian glosses, and a dozen examples apiece — are in the dictionary: **[1,297 prepositions of time](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md)**, with *at*, *on* and *in* in **[the core three](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/01-core-in-on-at.md)**. This section is about choosing between them.

## *at* / *on* / *in*: the size hierarchy

### The principle

**Points get *at*. Days get *on*. Containers get *in*.** Zoom out from a clock time to a day to a month and the preposition climbs the ladder with you; zoom back in and it climbs down.

```
   in    2027        a year          ── the widest container
   in    March       a month
   on    Tuesday     a day           ── the calendar square
   on    Tuesday morning              ── still a day: the date outranks the part
   at    6:15        a clock time    ── the point
```

That is the whole rule, and it predicts the right answer perhaps nine times in ten. The remaining tenth is a short list of fixed phrases that have to be memorized, and they are below.

One thing the ladder does **not** do is tell you the preposition from the size of the *thing*. It tells you the preposition from the size of the **unit you named**. The same Tuesday is *on Tuesday*, *at 3 p.m. on Tuesday*, and *in the first week of the month* — three prepositions for one afternoon, because three different units got named.

### The full table

| | Takes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | clock times; precise moments; points in a process; mealtimes; ages; holidays treated as a season | *at 6:15* &middot; *at noon* &middot; *at midnight* &middot; *at dawn* &middot; *at sunset* &middot; *at that moment* &middot; *at the same time* &middot; *at present* &middot; *at breakfast* &middot; *at lunch* &middot; *at the start* &middot; *at the end* &middot; *at the age of ten* &middot; **at night** &middot; *at Christmas* &middot; *at Thanksgiving* |
| **on** | days; dates; named days; a day plus a part of the day; anniversaries; and (American) the weekend | *on Tuesday* &middot; *on July 4* &middot; *on Christmas Day* &middot; *on my birthday* &middot; *on New Year's Eve* &middot; *on Tuesday morning* &middot; *on the night of the 12th* &middot; *on the first of the month* &middot; **on the weekend** &middot; *on weekdays* &middot; *on arrival* |
| **in** | months; seasons; years; decades; centuries; long historical periods; the general parts of the day; future intervals | *in March* &middot; *in the fall* &middot; *in 1998* &middot; *in the '90s* &middot; *in the twentieth century* &middot; *in the Middle Ages* &middot; **in the morning / afternoon / evening** &middot; *in my twenties* &middot; *in an hour* &middot; *in the meantime* &middot; *in the past* &middot; *in the future* |

### The fixed phrases that break the pattern

There are eleven of them, and between them they account for most of the errors that survive into advanced English.

| Case | What to write | Why it breaks the ladder |
|---|---|---|
| ***at night*** vs. *in the morning / afternoon / evening* | *I work **at night**.* / *I work **in the morning**.* | The one genuine hole. *Night* is treated as a point, the other three as containers, and no principle explains it. |
| ***in the night*** | *I woke up twice **in the night**.* | Not a variant of *at night*. With *the*, it means one particular night, and it is nearly always about something that interrupted sleep. |
| ***on Tuesday morning*** | ✗ *in Tuesday morning* | A named day outranks the part of the day. Once a date is present, *on* wins: *on the night of the 12th*, *on Christmas morning*. |
| ***at Christmas*** vs. ***on Christmas Day*** | *We fly home **at Christmas**.* / *We open presents **on Christmas Day**.* | The holiday as a season is a point on the year's map; the single dated day is a day. Same for *at Easter* / *on Easter Sunday*. |
| ***on the weekend*** (American) | ✗ *at the weekend* | *At the weekend* is British and reads as foreign in American copy. Americans say *on the weekend*, *on weekends*, and *over the weekend*. |
| ***at the end*** vs. ***in the end*** | *A twist **at the end** of the movie.* / ***In the end**, we drove.* | *At the end* locates a moment inside a sequence; *in the end* means "finally, after all." The pairs line up: *at first* with *at last*, *in the beginning* with *in the end*. |
| ***in time*** vs. ***on time*** | *We got there **in time** for the toast.* / *The train left **on time**.* | *In time* is soon enough, with room to spare. *On time* is exactly at the scheduled moment. |
| ***in the future*** (American) | ✗ *in future* | Meaning "from now on," American English keeps the article. Article-less *in future* is British. |
| ***in years*** after a negative or superlative | *I haven't seen him **in years**.* &middot; *the coldest week **in a decade*** | The American default where British English says *for years*. Both varieties accept *for*; only American makes *in* the unmarked choice. |
| ***on Fridays*** vs. ***on Friday*** | *We're closed **on Mondays**.* / *We're closed **on Monday**.* | The plural is habitual, the singular is one occasion. And *every Friday* takes no preposition at all — see below. |
| ***at the age of ten*** vs. ***in his thirties*** | *She started **at the age of ten**.* / *He's **in his thirties**.* | A single age is a point; a decade of life is a container. |

Two more that are simply unanalyzable and are learned whole: *at once*, *at last*, *at the last minute*, *on the dot*, *on schedule*, *on arrival*, *in a minute*, *in the nick of time*, *in advance*.

And one American habit worth knowing before you hear it: **the *on* can be dropped in front of a day.** *I'll call you Monday. We're closed Sundays. The game's Saturday.* This is standard in American speech and informal writing and impossible in British English. Keep the *on* in formal prose.

The entries behind this table — every sense, with pronunciation and examples — are in [the core three](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/01-core-in-on-at.md), and the clock formats themselves (*ten after six*, *a quarter of three*, *a quarter till three*) are in [Telling the time](01-telling-the-time.md).

## The zero preposition

### The rule nobody tells you

This is the single most useful rule on the page, and most textbooks bury it in a footnote.

**A time phrase that begins with *last*, *next*, *this*, *that*, *every*, *each*, *some*, *any*, *all*, *one*, or *another* takes no preposition at all. Neither do *yesterday*, *today*, *tomorrow*, and *tonight*.** The determiner does the locating, and English refuses to locate the same phrase twice.

> I saw her **last week**. — ✗ *in last week*  
> We're leaving **next Monday**. — ✗ *on next Monday*  
> **This morning** was a disaster. — ✗ *in this morning*  
> He calls **every Sunday**. — ✗ *on every Sunday*  
> It rained **all day**. — ✗ *during all day*  
> **Yesterday** I forgot my badge. — ✗ *at yesterday*  
> The results come out **tomorrow**. — ✗ *on tomorrow*  
> Let's do it **some other time**. — ✗ *at some other time* (grammatical, but heavier than anyone needs)

There is no way to argue with this one. ✗ *in last week* is not a stylistic slip; it is the mark of a learner, and it is completely invisible to the person making it, because the Russian, the Polish, the Spanish and the French all put a preposition there.

### The full table

| Blocker | Phrases | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **last** | last night, last week, last month, last year, last summer, last Tuesday, last time | *We moved **last March**.* |
| **next** | next week, next month, next year, next Friday, next time | *The lease is up **next June**.* |
| **this** | this morning, this afternoon, this evening, this week, this year, this Friday | *I'll finish it **this afternoon**.* |
| **that** | that day, that morning, that week, that year | *Nobody worked **that afternoon**.* |
| **every** | every day, every Monday, every morning, every three hours, every other week | *Take one **every four hours**.* |
| **each** | each day, each morning, each quarter | *Numbers go out **each Friday**.* |
| **some** | some day, some evening, some other time | *Come by **some evening**.* |
| **any** | any day, any time, any minute | *It could go out **any minute**.* |
| **all** | all day, all night, all week, all year, all morning | *The phones rang **all afternoon**.* |
| **one** | one day, one morning, one Tuesday in April | ***One night** the power just quit.* |
| **another** | another day, another time, another week | *We'll try **another day**.* |
| **bare deictics** | yesterday, today, tomorrow, tonight, now, then | *Call me **tonight**.* |
| **the day before / after** | the day before yesterday, the day after tomorrow, the night before | *She flew in **the day before yesterday**.* |

### What does *not* block the preposition

The blockers are that closed list. Put a definite article in front of the same noun and the preposition comes straight back, because *the* makes the phrase a period rather than a pointer:

| Zero preposition | Preposition required | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| *last week* | ***in** the last week* / ***over** the past week* | *Last week* is the calendar week before this one. *In the last week* is the rolling seven days just gone. |
| *next month* | ***in** the next month* | *Next month* is a named month. *In the next month* is the thirty days ahead. |
| *last year* | ***in** the last year* / ***over** the last year* | Same split: the calendar year, versus the twelve months just finished. |
| *this week* | ***during** the week* | *During the week* in American usage often means "on weekdays," as opposed to the weekend. |
| *every Friday* | ***on** Fridays* | Two ways to say the same habit. |
| *one morning* | ***on** the morning of the 12th* | A date brings *on* back with it. |

A second, narrower exception: **nouns that name a position in a sequence rather than a measure of time** keep *at*, even after *this* or *that*. *Moment*, *point*, *stage*, *time* and *juncture* behave this way.

> ***At this point** we may as well wait for the next one.*  
> ***At that moment** the lights came back on.*  
> *The project was canceled **at that stage**.*  
> ***At the time**, nobody thought it was strange.*

The test is whether the noun could be replaced by a stretch of calendar. *This week* can (a week is seven days). *This point* cannot, because a point in a process is not a length of anything — so the preposition survives.

### The mirror-image error

The block is so counterintuitive that some learners overcorrect and start dropping prepositions where English wants them. The rule cuts one way only: a **determiner** from the list above blocks the preposition, and nothing else does.

> ✗ *We got back **the** Friday.* → *We got back **on Friday**.*  
> ✗ *The meeting is **1 p.m.*** → *The meeting is **at 1 p.m.***  
> ✗ *He was born **1994**.* → *He was born **in 1994**.*

## Duration: how long, and what it sits inside

Six prepositions share this ground, and they do not overlap as much as they look. Three of them take a **measured length** (*for*, *within*, *in*), two take a **named period** (*during*, *throughout*), one takes a **starting point** (*since*), and one does both a period and a span (*over*). Feeding the wrong kind of object to one of them is the commonest error in the whole family.

| Preposition | Takes | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **for** | a measured length | this much time passed | *for three hours*, *for two weeks*, *for a while* |
| **since** | a starting point | from then up to now | *since Monday*, *since 2019*, *since the storm* |
| **during** | a named period | at some time inside it | *during the war*, *during the meeting* |
| **over** | a period, or a span | across the whole of it | *over the weekend*, *over the past decade* |
| **throughout** | a named period | across all of it, with no gaps | *throughout the winter* |
| **within** | a measured length | before that much time is up | *within two weeks*, *within the hour* |
| **in** | a measured length | at the end of that much time | *in an hour* = sixty minutes from now |
| **inside of** | a measured length | informal American for *within* | *inside of an hour* |

### *for*, *since*, and *ago*

These three answer three different questions, and each takes a different kind of object.

| Word | Object | Question answered | Tense it lives in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **for** | a length | *How long?* | any — *I lived there for six years* / *I've lived here for six years* |
| **since** | a point | *Starting when?* | perfect only — *I've lived here since 2019* |
| **ago** | a length | *How long back?* | simple past — *I moved here six years ago* |

> I've worked here **for** three years. — ✗ *since three years*  
> I've worked here **since** 2022. — ✗ *for 2022*  
> I started here three years **ago**. — ✗ *before three years*  
> ✗ *I am here since Monday.* → *I**'ve been** here since Monday.*

Two structural notes. **Ago is a postposition** — it follows its object instead of preceding it, which makes it the only word in this section that does (*two weeks ago*, never ✗ *ago two weeks*). And **American English drops *for* freely** in front of a plain measure: *I've lived here three years* is completely ordinary, where ✗ *I've lived here since three years* never is.

The tense side of this — why *since* forces a perfect and *ago* forbids one — is in [The present perfect](../04-tenses/03-present-perfect.md); *ago* against adverbial *before* and *beforehand* is in [Adverbs and sequence](05-adverbs-and-sequence.md).

### *during* versus *for*

**During names the period. For measures it.** *During* answers *when*, not *how long*, which is why a number after it is almost always an error.

> ✗ *We talked **during** three hours.* → *We talked **for** three hours.*  
> We talked **during** the flight. — inside that period, not necessarily the whole of it  
> He fell asleep **during** the second half.  
> Nothing was decided **during** the meeting.

*During* also refuses a gerund: ✗ *during eating* is not English. The repairs are *while eating* or *in the middle of eating*.

One overlap is real: with a named period that also has a length, both work and mean different things. *I read it **during** the flight* says the reading happened somewhere inside the flight; *I read it **for** the whole flight* says it filled the flight.

### *over* and *throughout*

*Over* spans a period and usually implies that something developed across it or happened repeatedly inside it. *Throughout* insists on the entire extent with no gaps, and is the emphatic version of *during*.

> We'll be out of town **over** the weekend.  
> Rates have fallen **over** the past decade.  
> We talked about it **over** dinner. — during the meal, and the meal is the occasion  
> The store stays open **throughout** the holidays.  
> She was in pain **throughout** the flight. — all of it, continuously

*Over the weekend* is the American phrase; *over Christmas*, *over the summer*, and *over the years* work the same way. Note that *over* is the one word here that also takes a bare meal (*over coffee*, *over lunch*), where *during lunch* is possible but colder.

### *in*, *within*, and *inside of*

**This is the trio that costs money.** All three attach to a measured length, and they mean three different things about the end of it.

| Phrase | Means | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| ***in** an hour* | at the end of the hour — sixty minutes from now | one point, at the far edge |
| ***within** an hour* | at any time before the hour is up | the whole interval, with a hard edge |
| ***inside of** an hour* | the same as *within*, informal American | the whole interval |

> I'll be there **in** an hour. — around 4:00 if it's 3:00 now  
> I'll be there **within** an hour. — any time between now and 4:00  
> They had the power back on **inside of** twenty minutes.  
> Payment is due **within** thirty days of the invoice date. — a contract would never write *in thirty days* here

Casual American speech lets *in* do both jobs, and nobody misunderstands *I'll call you in five minutes*. Formal writing keeps them apart, and for a deadline **within** is the unambiguous word.

*In* + length also has a second, unrelated life: after a negative or a superlative it means "for," and it is the American default — *I haven't been back **in** years*, *the worst storm **in** a decade*.

### *from … to* and *between … and*

Two frames, both taking a pair of endpoints.

> The office is open **from** nine **to** five.  
> The road was closed **from** Tuesday **until** Thursday.  
> He was governor **from** 1998 **to** 2006.  
> The store is closed **between** one **and** two.  
> Somebody called **between** eight and nine.

The difference: ***from … to*** **names a run**, and something continues across it. ***Between … and*** **names a window**, and something happens at an unspecified point inside it. *We drove from noon to six* is six hours of driving; *We left between noon and six* is one departure, time unknown.

And *from … to* has the American inclusive problem built into it — see *through* below.

## Deadlines and boundaries: *by* versus *until*

This is the most useful pair in the section, and the one where a mistake changes what somebody is expected to do.

### The two meanings

> **by** = **the last acceptable moment for a single act.** It can happen at any point up to then.  
> **until** = **the point where a continuing state or activity stops.** It goes on the whole time up to then.

> Finish the report **by** Friday. — any time before Friday is fine; Friday is the outside limit  
> Stay **until** Friday. — remain continuously, and leave on Friday

### The test

**Look at the verb.** A verb that names one completed act — *finish*, *submit*, *send*, *pay*, *arrive*, *call*, *decide* — takes ***by***. A verb that names a state or an activity stretching out in time — *wait*, *stay*, *keep*, *sleep*, *work*, *live*, *be open* — takes ***until***.

> ✗ *Finish it **until** Friday.* → *Finish it **by** Friday.*  
> ✗ *Wait **by** Friday.* → *Wait **until** Friday.*

A second test, if the verb is ambiguous: try inserting the paraphrase. ***By*** takes "at any time before," and ***until*** takes "continuously up to."

| Sentence | Paraphrase that fits | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| *Get the numbers to me ___ Thursday.* | at any time before Thursday | **by** |
| *The pool is open ___ nine.* | continuously up to nine | **until** |
| *I need an answer ___ noon.* | at any time before noon | **by** |
| *We're staying ___ Sunday.* | continuously up to Sunday | **until** |
| *He should be home ___ six.* | at any time before six | **by** |
| *He'll be home ___ six.* | continuously up to six — then he leaves | **until** |

That last pair is worth staring at. *He should be home **by** six* means he will arrive no later than six. *He'll be home **until** six* means he is there now and leaves at six. One word, two opposite schedules.

### More pairs

> Pay the balance **by** the 15th. / The offer stands **until** the 15th.  
> Turn it in **by** Monday. / Don't turn it in **until** Monday.  
> The lock changes **by** noon. / The old key works **until** noon.  
> We should hear **by** now. / Nothing **up until** now has worked.

### *not … until*

The negative frame is where *until* does something *by* cannot: it names the point at which something **finally** happens.

> He didn't call **until** Tuesday. — the call happened on Tuesday  
> She didn't find out **until** the movers were loading the truck.  
> Nothing was decided **until** well after midnight.

Fronted for emphasis, it inverts the main clause: ***Not until** Tuesday **did he** call.* That inversion belongs to the same family as fronted *only if* and *rarely*; it is worked through in [Inversion and formal conditionals](../03-conditionals/04-inversion-and-formal.md).

### *by the time*

*By the time* is a complex preposition that takes a **clause**, and it is the only member of this family that does. Its clause obeys the no-*will* rule that governs every time clause in English.

> **By the time** we got there, everyone had already eaten.  
> **By the time** you read this, I'll be in Denver. — ✗ *by the time you will read this*  
> **By the time** the permit clears, it'll be October.

Note the tense pairing: a past *by the time* clause usually pulls a past perfect into the main clause, because the main event was finished first. The rule about *will* is in [The present simple](../04-tenses/01-present-simple.md), and the conjunction side of *until*, *before*, *after* and *by the time* is in [70 subordinators of time](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md).

### The other deadline words

| Phrase | What it adds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***by*** | the plain deadline; leaves inclusion slightly open | *Get it in **by** Friday.* |
| ***before*** | **excludes** the stated day — a day tighter than *by* | *File it **before** Friday.* (Thursday at the latest) |
| ***no later than*** | includes the stated day and says so; the contract word | *Proposals must arrive **no later than** 5 p.m. on March 3.* |
| ***up to*** | the whole run leading to a limit, or a maximum | *Valid **up to** December 31.* &middot; *Allow **up to** six weeks.* |
| ***up to and including*** | inclusion, drafted out explicitly | *Claims **up to and including** the 30th.* |
| ***as late as*** | how much latitude there is | *You can cancel **as late as** the morning of.* |
| ***through*** | American inclusive endpoint | *Open Monday **through** Friday.* (Friday included) |
| ***pending*** | until a decision comes; formal and legal | *The license is suspended **pending** review.* |

**The inclusion problem is real and it is American English's one genuine contribution here.** *Open until Friday* does not tell a reader whether Friday counts; *before Friday* clearly excludes it; *by Friday* leaves it open; *through Friday* settles it. British English has no *through* solution, which is why British schedules say *Monday to Friday inclusive*.

> We're open Monday **through** Friday. — five days  
> The exhibit runs **through** September. — September included  
> The sale ends **Friday**. — spoken American, and still ambiguous; a careful writer says *through Friday* or *at midnight Thursday*

## Sequence: what comes before and what comes after

### The neutral pair

*Before* and *after* order two events on a line and carry nothing else — no cause, no judgment, no claim about the size of the gap.

> The pharmacy closes **after** nine.  
> Sign the lease **before** the walk-through.  
> Read the whole contract **before** signing anything. — both take a gerund  
> She quit two weeks **before** the merger was announced.

Two contrasts do most of the work. ***Before* excludes, *by* includes**: a deadline written *before Friday* is a day tighter than most writers intend. And ***before* is not *ago***: *ago* counts backward from now and takes the simple past (*three days ago*), while *before* counts backward from a point already in the past and goes with the past perfect (*he had left three days before*).

### The heavy register

Every one of these means *before* or *after* in a suit. None of them is clearer, and an American editor cuts most of them on sight — but they are everywhere in legal, corporate and journalistic prose, so they have to be readable.

| Phrase | Means | Register | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| *prior to* | before | bureaucratic | The commonest of them, and a syllable longer than *before*. |
| *in advance of* | before | formal | Carries a note of preparation: you do something *in advance of* an event you are getting ready for. |
| *ahead of* | before | neutral to journalistic | The live one. *Ahead of the vote* is ordinary American news English, and *ahead of schedule* is idiomatic. |
| *following* | after | journalistic | Newsroom dress for *after*: *following the hearing*. |
| *subsequent to* | after | bureaucratic | The mirror of *prior to*, and heavier still. |
| *post* | after | finance, policy | Attributive and prepositional: *post-2008*, *post-merger*. |
| *upon* | on, at the moment of | contractual | *Upon signing*, *upon arrival*. Ordinary English says *on signing*. |
| *pending* | until, awaiting | legal | Not a synonym for *during*: *pending review* means the outcome is still open. |
| *on the eve of* | just before | journalistic | Figurative as often as literal: *on the eve of the strike*. |

> **Prior to** the hearing, both sides exchanged exhibit lists.  
> **Ahead of** Tuesday's vote, three members changed their positions.  
> **Following** the merger, the Dallas office closed.  
> The permit is suspended **pending** the outcome of the appeal.

Note the complement: *prior to*, *subsequent to*, *ahead of*, *in advance of*, *following* and *upon* all take a **gerund** as happily as a noun — *prior to filing*, *upon signing*, *ahead of announcing the cuts*. *Pending* does not.

### *past*

*Past* puts a time on the far side of a line, and the line is the whole point. *After midnight* just names a later time; ***past midnight*** **says a boundary was crossed**, and usually that somebody should not have been up.

> It's **past** midnight.  
> The kids were up way **past** their bedtime.  
> The milk is a week **past** its expiration date.  
> By then she was **past** caring what anyone thought.  
> It's twenty **past** four. — the clock sense, alive and well in American speech

That evaluative edge is in *past* and not in *after*, which is why *past the deadline* is a failure and *after the deadline* is a date.

### Runs and endpoints

| Frame | Includes the end? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *from … to* | ambiguous | *from Monday to Friday* |
| *from … until* | ambiguous | *from nine until five* |
| *from … through* | **yes**, explicitly (American) | *from Monday through Friday* |
| *between … and* | names a window, not a run | *between one and two* |
| *as of* | starts here and continues | *as of January 1* |

***As of*** deserves its own line, because American business English runs on it. It means "starting from, and still true," and it works forward (*The policy takes effect **as of** July 1*) and backward (***As of** this morning, we had eleven confirmed*). Its British twins are *as from* and *with effect from*, which read as imported in American copy; the American alternative is ***effective*** — *effective Monday*, *effective immediately*.

## Approximation: roughly when

Six ways to hedge a time, and in American English two of them cover nearly everything. (For a date rather than a clock time there is a seventh, the historian's *circa* — *a farmhouse built **circa** 1890*.)

| Word | Means | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **around** | approximately | neutral, the American default | *Come by **around** six.* |
| **about** | approximately | neutral, interchangeable with *around* | *It was **about** four when they left.* |
| **toward** | as it approaches, and not yet arrived | neutral, slightly literary | *It clouded over **toward** evening.* |
| **near** | close to a point, statically | neutral | ***Near** the end of the year, everything slows down.* |
| **close to** | the same, more gradable | neutral | *It was **close to** midnight.* |
| **along about** | around, in a particular voice | regional — Southern, Midland | ***Long about** four he gave up and went home.* |

Three notes. ***Around* and *about* are interchangeable** in American English for time, and *around* is the more distinctly American of the two; British *round about six* is not written here. ***Toward*** is directional — it says the time is being approached, so *toward evening* works and ✗ *toward six o'clock sharp* does not. And American English writes *toward*, not *towards*, along with *among* over *amongst* and *while* over *whilst*.

For legal hedging there are two fixed phrases — *on or about* (a date: *on or about March 3*) and *in or about* (a month or a year) — which exist precisely so that a document can be wrong about the date without being false.

## Frequency: how often

Four patterns, and the first one catches learners because the preposition is an article.

| Pattern | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***a*** **/** ***an*** | [count] + *a* / *an* + [singular unit] | *twice **a** week* &middot; *three times **a** day* &middot; *$20 **an** hour* |
| ***per*** | *per* + bare singular unit | *40 hours **per** week* &middot; *twice **per** day* |
| ***every*** **/** ***each*** | no preposition at all | *every day* &middot; *every other Tuesday* &middot; *every three hours* |
| ***on*** + plural day | habitual | *on Mondays* &middot; *on weekends* |

> She goes twice **a** week.  
> The medication is one tablet **a** day, or four **a** day at the start.  
> The lease allows two guests **per** night.  
> The bus runs **every** fifteen minutes.  
> I work **on** Saturdays. — or, in American speech, *I work Saturdays.*

***A*** **and** ***per*** **mean the same thing and differ only in register.** *Twice a week* is what people say; *twice per week* is what a lab report or a rate card says, and it sounds stiff in conversation. Neither one takes an extra article: ✗ *per a week*, ✗ *twice the week*.

***Every*** is not a preposition and never takes one — that is the zero-preposition rule again. It also does something *a* and *per* cannot: it counts in multiples (*every three hours*, *every other week*, *every fourth Tuesday*), where ✗ *three hours a time* is not English.

The one-word frequency adverbs — *always*, *usually*, *often*, *rarely*, *never* — are a different system with a different position rule, and they belong to [Duration and frequency](04-duration-and-frequency.md) and [Adverbs and sequence](05-adverbs-and-sequence.md).

## The multi-word time prepositions

Phrases of two to four words that behave as single prepositions. Most take a gerund as readily as a noun, which is their practical advantage over the one-word words.

| Phrase | Means | Takes a gerund? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *in the course of* | during, over the span of | yes | *In the course of one winter, three of them closed.* |
| *in the middle of* | partway through | yes | *He called in the middle of dinner.* &middot; *in the middle of moving* |
| *at the start of* / *at the beginning of* | at its opening edge | no | *at the start of the quarter* |
| *at the end of* | at its closing edge | no | *at the end of the month* |
| *by the end of* | no later than its close | no | *by the end of the week* |
| *at the time of* | when it happened | yes | *at the time of the sale* &middot; *at the time of filing* |
| *for the duration of* | for all of it | no | *for the duration of the strike* |
| *as of* | starting from, and still true | no | *as of January 1* |
| *as from* | British for *as of* | no | *as from 1 July* — write *as of* |
| *up until* / *up till* | emphatic *until* | no | *Up until the recount, she thought she'd won.* |
| *right up to* | emphatic, and right against the edge | yes | *right up to the last minute* &middot; *right up to closing* |
| *in advance of* | before, with preparation | yes | *in advance of the inspection* |
| *subsequent to* | after | yes | *subsequent to the filing* |
| *on the eve of* | just before | no | *on the eve of the vote* |

Two of these hide a trap.

***In the course of*** is not a synonym for *during* in every position: it prefers a period with internal development (*in the course of the investigation*), and it sounds wrong pinned to a short fixed event (✗ *in the course of the toast*).

***In the middle of*** is the repair for the gerund that *during* refuses. ✗ *during eating* has two fixes — *while eating*, which is a conjunction, and *in the middle of eating*, which is a preposition and keeps the phrase a noun phrase.

## For Russian speakers

Five interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

### 1. Russian «в» splits three ways, and nothing marks the split

One Russian preposition covers the whole *at / on / in* ladder, so the choice English forces is invisible from the Russian side. The good news is that the ladder is mechanical once you see it; the bad news is that «в» gives you no clue which rung you are on.

| Russian | English | Rung |
|---|---|---|
| в пять часов | **at** five o'clock | point |
| в полдень / в полночь | **at** noon / **at** midnight | point |
| в понедельник | **on** Monday | day |
| в мой день рождения | **on** my birthday | day |
| в марте | **in** March | container |
| в 2019 году | **in** 2019 | container |
| в двадцатом веке | **in** the twentieth century | container |
| ночью | **at** night | the one hole |
| утром / днём / вечером | **in** the morning / afternoon / evening | container |
| в выходные | **on** the weekend *(American)* | day |
| в конце месяца | **at** the end of the month | edge |
| в то время | **at** that time | point in a sequence |

> ✗ *in Monday* → **on** Monday  
> ✗ *at the morning* → **in** the morning  
> ✗ *in night* → **at** night  
> ✗ *in the weekend* → **on** the weekend

### 2. «Через час» is *in an hour*, and never ✗ *through an hour*

«Через» is a false friend in two directions at once. The preposition that translates it is ***in***, and the two words that look like it — *through* and *after* — are both wrong for the "from now" meaning.

> Через час → *I'll be there **in** an hour.* — ✗ *through an hour*, ✗ *after an hour*  
> Через два дня → *The results come **in** two days.*  
> Через неделю после операции → ***A week after** the surgery*, she went back to work.

The honest complication: ***after*** **is correct when the reference point is in the past**, not now. *After an hour, he gave up* is fine, because the hour is measured from a point in the story. ✗ *After an hour I'll call you* is not, because the hour is measured from now, and that is *in*.

And «через каждые два дня» is *every other day* or *every two days* — with no preposition at all.

### 3. «На прошлой неделе» loses its preposition entirely

Russian puts a preposition on nearly every time phrase. English deletes it in front of the whole *last / next / this / every* family, and that deletion is the single most reliable giveaway of a Russian-speaking writer.

| Russian | English | Not |
|---|---|---|
| на прошлой неделе | **last week** | ✗ *in last week*, ✗ *on last week* |
| в прошлом году | **last year** | ✗ *in last year* |
| в следующий понедельник | **next Monday** | ✗ *on next Monday* |
| на этой неделе | **this week** | ✗ *in this week* |
| сегодня утром | **this morning** | ✗ *in this morning* |
| вчера | **yesterday** | ✗ *at yesterday* |
| завтра | **tomorrow** | ✗ *on tomorrow* |
| каждый день | **every day** | ✗ *in every day* |
| весь день | **all day** | ✗ *during all day* |
| на следующий день | **the next day** | ✗ *on the next day* in narrative |

The one free ride in the whole section: «каждый день» takes no preposition in Russian either, so *every day* transfers without thinking about it. Use it as the anchor and extend the feeling to the rest of the list.

### 4. «До пяти» is two different English sentences

Russian «до» covers both the deadline and the duration, and Russian has no way to mark which one is meant — the verb does it, or nothing does. English has two words and forces the choice.

| Russian | Meaning | English |
|---|---|---|
| Закончи до пяти. | one act, deadline | Finish it **by** five. |
| Жди до пяти. | continuing state | Wait **until** five. |
| Магазин работает до девяти. | continuing state | The store is open **until** nine. |
| Сдай отчёт до пятницы. | one act, deadline | Turn in the report **by** Friday. |
| Я буду дома до шести. | continuing — I'm there, then I leave | I'll be home **until** six. |
| Я буду дома к шести. | deadline — I arrive | I'll be home **by** six. |

Russian «к» maps cleanly onto *by*, and that is the pair to memorize: **«к» → *by*, «до» → *until* or *by* depending on the verb.** The test is the one above — one completed act takes *by*, a continuing state takes *until*.

### 5. «За два дня» and «два дня назад» are not the same shape in English

Three Russian phrases that all involve a measured length, and three different English structures.

| Russian | English | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| два дня назад | **two days ago** | length + postposition, simple past |
| за два дня (до) | **two days before** | length + *before* + past perfect |
| за два дня (успеть) | **in two days** | completion inside a span |
| в течение двух дней | **for** two days *(duration)* &middot; **within** two days *(deadline)* &middot; **during** those two days *(when)* | three different words |
| на две недели | **for** two weeks | ✗ *on two weeks* |
| с 2019 года | **since** 2019 | requires a perfect tense |
| по пятницам | **on** Fridays | habitual plural |
| раз в неделю | **once a week** | the article is the preposition |

«В течение» is the worst of these, because a single Russian phrase splits three ways and the split is exactly the *for / within / during* distinction English cares most about.

> «Мы говорили в течение трёх часов» → *We talked **for** three hours.* — ✗ *during three hours*  
> «Ответьте в течение трёх дней» → *Reply **within** three days.*  
> «В течение встречи никто не возражал» → ***During** the meeting*, nobody objected.

**What transfers for free.** Russian «около пяти» is *around five* with no surprises, «после обеда» is *after lunch*, and «перед встречей» is *before the meeting*. The sequence prepositions are the easy half of this section for a Russian speaker; the trouble is concentrated in the zero-preposition family, in «до», and in «в течение».

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I saw her **in last week**.* | *I saw her **last week**.* | *Last* blocks the preposition outright. |
| ✗ *We're leaving **on next Monday**.* | *We're leaving **next Monday**.* | So does *next*. |
| ✗ ***At yesterday** I forgot my badge.* | ***Yesterday** I forgot my badge.* | *Yesterday*, *today*, *tomorrow* and *tonight* never take one. |
| ✗ *I work **in the night**.* | *I work **at night**.* | *At night* is the fixed exception; *in the night* means one particular night. |
| ✗ *See you **at the morning**.* | *See you **in the morning**.* | Parts of the day take *in* — except *night*. |
| ✗ *What are you doing **at the weekend**?* | *What are you doing **on the weekend**?* | *At the weekend* is British. |
| ✗ *We talked **during** three hours.* | *We talked **for** three hours.* | *During* names a period; only *for* measures one. |
| ✗ *I've lived here **since** three years.* | *I've lived here **for** three years.* | *Since* takes a starting point, never a length. |
| ✗ *I **am** here since Monday.* | *I**'ve been** here since Monday.* | *Since* requires a perfect tense. |
| ✗ *He left **before** three days.* | *He left three days **ago**.* | *Ago* counts back from now and follows its object. |
| ✗ *Finish it **until** Friday.* | *Finish it **by** Friday.* | A single completed act takes the deadline word. |
| ✗ *Wait **by** Friday.* | *Wait **until** Friday.* | A continuing state takes the duration word. |
| ✗ *Reply **in** three days.* — meaning a deadline | *Reply **within** three days.* | *In* puts the reply at the end of the three days; *within* puts it anywhere inside them. |
| ✗ *I'll call you **after** an hour.* | *I'll call you **in** an hour.* | Measured from now, «через» is *in*. *After* needs a past reference point. |
| ✗ *by the time you **will read** this* | *by the time you **read** this* | No *will* in a time clause. |
| ✗ *We're open Monday **to** Friday inclusive.* | *We're open Monday **through** Friday.* | American English has a one-word inclusive endpoint. |
| ✗ *twice **per a** week* | *twice **a** week* / *twice **per** week* | The rate takes one word, not both. |
| ✗ *during eating* | *while eating* / *in the middle of eating* | *During* never takes a gerund. |

## Quick reference

**The ladder, compressed.**

```
   at    a point      at 6:15 · at noon · at night · at Christmas · at the age of ten
   on    a day        on Tuesday · on July 4 · on Tuesday morning · on the weekend
   in    a container  in March · in 1998 · in the morning · in an hour · in her thirties
   —     blocked      last week · next Monday · this morning · every day · all night
                      yesterday · today · tomorrow · tonight
```

**The five contrasts to memorize.**

> *Finish it **by** Friday.* ≠ *Stay **until** Friday.* — a deadline, versus a duration.  
> *I'll be there **in** an hour.* ≠ *I'll be there **within** an hour.* — at the end of it, versus anywhere inside it.  
> *We talked **for** three hours.* ≠ *We talked **during** the flight.* — how long, versus when.  
> ***at** Christmas* ≠ ***on** Christmas Day* — the season, versus the dated day.  
> *We're open **through** Friday.* ≠ *We're open **before** Friday.* — Friday included, versus Friday excluded.

**What never takes a length of time:** *since*, *during*, *until*, *by*, *no later than*. All five take a point.

**What never takes a gerund:** *during*, *until*, *pending*, *no later than*.

**What takes a gerund freely:** *before*, *after*, *since*, *upon*, *prior to*, *subsequent to*, *ahead of*, *in advance of*, *at the time of*, *in the course of*, *in the middle of*.

**What follows its object instead of preceding it:** *ago*, and *hence* in formal writing (*three days hence*).

**British forms to recognize but not to write:** *at the weekend* → *on the weekend* &middot; *Monday to Friday* (inclusive) → *Monday through Friday* &middot; *in future* → *in the future* &middot; *as from July 1* → *as of July 1* &middot; *with effect from* → *effective* &middot; *towards* → *toward* &middot; *a fortnight* → *two weeks*.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | She's been with the company **since** 2018. | A starting point, with a perfect tense. |
| 2 | We waited **for** two hours. | A measured length — ✗ *during two hours*. |
| 3 | I'll see you **—** next Thursday. | *Next* blocks the preposition. |
| 4 | The package should get there **by** Friday. | A single completed act, with a deadline. |
| 5 | Don't open it **until** I get there. | A continuing state stopping at a point — and here *until* is a conjunction. |
| 6 | He works **at** night and sleeps **in** the morning. | The one hole in the ladder, next to the rule it breaks. |
| 7 | We're closed **on** Sundays. | Habitual plural day. In American speech, *We're closed Sundays.* |
| 8 | The exhibit runs **through** September 30 — **the 30th is included**. | The American inclusive endpoint. *Until* would leave it open. |
| 9 | Reply **within** ten business days. | Any point before the ten days are up. *In ten days* would mean on the tenth. |

### One more pass

Say what changes in each pair, in one sentence:

1. *I'll be home **by** six.* / *I'll be home **until** six.*
2. *She read it **during** the flight.* / *She read it **for** the whole flight.*
3. *The sale ends **Friday**.* / *The sale runs **through Friday**.*
4. *I haven't seen him **in** years.* / *I didn't see him **for** years.*

Answers: (1) arrival versus departure — *by* means he gets there no later than six, *until* means he is there now and leaves at six. (2) somewhere inside it, versus filling it. (3) ambiguous, versus Friday explicitly included. (4) the American present-perfect idiom about now, versus a finished stretch of past time.
