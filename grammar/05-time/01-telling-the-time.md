# Telling the time

> Every way English has of saying what the clock says — the digital reading and the analog one, *a.m.* and *p.m.*, noon and midnight, military time, and the half hour that Russian speakers get backwards.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

English gives you two completely different ways to read a clock, and a fluent speaker uses both without noticing the switch.

> It's **six forty-five**. — read the numerals  
> It's **a quarter to seven**. — read the face

The first is the **digital reading**: say the hour, then say the minutes as a number. The second is the **analog reading**: say how far the minute hand is from the nearest hour, and in which direction. They describe the same instant, they are equally correct, and they do not mix — ✗ *a quarter to six forty-five* is not a sentence.

One fact shapes everything below: **in American English the digital reading is the default.** Where a British speaker reaches for *a quarter to seven*, an American is more likely to say *six forty-five*, and where a British speaker says *half past six*, an American almost always says *six thirty*. The analog forms are alive and completely standard in the US — nobody will blink at *a quarter after three* — but they are the marked choice, not the neutral one. A learner who masters the digital reading first can say every time on the clock; a learner who masters only the analog one cannot say 6:37 at all.

The rest of this section works through the asking, the two systems position by position, the *a.m.* / *p.m.* apparatus, noon and midnight, military time, approximation, punctuation, and the verbs that go with clocks. Where the clock meets a preposition — *at six*, *by six*, *until six*, *in an hour* — the entries are in [prepositions of time](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md).

## Asking what time it is

### The questions

| Question | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **What time is it?** | neutral | The default. Safe with anyone, anywhere. |
| **Do you have the time?** | neutral, to a stranger | The polite street version. The **the** is not optional — see below. |
| **Got the time?** | casual | Clipped from *Have you got the time?* Fine with a coworker, brusque with a stranger. |
| **You got the time?** | casual | Same thing with the auxiliary dropped instead of the subject. Very American. |
| **What time do you have?** | neutral | Asks what **your** watch says, when two clocks disagree. |
| **Could you tell me the time?** | polite, formal | A full request, not a bare question. |
| **Do you know what time it is?** | polite | Literally a yes/no question; nobody answers *yes*. |
| **Do you happen to have the time?** | extra polite | *Happen to* softens it further. |
| **What's the time?** | neutral, more British | Understood everywhere; Americans say *What time is it?* |
| **Have you got the time?** | more British | Fine in the US, slightly bookish. |
| **What time do you make it?** | British only | Americans say *What time do you have?* |
| **Is it six yet?** | casual, specific | Not asking the time; checking against one. |

The polite forms almost always carry an opener: *Excuse me — do you have the time?* Walking up to a stranger with a bare *What time is it?* is not wrong, but it lands abruptly.

### *the time* and *time* are two different questions

This one is worth isolating, because the error is invisible to the learner and very visible to the listener.

> **Do you have the time?** — What time is it?  
> **Do you have time?** — Are you free? Can you spare a moment?

The definite article carries the whole difference. *The time* is the reading on the clock, a specific thing; *time* is the uncountable stuff, a quantity. So:

> ✗ *Excuse me, do you have time?* — if you meant the clock, you have just asked a stranger whether they are busy  
> ✓ *Excuse me, do you have **the** time?*

And in the other direction: *Do you have a minute?* / *Do you have a second?* ask for a moment of someone's attention and never for the time of day. The uncountable / countable split behind this is in [time as a noun](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md).

### Word order in the polite version

*Do you know what time it is?* is an **embedded question**, and an embedded question keeps statement word order. The inversion happens once, on *do you know*, and not again inside.

> ✓ *Do you know **what time it is**?* &middot; ✗ *Do you know **what time is it**?*  
> ✓ *Could you tell me **what time it is**?* &middot; ✗ *Could you tell me **what time is it**?*

Said with a particular intonation, *Do you know what time it is?* can also be a reproach — *Do you know what time it is?* to a teenager coming home at two in the morning is not a request for information.

### Answering

The answer begins with ***It's***. English has no way to state a time without a subject, and *it* is the only subject available.

> **It's** ten after six. &middot; **It's** almost noon.  
> ✗ *Is six o'clock.* &middot; ✗ *Now six o'clock.*  
> ✗ *The time is six o'clock now.* — grammatical, but it sounds like a recorded announcement

In speech the *It's* is very often dropped altogether, and the bare time is the most natural answer of all:

> "What time is it?" — "**Six forty-five.**"  
> "Got the time?" — "**Almost seven.**"  
> "Do you have the time?" — "**Sorry, I don't** — my phone's dead."

*Sorry, I don't have a watch* and *Sorry, I don't know* are the standard refusals. Note that the short answer repeats the auxiliary, exactly as it does everywhere else: *Do you have the time?* — *No, I don't.*

## The two systems

### The digital reading

**Hour, then minutes, as two numbers side by side.** No preposition, no *minutes*, nothing between them.

> It's **six thirty**. &middot; It's **nine fifteen**.  
> The train gets in at **eleven forty**.  
> **Two twenty-five** — we've got time.

Two mechanical rules. **Minutes under ten take *oh*.** The digit 0 is read /oʊ/, the name of the letter, never *zero* and never nothing at all.

> 6:05 → **six oh five** &middot; 9:01 → **nine oh one** &middot; 12:09 → **twelve oh nine**  
> ✗ *six five* &middot; ✗ *six zero five* &middot; ✗ *six and five*

**A whole hour is not read with *oh oh*.** 6:00 is *six* or *six o'clock*, never ✗ *six oh oh* — that is military time, and it is read differently again.

The digital reading has one enormous advantage, and it is why Americans default to it: **it covers every minute on the clock.** 6:37 has no analog reading anyone says. *Twenty-three to seven* is arithmetic, not English.

> 6:37 → **six thirty-seven** &middot; 10:52 → **ten fifty-two** &middot; 3:08 → **three oh eight**

### The analog reading

**Minutes, then a direction word, then an hour.** The direction word tells you which side of the hour you are on.

> It's **ten after six**. — 6:10, past the hour  
> It's **twenty to seven**. — 6:40, short of the hour

The pivot is the half hour. **Up to :30 you count forward from the hour that has just gone; from :31 you count backward to the hour that is coming.** The hour named therefore changes halfway around the face, which is the part that has to be internalized rather than worked out.

> 6:20 — *twenty after **six*** &middot; 6:40 — *twenty to **seven***

Both of those are the six o'clock hour on a digital display, and only one of them says *six*.

The analog reading is **restricted to multiples of five**, plus the quarters and the half. Nobody says *seventeen after six*. If the minute number is not a multiple of five, the digital reading is the only option.

### Which one to use

| Time | Digital | Analog | What an American most likely says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6:00 | *six* | *six o'clock* | **six** / **six o'clock** |
| 6:15 | *six fifteen* | *a quarter after six* | **six fifteen** |
| 6:30 | *six thirty* | *half past six* | **six thirty** — by a wide margin |
| 6:45 | *six forty-five* | *a quarter to seven* | either; **quarter to seven** is common |
| 6:37 | *six thirty-seven* | — | **six thirty-seven** |
| 6:05 | *six oh five* | *five after six* | either |

The rough generalization: **the quarters and the half survive in the analog form; everything else has gone digital.** *A quarter after* and *a quarter to* are heard constantly in American speech. *Twenty-five after four* is heard far less than *four twenty-five*.

## Every position on the clock

### The whole hour: *o'clock*

***O'clock*** is a contraction of *of the clock*, and the apostrophe is the surviving mark of the missing *the*. It is **one word**, it takes exactly one apostrophe, and it is pronounced /əˈklɑk/ — the *o'* is a schwa, never /oʊ/.

> It's **six o'clock**.  
> The meeting's at **two o'clock**.  
> ✗ *six oclock* &middot; ✗ *six o clock* &middot; ✗ *six aclock* &middot; ✗ *six o'clocks*

Four restrictions, and they are absolute:

**1. Whole hours only.** *O'clock* means the hand is on the twelve. Any other minute reading rules it out.

> ✗ *six thirty o'clock* &middot; ✗ *half past six o'clock* &middot; ✗ *a quarter to seven o'clock* &middot; ✗ *6:30 o'clock*

**2. The numbers one through twelve only.** ✗ *eighteen o'clock* does not exist in English. Eighteen hundred is military time and is read another way entirely.

**3. Never with *a.m.* or *p.m.*** The two are alternative ways of doing the same job, and using both is like saying the hour twice.

> ✗ *six o'clock p.m.* → *six p.m.* or *six o'clock in the evening*

**4. It is optional, and usually omitted.** *It's six* is the ordinary spoken form; *It's six o'clock* is a shade more deliberate, and is what you say when the hour itself is the point — *I'll be there at six o'clock, not six fifteen.*

*O'clock* survives in a handful of fixed phrases where no time is meant at all: *bogey at two o'clock* (a direction, from aviation), *five o'clock shadow* (a man's stubble by late afternoon), *the five o'clock news*.

### *past* and *after*

For minutes between :01 and :30, English offers two words and American English prefers the second.

| Form | Where | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***past*** | universal; the default in Britain, common in the US | *ten **past** six* |
| ***after*** | American, and the American default | *ten **after** six* |

> It's five **after** six. &middot; It's ten **past** six.  
> Twenty **after** three — I'm on my way.

Neither is more correct: *after* sounds slightly more American, *past* never sounds wrong. What is wrong is pluralizing them — ✗ *five afters six*, ✗ *ten pasts six*. The word *minutes* is normally left out, but it can go back in for precision: *It's twenty-two minutes past six.*

### *a quarter past* / *a quarter after*

Fifteen minutes is **a quarter** of an hour, and the fraction is the normal way to say it.

> It's **a quarter after six**. — 6:15  
> It's **a quarter past six**.  
> **Quarter after six.** — the article dropped, which is very common in American speech

The article is genuinely optional in the US; in careful writing, keep the *a*. **Quarter** is /ˈkwɔrtər/ — two syllables, the vowel of *war* rather than of *car*, both *r*'s pronounced.

> ✗ *a quarter past of six* — there is no *of*  
> ✗ *fifteen past six* — grammatical, and nobody says it  
> ✗ *one quarter past six* — the fraction takes *a*, not *one*

### *half past* — and why Americans say *six thirty*

Thirty minutes is **half past**, and the hour named is **the hour that has already struck**.

> It's **half past six**. = 6:30  
> It's **half past ten**. = 10:30  
> ✗ *half past of six* &middot; ✗ *half of past six* &middot; ✗ *half to seven* &middot; ✗ *a half past six*

**But the American default is the digital reading.** *Six thirty* outnumbers *half past six* in American speech by a wide margin. *Half past six* is correct and instantly understood, and it sounds a little formal or a little British. If you learn one form for :30, learn *six thirty*.

**British *half six* is not American.** In Britain it is a casual clipping of *half past six* and means 6:30. In the United States it is simply not said, and an American hearing it will either fail to parse it or guess wrongly.

**And *half past* is not *half an hour*.** *Half past six* is a point in time; *half an hour* is a length of one. *I'll be there in half an hour* has nothing to do with :30.

### *to*, *of*, and *till*

For minutes between :31 and :59, English counts **down to the next hour**, and offers three words for the job.

| Form | Where | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***to*** | universal, and the safest choice | *ten **to** seven* |
| ***of*** | American, Northeastern and Mid-Atlantic, somewhat old-fashioned | *ten **of** seven* |
| ***till*** | American, Southern and Midland | *ten **till** seven* |

> It's a quarter **to** seven.  
> It's ten **of** seven. — Boston, Philadelphia, New York  
> It's twenty **till** seven. — Texas, Tennessee, Ohio

All three are standard American English within their regions. **Use *to***, the one that is neutral everywhere — but recognize the other two, because *ten of seven* is genuinely opaque if you have never met it and is said by millions of people. *Till* is also spelled *til* and *'til* informally; the entry is at [till](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#till).

> ✗ *ten before seven* — said of the hour in radio and TV (*ten before the hour*), but not of a named hour  
> ✗ *seven minus ten* &middot; ✗ *without ten seven* — the second is a direct translation of Russian and is treated below

### The whole hour, every reading

One hour of the clock, in five-minute steps, with everything a speaker might say. The bolded column is the form to produce; the rest is what to recognize.

| Clock | **Say this** | Also heard | Analog, regional |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6:00** | **six** / **six o'clock** | six sharp, six on the dot | — |
| **6:05** | **six oh five** | five after six | five past six |
| **6:10** | **six ten** | ten after six | ten past six |
| **6:15** | **six fifteen** | a quarter after six | a quarter past six |
| **6:20** | **six twenty** | twenty after six | twenty past six |
| **6:25** | **six twenty-five** | twenty-five after six | twenty-five past six |
| **6:30** | **six thirty** | half past six | half six *(British only)* |
| **6:35** | **six thirty-five** | twenty-five to seven | twenty-five of seven, twenty-five till seven |
| **6:40** | **six forty** | twenty to seven | twenty of seven, twenty till seven |
| **6:45** | **six forty-five** | a quarter to seven | a quarter of seven, a quarter till seven |
| **6:50** | **six fifty** | ten to seven | ten of seven, ten till seven |
| **6:55** | **six fifty-five** | five to seven | five of seven, five till seven |
| **7:00** | **seven** / **seven o'clock** | seven sharp | — |

Read the table down the "Say this" column and the whole system is one rule: **hour, then minutes.** Read it down the third column and the pivot is visible — *six* for the first half of the hour, *seven* for the second.

Two things the table cannot show. First, the odd minutes: 6:37, 6:52, 6:03 have only the digital reading. Second, the frequency: rows 6:15, 6:30 and 6:45 are where the analog forms actually live in American speech. The rest are correct but comparatively rare.

## *a.m.* and *p.m.*

### What they abbreviate

They are Latin, and the full forms explain the whole system:

| Abbreviation | Latin | Literally | Covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **a.m.** | *ante merīdiem* | before midday | 12:00 midnight → 11:59 in the morning |
| **p.m.** | *post merīdiem* | after midday | 12:00 noon → 11:59 at night |

The word is *merīdiem*, the accusative of *merīdiēs*, "midday" — not ✗ *meridian*, which is a line of longitude. Nobody ever says the Latin out loud; *a.m.* and *p.m.* are read as two letter names, /ˌeɪ ˈɛm/ and /ˌpi ˈɛm/, with the stress on the second letter.

### Punctuation

American practice has settled on **lowercase with periods**, and the alternatives are style choices rather than errors.

| Form | Status |
|---|---|
| **6:45 p.m.** | The American standard. Chicago, AP, and most edited prose. |
| **6:45 P.M.** | Correct, more formal, older. Common in small caps in typeset books. |
| **6:45 pm** | Correct, and the British default. Increasingly common in American informal writing. |
| **6:45 PM** | Common on signs, in email, and on digital displays. Not wrong, not the style of careful prose. |
| ✗ *6:45p.m.* | Missing the space. |
| ✗ *6:45 P.m.* | Mixed case. |

Two mechanical points. **There is a space between the time and the abbreviation**: *6:45 p.m.*, not ✗ *6:45p.m.* And at the end of a sentence, **the abbreviation's period doubles as the sentence's**: *The store closes at 9 p.m.* — one period, not two.

### When Americans actually use them

In **writing**, constantly: schedules, invitations, appointment cards, email — anything where the reader is not in the room to infer the rest. In **speech**, only when the ambiguity is real.

> The flight's at six **a.m.** — worth saying; six in the evening is just as plausible  
> Dinner's at six. &middot; I get up at seven. — nobody needs the abbreviation

Where a speaker does want to disambiguate out loud, the ordinary choice is not *a.m.* / *p.m.* but a **phrase**:

| Instead of | Americans say |
|---|---|
| *6 a.m.* | six **in the morning** |
| *2 p.m.* | two **in the afternoon** |
| *7 p.m.* | seven **in the evening** |
| *11 p.m.* | eleven **at night** |
| *3 a.m.* | three **in the morning** — even in the dark |

The rough boundaries: **morning** runs until noon, **afternoon** from noon to about five or six, **evening** from then until about nine, and **night** from there through the small hours. Note that the dead of night is *in the morning* in English once midnight has passed — *three in the morning*, never ✗ *three at night*. The prepositions themselves — *in the morning* but *at night*, *at noon*, *at midnight* — are catalogued at [prepositions of time](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md).

### What not to do with them

**Do not combine them with *o'clock***, and do not combine them with *in the morning* / *at night*. Each pair is two solutions to one problem, and the second error is extremely common.

> ✗ *five o'clock p.m.* → *five p.m.* / *five o'clock in the afternoon*  
> ✗ *at 6 a.m. in the morning* → *at 6 a.m.* / *at six in the morning*  
> ✗ *at 11 p.m. at night* → *at 11 p.m.* / *at eleven at night*  
> ✗ *18:00 p.m.* → *18:00* — the *18* already says which half of the day it is

**In a range, one abbreviation is often enough** when both ends are in the same half: *The store is open 9 to 5 p.m.* is loose but idiomatic; *9 a.m. to 5 p.m.* is the careful version and is never wrong.

## Noon and midnight

These two points are where the *a.m.* / *p.m.* system breaks, and the breakage is worth understanding rather than memorizing.

*Merīdiēs* **is** noon. Noon is therefore neither before midday nor after it, so strictly speaking 12:00 is neither *a.m.* nor *p.m.* Convention has assigned them anyway, and the convention is the opposite of what most people guess:

| Time | Means | Why it confuses |
|---|---|---|
| **12 p.m.** | **noon** — the middle of the day | People expect *p.m.* to mean "evening" |
| **12 a.m.** | **midnight** — the start of the day | People expect *a.m.* to mean "daytime" |

Because roughly half of any audience gets this wrong, careful American writing avoids the numbers entirely — **noon**, **twelve noon**, **12:00 noon**; **midnight**, **twelve midnight**, **12:00 midnight**. AP style drops the *12* as redundant; *12 noon* is nevertheless everywhere, because redundancy is exactly what the reader wants here. Both are defensible; ✗ *12 p.m. noon* is not.

**Midday** means noon and is a perfectly good word, but it is much less common in the US than in Britain. Americans say *noon*, and use *midday* mostly as a modifier — *the midday news*, *a midday flight*.

### Why schedules write *11:59 p.m.*

A deadline of "midnight Friday" is ambiguous in a way that costs money. Does it mean the midnight that begins Friday or the midnight that ends it? Both readings are defensible, so contracts, submission portals, coupons and ballots sidestep the question:

> Entries must be received by **11:59 p.m. on March 3**.  
> Coverage begins at **12:01 a.m. on the first of the month**.

Those two minutes are unambiguously inside the day named, and that is the whole reason they are chosen. If you must use midnight in writing, anchor it: *midnight on Friday night*, *midnight between Friday and Saturday*.

Fixed phrases: *at noon*, *at midnight* — no article, ✗ *at the noon*. *High noon* is twelve exactly, with a whiff of the Western. *The stroke of midnight* is the moment the clock finishes striking twelve. *Burn the midnight oil* is to work very late. The noun entries are at [noon](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md#noon), [midnight](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md#midnight) and [midday](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md#midday).

## The 24-hour clock: *military time*

### What Americans call it and where it lives

The United States is one of the few places where the 24-hour clock is **not** the civilian default. Americans call it **military time**, and the name says where it is used:

- the armed forces, aviation, and air traffic control
- hospitals, EMS, and medical charting — where a mis-read *6:00* can kill someone
- police, fire, and dispatch; lab logs, computer systems, and anything internationally facing

It is **not** used in ordinary conversation, on storefronts, in invitations, on TV listings, or in most American writing. An American who says *I'll see you at nineteen hundred* about dinner is either in uniform or making a joke.

### How it is written

**Four digits, no colon, no *a.m.* / *p.m.*** The leading zero is obligatory.

> 0600 &middot; 0915 &middot; 1200 &middot; 1345 &middot; 2359

Civilian and ISO-style writing keeps the colon — *06:00*, *13:45* — and that is what you see on a European timetable or in a computer log. Military writing drops it.

### How it is read

| Written | Said | Civilian equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **0000** | *zero hundred hours* / *twenty-four hundred* | midnight |
| **0100** | *oh one hundred* / *zero one hundred* | 1 a.m. |
| **0600** | *oh six hundred* / *zero six hundred* | 6 a.m. |
| **0745** | *oh seven forty-five* | 7:45 a.m. |
| **1200** | *twelve hundred* | noon |
| **1300** | *thirteen hundred* | 1 p.m. |
| **1500** | *fifteen hundred* | 3 p.m. |
| **1830** | *eighteen thirty* | 6:30 p.m. |
| **2359** | *twenty-three fifty-nine* | 11:59 p.m. |

The rules behind the table:

- **A whole hour is read as *hundred*,** not *o'clock* and not *oh oh*: 1300 is *thirteen hundred*, never ✗ *thirteen o'clock*.
- ***Hours*** **is often tacked on** — *thirteen hundred hours*. Optional, commoner in speech, and the part civilians imitate when they are being funny.
- **A leading zero is read *oh* or *zero*.** The Navy tends to *zero*, the Army to *oh*; both are ordinary.
- **Minutes are a plain number**, exactly as in the civilian digital reading: 1845 is *eighteen forty-five*.

### Converting

**After noon, add 12 to the civilian hour; to go back, subtract 12.** 3 p.m. + 12 = 1500. 1900 − 12 = 7 p.m. Before noon the two systems agree, except that military time pads the hour to two digits.

In practice an American reading a 24-hour display converts on the spot and then says the civilian time out loud: *It says nineteen forty-five — so, quarter to eight.* That is the reflex to imitate.

## Approximations and exactness

Almost no spoken time is exact. English has a large kit for saying *roughly*, and a smaller one for saying *precisely*.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **about** | approximately | *It's **about** six.* |
| **around** | approximately; the most common of all | *Let's meet **around** seven.* |
| **somewhere around** | vaguer still | ***Somewhere around** eight, I think.* |
| **roughly** | approximately, slightly more formal | *It takes **roughly** two hours.* |
| ***-ish*** | approximately, casual | *Come over at **sixish**.* |
| **just after** | a few minutes past | *It was **just after** nine when he called.* |
| **just before** | a few minutes short | *I got there **just before** noon.* |
| **a little after** | same as *just after* | *It's **a little after** three.* |
| **almost** | not quite there yet | *It's **almost** seven.* |
| **nearly** | same, slightly more formal | *It's **nearly** midnight.* |
| **going on** | approaching, with a note of "already" | *It's **going on** seven — we should eat.* |
| **coming up on** | approaching | *We're **coming up on** noon.* |
| **pushing** | approaching, informal | *It's **pushing** ten. Time for bed.* |
| **sharp** | exactly — follows the time | *Six o'clock **sharp**.* |
| **on the dot** | exactly | *She got here at nine **on the dot**.* |
| **on the nose** | exactly, more casual | *Two thirty **on the nose**.* |
| **exactly** / **precisely** | exactly — precedes the time | *At **exactly** six.* |
| **give or take** | with a margin either way | *Twenty minutes, **give or take**.* |
| **no later than** | a deadline | *Be there **no later than** eight.* |

Four of these repay a closer look.

***-ish*** attaches to any time and makes it casual: *sixish*, *seven-ish*, *six-thirtyish*, *noonish*. Hyphenate it when the run of letters gets hard to read — *seven-ish* beats *sevenish* — and leave it bare otherwise; both spellings are current. It is speech and informal writing only. And *around sixish* is technically redundant and completely ordinary.

***Going on*** says the hour is close enough to feel as if it has arrived, usually with a note that time has got away from the speaker: *It's going on eleven and he still isn't back.* The same idiom does ages: *She's going on forty.*

***Sharp*** comes **after** the time and *exactly* comes **before** it: *at six sharp*, *at exactly six*. ✗ *at sharp six* is not English.

***On the dot*** is the dot on a clock face that marks the minute; *on the nose* comes from broadcasting hand signals. Both mean exactly, both are informal. The prepositions themselves are at [about](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#about) and [around](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#around).

## Writing times

### The separator

**American English uses a colon**: 6:45. The period — 6.45 — is British and continental European, and the comma — 6,45 — is continental European. In American writing both look like errors.

> ✓ *6:45 p.m.*  
> ✗ *6.45 p.m.* &middot; ✗ *6,45 p.m.* &middot; ✗ *6-45*

### Numerals or words

| Context | Convention | Example |
|---|---|---|
| With *a.m.* / *p.m.* | **numerals** | *9 a.m.*, *6:45 p.m.* |
| With *o'clock* | **words** | *six o'clock*, not usually *6 o'clock* |
| Whole hour with *a.m.* / *p.m.* | drop the *:00* in prose | *9 a.m.*, not *9:00 a.m.* |
| In a table or schedule | keep the *:00* for alignment | *9:00 &nbsp; 10:30 &nbsp; 12:00* |
| Noon and midnight | words | *noon*, *midnight* |
| Formal invitations | words throughout | *at half past six in the evening* |

*9:00 a.m.* in running prose is not wrong; it is just heavier than it needs to be. Both Chicago and AP prefer the bare *9 a.m.*

### Ranges

| Form | Status |
|---|---|
| **9–5** | En dash, no spaces. Standard in tables and headings. |
| **9 a.m.–5 p.m.** | The careful full form. |
| **from 9 to 5** | Standard in prose. |
| **9 to 5** | Standard. |
| ✗ *from 9–5* | Mixing *from* with a dash. Use one or the other. |
| ✗ *between 9–5* | Same objection: *between 9 and 5*. |
| **a nine-to-five job** | Hyphenated when it modifies a noun. |
| **the nine-to-five** | Hyphenated as a noun meaning ordinary office work. |
| **24/7** | Round the clock, all week. Read *twenty-four seven*. |

The *from… to…* rule is worth stating plainly because it generalizes: **a dash replaces the whole *from… to…* construction, so you never use both.** *The store is open from 9 to 5* or *The store is open 9–5*, never ✗ *open from 9–5*.

### *o'clock* in writing

Only with whole hours, and normally with the number spelled out: *at six o'clock*. Never with *a.m.*, never with a minute count, and never abbreviated to ✗ *6 o'c.*

## Clocks, watches, and the verbs that go with them

A clock **face** carries three **hands** — the short **hour hand**, the long **minute hand**, the thin **second hand**. A clock with hands is **analog**; one with numerals is **digital**. *Half past* and *a quarter to* describe a hand's position, which is exactly why those readings lose ground every year that fewer clocks have hands.

### What it says, and whether it's right

> The clock **says** ten to six. &middot; What **does** your phone **say**?  
> The microwave **reads** 12:00 — it's been blinking since the power went out.  
> My watch is five minutes **fast**. — it shows a later time than the real one  
> That clock is **slow**. — it shows an earlier one  
> It **gains** a minute a week. &middot; It **loses** two minutes a day.  
> A good watch **keeps** good **time**.

> ✗ *My watch is five minutes quick.* → *five minutes **fast***  
> ✗ *My watch hurries.* → *My watch is **fast**.* / *It **gains** time.*  
> ✗ *My watch is late.* → *My watch is **slow**.*

### Setting, striking, alarms

| Phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **set the clock** | put it to the right time | *I **set** the clock **to** the time on my phone.* |
| **set it ahead / forward** | move it later | *We **set the clocks ahead** an hour in March.* |
| **set it back** | move it earlier | *Don't forget to **set the clocks back** Sunday.* |
| **wind** *(a clock)* | tighten the spring | *It's old; you have to **wind** it.* |
| **strike** | sound the hour | *The clock **struck** twelve.* — *strike, struck, struck* |
| **chime** | sound a tune, not a count | *It **chimes** on the quarter hour.* |
| **stop** | stop running | *The clock **stopped** at 3:40.* |
| **set an alarm for** | ✗ *on*, ✗ *at* | ***Set an alarm for** six.* |
| **the alarm goes off** | ✗ *turns on*, ✗ *rings up* | *My alarm **goes off** at six.* |

*Spring forward, fall back* is the American mnemonic for daylight saving: the clocks go ahead an hour in spring and back an hour in fall.

### The idioms

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **tell time** | read a clock — *He's six; he can't **tell time** yet.* American; British is *tell the time*. |
| **clock in / clock out**, **punch the clock** | record your arrival and departure at work |
| **around the clock** | continuously, day and night |
| **against the clock** | under time pressure — and **beat the clock**, finish in time |
| **run out the clock** | waste the remaining time deliberately |
| **like clockwork** | with perfect regularity |
| **watch the clock** | be impatient for the day to end — hence *a clock-watcher* |
| **the eleventh hour** | the last possible moment |

## Every form, and how it sounds

| Form | Said | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **o'clock** | /əˈklɑk/ | One word, one apostrophe. The *o'* is a schwa, never /oʊ/. |
| **quarter** | /ˈkwɔrtər/ | Two syllables, the vowel of *war*. Both *r*'s are pronounced in General American. |
| **half** | /hæf/ | The *l* is silent. *Half past* runs together as /ˈhæfpæst/. |
| **hour** | /ˈaʊər/ | Silent *h*, so ***an*** *hour*, never ✗ *a hour*. |
| **minute** *(noun)* | /ˈmɪnɪt/ | The adjective *minute* "tiny" is /maɪˈnut/ — same spelling, different word. |
| **a.m.** | /ˌeɪ ˈɛm/ | Two letter names, stress on the second. |
| **p.m.** | /ˌpi ˈɛm/ | Same. The Latin is never said aloud. |
| **noon** | /nun/ | |
| **midnight** | /ˈmɪdnaɪt/ | |
| **oh** *(for 0)* | /oʊ/ | 6:05 = *six oh five*. Never *zero* on a civilian clock. |
| **forty** vs **fourteen** | /ˈfɔrɾi/ vs /fɔrˈtin/ | 6:40 against 6:14 — the commonest mishearing in the language. |
| **thirty** | /ˈθɜrɾi/ | The *t* flaps: it sounds close to *thirdy*. |
| **twenty** | /ˈtwɛni/ | The *t* drops after *n* in ordinary speech: *twenny*. |
| **fifteen** | /fɪfˈtin/ | Final stress, clean /t/. |
| **fifty** | /ˈfɪfti/ | First stress. |
| **to** *(in* ten to seven*)* | /tə/ | Reduced to a schwa, which is why it can be mistaken for *of*. |
| **of** *(in* ten of seven*)* | /ə/ | Reduced to almost nothing — *ten'a seven*. |

**The *-teen* / *-ty* problem deserves its own line**, because it is the one that actually causes missed flights. *Fourteen* has stress on the **second** syllable and a clean /t/; *forty* has stress on the **first** and a flapped /ɾ/ that sounds like a soft *d*. So 6:14 is *six four-TEEN* and 6:40 is *six FOR-dee*. When it matters, Americans disambiguate by saying *six one four* or *twenty minutes to seven* instead. The stress pattern is covered in [Pronunciation](../../10-pronunciation/README.md).

## For Russian speakers

Russian and English both read a clock face, and they read it in **opposite directions on the second half of the hour**. That single fact generates most of the errors below.

**1. The half hour is inverted. This is the commonest time error a Russian speaker makes.**

«Полседьмого» — «половина седьмого» — is literally "half of the seventh [hour]", meaning half of the stretch between six and seven has gone. It is **6:30**. English names the hour that has already struck, not the one being filled:

> «Полседьмого» = **6:30** = *half past **six*** = *six thirty*  
> ✗ *half past seven* — that is 7:30, a full hour late

The two numbers are **never the same**: Russian says seven, English says six. A mnemonic that works — ***half past six* keeps the six you can still see on the clock**, because the hour hand has not reached seven yet. Better still, avoid the construction: **say *six thirty***, which is the American default anyway and cannot be got backwards. British *half six* is no help either — it is not American, and it removes the *past* that at least pointed in a direction.

**2. Counting down to the next hour transfers for free.**

This is the good news. Russian «без» + genitive maps directly onto English *to*:

| Russian | English | Clock |
|---|---|---|
| без пяти семь | five **to** seven | 6:55 |
| без десяти семь | ten **to** seven | 6:50 |
| без двадцати семь | twenty **to** seven | 6:40 |
| без четверти семь | a quarter **to** seven | 6:45 |

Same direction, same hour named, same logic. What does not transfer is the word: «без» is *without*, and ✗ *without twenty seven* is not English.

**3. Russian uses an ordinal and a genitive. English uses a cardinal and nothing.**

«Половина седьмого» has an ordinal in the genitive — *of the seventh*. Learners carry both across, and both are wrong:

> ✗ *half past **of** six* → *half past six* &middot; ✗ *a quarter past **of** six* → *a quarter past six*  
> ✗ *half of the seventh* → *six thirty* &middot; ✗ *half past the **seventh*** → *half past **six***

English clock hours are plain cardinals — *six*, *seven*, *twelve* — with no article, no ordinal, and no preposition attached.

**4. «В шесть часов» → *at six o'clock*, with no *of* and no plural.**

«Часов» is a noun in the genitive plural, and it tempts learners into producing a noun in English too:

> ✓ *at **six o'clock*** &middot; ✓ *at **six***  
> ✗ *at six of clock* &middot; ✗ *at six a clock* &middot; ✗ *at six o'clocks* &middot; ✗ *at six hours* &middot; ✗ *at six of the clock*

*O'clock* is not a noun and does not pluralize, take an article, or take a preposition. And the preposition in front is **at**, always: «в шесть» → *at six*, never ✗ *in six* and never ✗ *on six*. The point-in-time *at* is at [at](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/01-core-in-on-at.md#at).

**5. Russian has no dummy subject. English requires one.**

«Сейчас шесть часов» has no subject at all, and «Шесть часов» on its own is a complete sentence. English needs *it*:

> ✗ *Now six o'clock.* &middot; ✗ *Now is six o'clock.* &middot; ✗ *Is six o'clock.*  
> ✓ ***It's** six o'clock.* / ✓ ***It's** six.*

The same empty *it* runs the weather (*It's raining*) and distance (*It's two miles*). It means nothing; it is there because an English sentence must have a subject.

**6. Asking the time.**

| Russian | English | Not |
|---|---|---|
| Сколько времени? | **What time is it?** | ✗ *How much time is it?* |
| Который час? | **What time is it?** | ✗ *Which hour is it?* &middot; ✗ *What hour is it?* |
| Не подскажете, сколько времени? | **Do you have the time?** | ✗ *Do you have time?* |
| У вас есть время? | **Do you have time?** — are you free | a different question |
| Во сколько? | **What time?** / **When?** | ✗ *In how much?* |

**7. The 24-hour clock is normal in Russia and abnormal in American speech.**

Russian writes 19:00 and says «в девятнадцать ноль-ноль» in an announcement, «в семь вечера» in conversation. American English does neither in daily life:

> ✗ *The movie starts at nineteen o'clock.* → *at seven p.m.* / *at seven in the evening*  
> ✗ *I'll be there at nineteen hours.* → *at seven*

Read a 24-hour display the way an American does: convert, then say the civilian time.

**8. The four Russian times of day do not match the four English ones.**

| Russian | English | Careful |
|---|---|---|
| в девять **утра** | at nine **in the morning** | |
| в два часа **дня** | at two **in the afternoon** | ✗ *in the day* |
| в семь **вечера** | at seven **in the evening** | |
| в одиннадцать **ночи** | at eleven **at night** | *at* night, not ✗ *in the night* |
| в три часа **ночи** | at three **in the morning** | English switches to *morning* after midnight — ✗ *three at night* |

The boundary that moves is the one at midnight. Russian «ночи» stretches to dawn; English *night* effectively stops at twelve, and 1 a.m. through 4 a.m. are *in the morning* however dark it is.

**9. Prepositions around the clock.**

> «в шесть» → **at** six &middot; «к шести» → **by** six &middot; «до шести» → **until** / **till** six  
> «с шести до восьми» → **from** six **to** eight &middot; «после шести» → **after** six  
> «через час» → **in** an hour — ✗ *after an hour*, ✗ *through an hour*  
> «час назад» → an hour **ago** &middot; «за час» → **in** an hour *(it took me an hour)*

«Через час» is the one that catches everybody: the English word is *in*. *I'll call you in an hour.*

**10. «Ровно в шесть» has three English equivalents.**

> at six **sharp** &middot; at **exactly** six &middot; at six **on the dot**

Note the position: *sharp* and *on the dot* follow the time, *exactly* precedes it. ✗ *at sharp six*.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *It's **half past seven**.* (for 6:30) | *It's **half past six**.* / *It's **six thirty**.* | English names the hour that has passed, not the one being filled. |
| ✗ *half past **of** six* | *half past six* | No preposition between *past* and the hour. |
| ✗ *at six **of clock*** | *at six **o'clock*** | *O'clock* is one word and not a noun. |
| ✗ *six **thirty o'clock*** | *six thirty* | *O'clock* is for whole hours only. |
| ✗ *five **o'clock p.m.*** | *five p.m.* / *five o'clock in the evening* | *O'clock* and *p.m.* do the same job. |
| ✗ *at 6 **a.m. in the morning*** | *at 6 a.m.* / *at six in the morning* | Say it once. |
| ✗ *It's **six five**.* (for 6:05) | *It's **six oh five**.* | Minutes under ten take *oh*. |
| ✗ *It's **six oh oh**.* | *It's **six** / **six o'clock**.* | A whole hour has no minute reading. |
| ✗ ***Now is** six o'clock.* | ***It's** six o'clock.* | English requires the dummy subject *it*. |
| ✗ *Do you know **what time is it**?* | *Do you know **what time it is**?* | Embedded questions keep statement order. |
| ✗ *Do you have **time**?* (meaning the clock) | *Do you have **the** time?* | Without *the*, you are asking if someone is free. |
| ✗ *at **nineteen o'clock*** | *at seven p.m.* | *O'clock* takes 1–12 only; 24-hour time is read differently. |
| ✗ *The meeting is at **18:00 p.m.*** | *at 18:00* / *at 6 p.m.* | The 24-hour clock already says which half of the day. |
| ✗ *We're open **from 9–5**.* | *from 9 to 5* / *9–5* | A dash replaces *from… to…*; never use both. |
| ✗ *My watch is five minutes **quick**.* | *five minutes **fast*** | *Fast* and *slow* are the clock words. |
| ✗ *The alarm **turns on** at six.* | *The alarm **goes off** at six.* | Fixed phrasal verb. |
| ✗ *Set an alarm **on** six.* | *Set an alarm **for** six.* | *For*, always. |
| ✗ *I'll call you **after an hour**.* | *I'll call you **in an hour**.* | «Через час» is *in*, not *after*. |
| ✗ *It's **three at night**.* | *It's **three in the morning**.* | After midnight, English says *morning*. |
| ✗ *12 p.m.* meaning midnight | *12 a.m.* / **midnight** | *p.m.* is afternoon, so *12 p.m.* is noon. |
| ✗ *at **sharp** six* | *at six **sharp*** | *Sharp* follows the time. |
| ✗ *It's **half six**.* (American context) | *It's **six thirty**.* | *Half six* is British and is not used in the US. |

## Quick reference

**The face, and where the pivot is.**

```
                         12
                  :55           :05
             :50                     :10
                     ← to | past →
         :45          of  |  after        :15
                    till  |
             :40                     :20
                  :35           :25
                         :30

    :00        six o'clock  /  six
    :01–:29    ... past / after SIX      the hour behind you
    :30        half past SIX  /  six thirty
    :31–:59    ... to / of / till SEVEN  the hour ahead of you
```

**The three rules that cover everything.**

1. **Hour, then minutes.** *Six forty-five.* This reading works for all sixty minutes, is the American default, and can never be got backwards. Learn it first and the clock is solved.
2. **The analog reading pivots at :30.** Before the half hour you count *past* / *after* the hour behind you; after it you count *to* / *of* / *till* the hour ahead. It works only for multiples of five.
3. **Say each thing once.** *O'clock* or *a.m.*, not both. *a.m.* or *in the morning*, not both. *from… to…* or a dash, not both.

**Choosing a reading in one question.**

```
   Are the minutes a multiple of five?

   NO ──── digital only              6:37 → six thirty-seven

   YES ─── Is it :00, :15, :30 or :45?

           NO  ──  digital is safer   6:20 → six twenty
           YES ──  either one         6:45 → six forty-five
                                            a quarter to seven
```

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **six forty-five** &middot; **a quarter to seven** | Digital names the hour behind; analog counts down to the hour ahead. |
| 2 | **seven oh five** — or *five after seven* | Minutes under ten take *oh*, never *zero* and never nothing. |
| 3 | **nine thirty-eight** | Not a multiple of five, so there is no analog reading at all. |
| 4 | **six thirty** / **half past six** | Russian names the hour being filled; English names the hour already struck. |
| 5 | *It's **half past seven**.* | No *of* between *past* and the hour. |
| 6 | *The store opens at **9 a.m.*** — or *at nine o'clock in the morning* | *O'clock* and *a.m.* cannot combine. |
| 7 | *Do you have time?* asks whether you are free. *Do you have **the** time?* asks what the clock says. | The definite article carries the whole difference. |
| 8 | **thirteen forty-five** (*hours*) = **1:45 p.m.** | Minutes are read as a plain number; subtract 12 after noon. |
| 9 | *Do you know **what time it is**?* | An embedded question keeps statement word order. |

| 11 | **a nine-to-five job** | Hyphenated when it modifies a noun. |
| 12 | *My watch is five minutes **fast**.* | Clocks are *fast* and *slow*; they *gain* and *lose* time. |
| 13 | **about six** &middot; **around six** &middot; **sixish** &middot; *(also* almost six, going on six*)* — and **six o'clock sharp** &middot; **six on the dot** *(also* at exactly six*)* | *Sharp* and *on the dot* follow the time; *exactly* precedes it. |
| 14 | ***It's** a quarter to eight.* | English needs the dummy subject *it*. |
| 15 | *(at) **three in the morning*** | After midnight English says *morning*, not ✗ *at night*. |
| 16 | **6:40** — the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic. | *Of* is a regional alternative to *to*; *till* is the Southern and Midland one. |
| 17 | *We're open **from 9 to 5**.* / *We're open **9 a.m.–5 p.m.*** | A dash replaces *from… to…*; using both is the error. |
