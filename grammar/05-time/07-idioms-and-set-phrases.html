Idioms and set phrases#

The ninety-six fixed expressions American English uses to say that something happened early, late, fast, slowly, often, rarely, long ago or not yet — each with its register, and with the handful whose Russian counterpart points the wrong way.

← Time · All grammar topics

Everything else in this topic is a system: prepositions chosen by rule, tenses chosen by meaning, clauses built from parts. This section is a list, because these expressions are not built from parts at all. In the nick of time contains a noun nobody uses anywhere else. Day in, day out is ungrammatical if you read it as a sentence. Time was, you could park here has no subject. They are single words that happen to be spelled with spaces in them, and they are learned the way words are learned — whole.

That does not make them arbitrary, and three properties run through the entire list.

They are frozen, down to the small words. The article, the number, and the preposition are part of the expression and cannot be adjusted: in the nick of time but at a moment's notice; once in a blue moon but ahead of schedule with no article at all; the good old days, never ✗ the good old times; a race against time with no the, but against the clock with one. Nearly every learner error in this file is a small word moved or dropped, not a wrong idea.

The possessive is the one slot that moves. A dozen of these contain your, and your is a placeholder that agrees with whoever is being talked about: take your time → he took his time; twiddling your thumbs → they were twiddling their thumbs; worth your while → make it worth my while; on your own time → she does it on her own time. Leaving your in place, or swapping in the, is the tell: ✗ twiddling the thumbs.

Register is the whole difference between most near-synonyms. At the last minute and at the eleventh hour describe the same moment; the first is what happened to your dry cleaning and the second is what happened to the budget negotiations. In a flash is current, quick as a flash sounds like somebody's grandmother, and in the blink of an eye is what a news anchor says. Choosing the wrong one is not a grammatical error — it is the kind of error that makes fluent English sound borrowed. Every entry below is labeled everyday, informal, formal or dated, and where American and British usage split, the entry says so.

One grammatical note that costs points on tests. Several of these end in a time clause, and a time clause takes the present tense for future meaning, exactly as in the tenses: when the time comes, till the cows come home, strike while the iron is hot — never ✗ when the time will come.

Being early and being on time#

English has far more ways to praise earliness than to excuse lateness, and most of them are neutral enough for a work email. The two that are not interchangeable at all are on time and in time, and they get their own treatment below.

ahead of time · ahead of schedule — everyday. Before the moment something was due. Ahead of time measures against a deadline or an expectation; ahead of schedule measures against a published plan and takes no article — ✗ ahead of the schedule.

Send me the questions ahead of time and I'll have something ready. They poured the foundation two weeks ahead of schedule. I always get to the airport well ahead of time.

in good time · with time to spare — everyday; in good time* leans British. Early enough that nobody has to rush. With time to spare* is the American default and is the more concrete of the two: it says a margin was left over. Do not confuse in good time with all in good time, which is about patience, not earliness.

We got there with time to spare and had coffee first. She finished the exam with twenty minutes to spare. Get your application in in good time — the portal closes at five.

bright and early · at the crack of dawn — everyday; the second is deliberate hyperbole. Early in the morning. Bright and early is cheerful and collocates with arriving and starting; at the crack of dawn is a complaint, and it is used for seven in the morning as readily as for five.

Be here bright and early — we start loading at six. He was up at the crack of dawn to beat the traffic. They showed up bright and early with doughnuts.

first thing — everyday. At the very start of the morning, before anything else. It takes no preposition in front of it, which is what trips learners: I'll call you first thing, never ✗ at first thing. The full form is first thing in the morning, and the mirror image is last thing at night.

I'll email them first thing tomorrow. Take the pill first thing in the morning, before you eat.

on the dot · on the nose — informal. Exactly. On the dot follows a stated time and only a time; on the nose also follows a number, a guess or an estimate, and carries a little swagger.

She called at nine on the dot. The doors open at seven on the dot, and they do not hold them. He guessed the weight on the nose.

like clockwork — everyday. With perfect regularity, every single time. It attaches to a verb (run, go, work, arrive) and describes reliability, not speed.

The 7:15 comes like clockwork. The whole event went like clockwork this year.

punctual to a fault — neutral to formal. So punctual that it has stopped being a virtue and become a personality trait. The template to a fault works with any good quality taken too far — generous to a fault, honest to a fault — and it is faint praise, never an insult.

My father-in-law is punctual to a fault; he waits in the car. She's punctual to a fault and expects the same from everybody else.

On time versus in time#

This is the pair Russian speakers get wrong most often, because Russian covers both with вовремя. There is no context in which the choice between them is free.

On time = punctual. It measures the event against a schedule and answers the question was it late? Its opposite is late.

In time = early enough. It measures the event against a purpose and answers the question was it soon enough to do X? Its opposite is too late. It is nearly always followed by for + noun or to + verb — and when it stands alone, that purpose is understood from the sentence.

The flight landed on time. — it landed at 4:40, as printed We landed in time to make the connection. — early enough to catch the next one He pays his rent on time every month. — by the first, as agreed Get the check to them in time to avoid a late fee. — before the penalty starts

Put them in the same sentence and the difference stops being subtle:

The train was on time, but we still weren't in time for the ceremony. She arrived on time for a meeting that had already been moved. — punctual, and it did her no good

Three things that come with the pair.

1. Just in time is the emphatic form — with no margin whatever — and there is no ✗ just on time. The intensifier for on time is right: right on time.

They got the roof on just in time; it rained that night. He walked in right on time, which surprised everybody.

2. In time has a second, unrelated sense: eventually. With no for and no to, and usually at the front of the clause, in time means "after a period has passed" — Russian со временем. Context separates the two senses completely, but the sense exists and learners meet it in reading.

In time, she came around to the idea. The bruise will fade in time.

3. Only on time becomes an adjective, and it takes a hyphen before the noun: on-time performance, on-time delivery, an on-time arrival rate of 82 percent. There is no ✗ in-time adjective; the adjective for "done at the right moment" is timely — a timely warning, timely payment.

✗ The train arrived in time, at 6:02 exactly. → The train arrived on time. ✗ I hope we get there on time for the movie. → …in time for the movie. ✗ Did you finish the report in time? → …on time? — if you mean by the deadline you were given

Being late and being rushed#

This group divides by how much is at stake. Running behind is about a dentist's office; at the eleventh hour is about a treaty. Using the big one for the small thing is a joke, and using the small one for the big thing sounds careless.

behind schedule · running behind — everyday; running behind* is spoken. Later than planned. Behind schedule* is the written, institutional form and takes no article. Running behind is what a person says about their own morning, usually with an apology attached, and it is happening right now.

The project is three months behind schedule. Sorry — I'm running behind. Can we push it to four? The doctor is running behind today, about forty minutes.

at the last minute · at the eleventh hour — everyday / formal and journalistic. With almost no time left. They describe the same moment at two registers: a cancelled dinner is last-minute, a rescued merger is at the eleventh hour. Note the articles — at the last minute, never ✗ in the last minute — and the attributive form, which is hyphenated: a last-minute change.

They cancelled at the last minute, as usual. I always pack at the last minute. The governor signed the bill at the eleventh hour, minutes before the session closed.

in the nick of time — everyday. Just barely soon enough — and, unlike the two above, always with a bad outcome avoided. Nothing ordinary happens in the nick of time; the phrase implies a near miss.

The fire department got there in the nick of time. She grabbed his arm in the nick of time.

down to the wire — informal. Still undecided at the final moment. It comes from horse racing and is used of contests, negotiations and deadlines, normally with go or come.

The vote went down to the wire. It came down to the wire, but we shipped on Friday.

cutting it close — informal. Leaving dangerously little margin. It is a judgment on somebody's planning, and it is usually said before the outcome is known. The British form is cutting it fine.

A nine o'clock flight and a seven-thirty cab is cutting it close. We made it, but we were cutting it close.

crunch time · against the clock · a race against time — informal / everyday / journalistic. The stretch when pressure peaks, the way you work in it, and the whole situation seen from outside. Note the articles: a race against time has no the, and against the clock has one.

It's crunch time — nobody's going home before this ships. The crews are working against the clock to reopen the bridge. Rescuers are in a race against time to reach the last two miners.

no time to lose — everyday. Act now and do not stop to discuss it. It appears as there's no time to lose or as a bare fragment, and it never takes a plural: ✗ no times to lose.

Get your coat — there's no time to lose. No time to lose, folks. Load it up.

better late than never — everyday proverb. Late is still better than not at all. Americans say it in two directions: as genuine reassurance, and as a dry greeting to somebody who has just walked in twenty minutes late.

"Sorry, traffic." "Better late than never." He finished his degree at fifty-one — better late than never.

fashionably late — informal, jocular. Deliberately a little late to a social event, because arriving first is worse. It applies only to parties and never to work, and it is always said with a wink.

Nobody shows up at eight; everyone's fashionably late. We arrived fashionably late and were still the first ones there.

Speed#

Two different meanings hide in this group. Some of these say an action was fast; others say the moment it happens is near. In a flash is speed; any second now is nearness; in no time is both, and the sentence decides which.

in no time — everyday. Very soon, or very quickly — and often it is hyperbole for "faster than you feared." In no time flat is the emphatic version.

The soup will be ready in no time. He had the tire changed in no time flat.

in a flash · in the blink of an eye · quick as a flash — everyday / everyday / informal and dated. Instantly. In a flash is the plain current one. In the blink of an eye is more literary and often marks how fast a long stretch of time went. Quick as a flash is almost always about a spoken reply and sounds like an older speaker.

I'll be back in a flash. Four years went by in the blink of an eye. Quick as a flash, she said, "Then why did you call?"

in a heartbeat — everyday. Without a second's hesitation. It is about willingness, not speed, and it almost always follows would: it answers an offer that has not been made.

If they offered me that job, I'd take it in a heartbeat. She'd move back to Portland in a heartbeat.

before you know it — everyday. Sooner than you expect. It is used to reassure somebody that an unpleasant wait will be short, and it is a fixed second person: not ✗ before he knows it in this sense.

You'll be done before you know it. Before you know it, it'll be December again.

any second now · any day now — informal / everyday. Imminently. Any second (or minute, or moment) is literal and everybody is watching for it; any day now covers a longer horizon and usually carries impatience about something overdue. Both are covered in Russian by вот-вот.

The results should come up any second now. They've been saying the permit will clear any day now since March.

at a moment's notice · on short notice — everyday. With little or no advance warning. At a moment's notice is the extreme case — no warning at all — and describes readiness; on short notice describes a request made late, and it is the American form. The British say at short notice, which is not an error but is not American either.

She's on call and has to be able to travel at a moment's notice. Thanks for covering for me on short notice. They found a replacement speaker on short notice.

right off the bat — informal, American. Immediately, at the very start, before anything else happened. It is baseball, and a British reader will hear it as an Americanism.

Right off the bat he asked what the salary was. I didn't like the idea right off the bat, and I still don't.

Slowness and waiting#

The first four of these are things you say to somebody who is impatient. The last five are what that somebody is doing while they wait, and all of them are unflattering.

take your time — everyday. Do not hurry. The possessive agrees with the subject, and the phrase has a second, sarcastic life when it is said to somebody who is already taking far too long.

Take your time — the reservation isn't until eight. He took his time getting back to me, which told me everything. "Sorry, I'm still looking." "Take your time."

in due course — formal. Eventually, through the normal process, and the speaker will not say when. It belongs to letters, notices and bureaucracies, and in conversation it sounds like a brush-off — which it frequently is.

You will be notified of the decision in due course. The records will be released in due course.

sooner or later — everyday. At some point, and it cannot be avoided. It is a warning as often as a prediction, and the order is frozen: ✗ later or sooner.

Sooner or later somebody was going to notice. You'll have to tell her sooner or later.

all in good time — everyday, slightly dated. It will happen when it should — so stop asking. A complete sentence on its own, and gently patronizing whichever way it is meant.

"When do we find out?" "All in good time." She'll tell you herself, all in good time.

a watched pot never boils — everyday proverb. Waiting makes time crawl, so go do something else. It is advice about attention, not about cooking.

Stop refreshing your inbox. A watched pot never boils. He sat by the phone all afternoon — a watched pot never boils.

time drags — everyday. Time is passing painfully slowly. It is the exact opposite of time flies, and the two are often used in the same breath.

The last hour of a shift drags. Time drags when you're waiting for news.

killing time · twiddling your thumbs · cooling your heels — everyday / informal / informal and journalistic. Three ways of doing nothing. Killing time is voluntary and harmless — you have an hour and you fill it. Twiddling your thumbs is involuntary and faintly pathetic: you would work if anyone let you. Cooling your heels means somebody with power is making you wait, and it carries a grievance.

We killed time in a bookstore until the movie started. I sat there for an hour twiddling my thumbs while they looked for the file. Reporters were left cooling their heels in the lobby for most of the afternoon.

Duration and endurance#

Every one of these exaggerates. That is their job: nobody works literally around the clock, and for ages is routinely used of a three-week wait.

around the clock · 24/7 — everyday / informal. Continuously, with no break. Around the clock works in a news report; 24/7 is speech, advertising and email, and it is said twenty-four seven. Before a noun, around the clock takes hyphens: around-the-clock coverage.

Crews worked around the clock to clear the runway. The pharmacy on Ninth is open 24/7. She's on her phone 24/7.

day in, day out · for hours on end — everyday. Relentless repetition. Day in, day out means every day without variation and always sounds weary; the comma is standard and the longer day in and day out is also correct. For hours on end means hour after uninterrupted hour, and on end also attaches to days, weeks and months.

The same three sandwiches, day in, day out. He practiced for hours on end and never complained. It rained for days on end that spring.

till the cows come home — informal. Indefinitely — and pointlessly. It only ever attaches to an activity that will not produce a result: you can argue, wait or complain till the cows come home, and the phrase says it will change nothing. Note the time clause: come, never ✗ will come.

You can argue about it till the cows come home; the vote was final. We could sit here till the cows come home waiting for him to apologize.

for ages · donkey's years — informal; donkey's years* is British. A very long time, by the speaker's impatient standards. For ages* is completely at home in American speech. Donkey's years is not: an American reader will understand it from context but will hear it as British, and it does not belong in American writing.

I haven't seen her for ages. We waited for ages and nobody came. British: I've had this coat for donkey's years.

in living memory · since time immemorial — formal; the second is often jocular. Two spans, both large. In living memory is bounded by the oldest person alive and is the phrase used for record weather and record events. Since time immemorial means "as far back as anyone knows," and outside legal and historical writing it is nearly always a joke about a habit or a family rule.

It was the worst flood in living memory. For the first time in living memory, the lake froze all the way across. My mother has been making that same casserole since time immemorial.

Rarity and frequency#

The first three are about how often something happens; the last four are about how the odds fall. Once and for all sits in this group only by its spelling — it is about finality, not frequency.

once in a blue moon · few and far between — everyday. Rare. Once in a blue moon modifies a verb and describes how often something happens; few and far between is a predicate adjective phrase and describes the things themselves, so it takes a plural subject and a form of be.

He calls his brother once in a blue moon. We go out once in a blue moon now that the kids are small. Jobs like that are few and far between.

every now and again — everyday. Occasionally. Every now and then and every so often mean exactly the same thing and are interchangeable with it; from time to time is the slightly more formal member of the set.

Every now and again she'll send a postcard. I still think about it every now and then.

time after time · nine times out of ten · more often than not — everyday; the last is neutral to formal. Repetition and odds. Time after time means repeatedly and nearly always carries exasperation. Nine times out of ten claims about ninety percent and is spoken; more often than not claims a bare majority and is what you write.

Time after time he promised to fix it, and time after time he didn't. Nine times out of ten the problem is the cable. More often than not, the meeting ends early.

once and for all — everyday. Finally and permanently, so that the matter never comes back. It attaches to verbs of settling, deciding and ending, and the order of the words is frozen.

Let's settle this once and for all. She quit smoking once and for all in 2019.

The past#

Four of these name a period. The other five make a claim about it — that it was better, that it is finished, or that it no longer matters.

back in the day · in those days — informal and American / everyday. An earlier period. Back in the day names no period at all: it means "in the era I am nostalgic about," the hearer works out which one, and the noun stays singular. In those days is neutral and requires that the period has already been named in the conversation.

Back in the day you could get a whole meal for four dollars. My uncle played semi-pro back in the day. They moved here in 1962. In those days the highway didn't exist.

a while back · way back when — informal; a while back* is American. Some time ago. A while back* is vague on purpose and covers anything from a month to a few years; a while ago is the neutral equivalent. Way back when pushes it much further into the past and usually ends the clause.

I ran into him a while back at the hardware store. We talked about this a while back, didn't we? The building was a cannery way back when.

the good old days — everyday. The past, remembered fondly and selectively. It is always plural, always takes the, and in American English it is used ironically about as often as it is used sincerely. Never ✗ the good old times.

He's always going on about the good old days. Back in the good old days we did all of this on paper — and it took a week.

water under the bridge · ancient history — everyday / informal. Over and done with. Water under the bridge is specifically a grievance that the speaker is declining to reopen; it is uncountable and takes no article. Ancient history is broader — any fact that has stopped being relevant — and it is often dismissive of the person who brought it up.

We had our fights, but that's all water under the bridge now. "What about the thing in March?" "Water under the bridge." Whatever he said at the time is ancient history.

a thing of the past · time was — neutral to formal / dated but alive in speech. Two ways of saying that something has stopped. A thing of the past is a predicate: X is no longer done anywhere. Time was is a frozen fragment of older English that still turns up in ordinary American conversation, followed by a comma and a clause — it is there was a time when, with the words rubbed off.

Cursive handwriting in schools is largely a thing of the past. Paper tickets are a thing of the past. Time was, you could park on this block for free.

The future#

The register split in this group is sharp. Down the road belongs in speech and in the short term belongs in a report, and neither survives being swapped for the other.

down the road · down the line — informal, American. At some later point. They are near-identical; down the line adds a faint sense of stages being passed through on the way, which is why further down the line is commoner than further down the road. Both are strongly American — the British say down the track or simply later on.

We can revisit the pricing down the road. That decision is going to cause problems down the line. Somewhere down the road they'll have to replace the whole roof.

in the long run · in the short term — everyday / neutral to formal. The two ends of a horizon. In the long run is the idiom and is about how things finally turn out; in the long term is the flatter business equivalent. In the short term is the standard opener for a concession — it grants a near-term cost and points past it.

Buying the better one is cheaper in the long run. In the short term the change will slow everybody down; in the long run it saves a day a week.

sooner rather than later · any day now — everyday. Soon, with an edge. Sooner rather than later is a polite push: it says the delay is itself the problem, and it is the phrase managers use instead of naming a deadline. Any day now is often sarcastic about something long overdue.

We need an answer sooner rather than later. I'd fix that step sooner rather than later. The check is supposed to arrive any day now.

when the time comes · the time is ripe — everyday / formal and literary. Both postpone. When the time comes is an ordinary time clause and takes the present tense — ✗ when the time will come. The time is ripe announces that conditions are finally right and is rare in speech; in conversation it sounds like a speech.

We'll cross that bridge when the time comes. She'll know what to do when the time comes. The committee decided the time was ripe for a second vote.

time will tell — everyday. We cannot know yet, and the speaker declines to guess. It stands alone as a full sentence, it is the direct equivalent of время покажет, and only time will tell is the emphatic version.

Whether it was the right call — time will tell. Only time will tell if the repair holds.

Time as a resource#

English talks about time the way it talks about money — you spend it, waste it, save it, buy it and borrow it — and this group is where that metaphor becomes literal vocabulary. Most of it is workplace English.

time is money — everyday proverb. Delay costs, so get to the point. Americans say it to justify impatience, and it is close enough to время — деньги to transfer with no adjustment.

Let's skip the preamble — time is money. Time is money, and that meeting cost us four thousand dollars.

buy time · borrowed time — everyday. Two different transactions. To buy time is to create delay on purpose, usually to avoid deciding; it is neutral in a negotiation and damning in a scandal. To be on borrowed time is to be continuing past the point where you should already have ended — a failing machine, a doomed manager, a dying patient — and the phrase is always on borrowed time.

The filing was meant to buy time while they found a buyer. He's just trying to buy himself a few days. That water heater has been on borrowed time since we moved in.

make up for lost time — everyday. Do intensively what the delay prevented. It is about compensation, not speed, and it is used as warmly of a reunion as it is of a schedule.

They left an hour late and tried to make up for lost time on the interstate. He retired at sixty and has been making up for lost time ever since.

on my own time · on company time — everyday, American. Whose hours the activity comes out of. This is an American pair, and it is a trap: the British in my own time means at my own pace, which is a different claim entirely. In American English, I did it on my own time means unpaid and after hours.

I wrote most of it on my own time, over two weekends. You can do that on your own time, not on company time. Half the department was shopping online on company time.

worth your while — everyday. Worth the time it will cost you. It occurs in two shapes: a plain predicate, and the transactional make it worth your while, which promises a reward and — depending on who is speaking — hints at one that should not be written down.

The drive is long, but the museum is worth your while. Stay till Friday and I'll make it worth your while.

time well spent · a waste of time — everyday. The verdict, either way. Both are noun phrases used predicatively; time well spent takes no article and a waste of time requires one — ✗ it was waste of time.

Two hours in that workshop was time well spent. The whole meeting was a waste of time. Don't waste your time arguing with him — it's a waste of time.

Proverbs and sayings#

A proverb is quoted, not composed, which means it cannot be adjusted: the words are fixed, the tense is fixed, and half of a well-known one is enough — Americans routinely say well, a stitch in time… and stop. Each one below is followed by what it actually claims, because several are used for situations their literal wording does not describe.

time flies — everyday. Time passes faster than you notice. It stands alone, and the longer forms are time flies when you're having fun (said of an evening that went quickly) and the Latin-flavored how time flies.

Time flies — she's in high school already. Is it really nine? Time flies.

time heals all wounds — everyday. Grief fades on its own, so wait. It is said as consolation, and it is said sardonically by people who do not believe it.

Give it a year. Time heals all wounds. Time heals all wounds, or so they tell me.

there's no time like the present — everyday. Do it now, while you are thinking about it. It is the standard nudge toward starting an unpleasant task, and it is often said by the person who will not be doing it.

You might as well call them now — there's no time like the present. "I should really clean the garage." "No time like the present."

a stitch in time saves nine — everyday, slightly dated. A small repair now prevents a large one later. The arithmetic is the point, and the first half is enough to invoke the whole.

Get the leak looked at — a stitch in time saves nine. Two hundred dollars now against four thousand next winter: a stitch in time.

the early bird gets the worm — everyday. Whoever starts first takes the prize. It is about being early rather than being good, which is why it turns up in sales and in parking. The American form is gets; catches is the older wording.

Doors open at eight and the good stuff goes fast — the early bird gets the worm. She files her taxes in January. The early bird gets the worm.

strike while the iron is hot — everyday. Act while the opportunity is still open, because it will close. Note the time clause: is, never ✗ will be.

They're interested now. Strike while the iron is hot. He had the offer in hand and didn't strike while the iron was hot.

let sleeping dogs lie — everyday. Leave a settled trouble alone; raising it will only restart it. This is the timing proverb that is not about speed at all — it is about not acting, and it is the natural reply to somebody who wants to reopen an old argument.

I wouldn't bring up the will at Thanksgiving. Let sleeping dogs lie. The audit closed two years ago. Let sleeping dogs lie.

hindsight is 20/20 — everyday, American. What is obvious afterward was invisible at the time. The numbers are read twenty-twenty, from the American notation for normal eyesight, and the phrase is used both to forgive a bad decision and, drily, to dismiss somebody's criticism of one.

Sure, we should have sold in March — hindsight is 20/20. Easy to say now. Hindsight is 20/20.

For Russian speakers#

Most of this file transfers with no trouble, because the metaphors are shared: time flies in Russian too, it is money in Russian too, and лучше поздно, чем никогда is better late than never word for word. The damage is done by a much smaller set — the expressions where a literal translation produces a real English phrase that means something else, so nothing sounds wrong and nobody corrects you.

Two more interference points worth naming.

1. The aspect habit adds a verb that English does not want. Russian often marks completion with a second verb — успеть сделать, закончить делать — and the English idiom has that meaning built in. We made it in time already says you succeeded; ✗ We managed to make it in time to succeed is what the literal instinct produces.

✗ I didn't succeed to finish it on time. → I didn't finish it on time. / I didn't get it done in time. ✗ We had time to arrive before it closed. → We got there in time. / We made it before it closed.

2. Russian proverbs do not survive translation, and English ones do not survive back-translation. Тише едешь — дальше будешь is not the early bird gets the worm and not slow and steady wins the race in every context; куй железо, пока горячо really is strike while the iron is hot, which lulls learners into trying the same trick with the rest. The safe rule: quote only the English proverbs you have actually heard an American use, and translate the others as plain statements.

✗ The train arrived in time, at 6:02.The train arrived on time.On time = punctual. In time = early enough for something.
✗ I hope we get there on time for the movie.…in time for the movie.The movie is a purpose, not a schedule you are being measured against.
✗ We arrived just on time to see the kickoff.…just in time to see the kickoff.The intensifier for on time is right; for in time it is just.
✗ They cancelled in the last minute.…at the last minute.The preposition is part of the phrase.

✗ It was a race against the time.…a race against time.A race against time, but working against the clock.

Quick reference#

The one contrast to memorize.

   on time  =  punctual          measured against a SCHEDULE    opposite: late
   in time  =  early enough      measured against a PURPOSE     opposite: too late

   The flight was on time.          It left at 4:40, as printed.
   We were in time for dinner.      We got there before it started.
   just in time / right on time     the two intensifiers, and they do not swap.

The small words that cannot be changed.

PatternMembers
no articleahead of schedule, behind schedule, on short notice, a race against time, water under the bridge, time well spent, on borrowed time
fixed thein the nick of time, at the last minute, at the eleventh hour, against the clock, around the clock, the good old days, down the road
fixed aat a moment's notice, once in a blue moon, a waste of time, a thing of the past, a while back
possessive that agreestake your time, twiddling your thumbs, cooling your heels, worth your while, on your own time, buy yourself time
present tense insidewhen the time comes, till the cows come home, strike while the iron is hot, before you know it
The three that are British, not American
donkey's years, at short notice (American: on), cutting it fine (American: cutting it close). In good time is understood everywhere but sounds British; Americans say with time to spare.

Registers at a glance. The same moment in four registers:

informal
We were cutting it close, but we made it.
everyday
We got there in the nick of time.
formal
The agreement was reached at the eleventh hour.
dated
Time was, they would have held the train for us.

Six that are whole sentences and are used with nothing else attached: Time flies. Time will tell. There's no time like the present. Better late than never. All in good time. Hindsight is 20/20.

Practice#

A. Choose on time or in time.

  1. The 8:05 is almost never ___.
  2. Did you get to the pharmacy ___ to pick up the prescription?
  3. She pays every invoice ___.
  4. We reached the gate just ___.
  5. The contractor finished ___ for the inspection, but not ___ — he was two days over.

B. Fix the phrase.

  1. ✗ They finished the wing well ahead of the schedule.
  2. ✗ He sat in the waiting room twiddling the thumbs for an hour.
  3. ✗ I'll send it at first thing Monday.
  4. ✗ We'll talk about it when the time will come.
  5. ✗ Honestly, the whole class was waste of time.
  6. ✗ That argument is a water under the bridge now.
  7. ✗ Thanks for filling in at short notice. (make it American)

C. Supply the idiom the register calls for.

  1. A press release about rescue crews digging for survivors, working continuously: "Crews are working ___ ."
  2. A text to a friend who is deciding whether to take a job in Denver: "If they offered me that, I'd go ___ ."
  3. A note in the minutes about a resolution passed at the last possible moment: "The amendment was adopted ___ ."
  4. Said to somebody refreshing a tracking page every ten seconds: "___ ."
  5. Said dryly to a coworker who has just arrived twenty-five minutes late: "___ ."
