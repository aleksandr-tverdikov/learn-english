# Dates, days, and years

> Every way American English names a point on the calendar — the capitalized day and month names, the month/day/year order the rest of the world reads backward, the digits you write and the ordinals you say, and the years read in halves.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

A date is the one corner of English where the written form and the spoken form come apart completely. You write *March 3* and say *March third*. You write *1985* and say *nineteen eighty-five*. You write *3/7/2026* and — if you are in the United States — you mean the seventh of March, while almost everyone else on the planet reads those same nine characters as the third of July.

So this section has two jobs. **What to write** is convention, and the American conventions differ from the Russian ones in three ways: the day and month names take capital letters, the month comes before the day, and the year stands alone with no noun after it. **What to say** follows rules the writing does not show — the digit *3* in a date is read as the ordinal *third*, never as *three*, and the four digits of a year are read as two two-digit numbers. Nothing on the page announces either rule; you have to bring it with you.

Here is the idea underneath everything below:

**A calendar expression is a name, and English capitalizes the names and lowercases the categories.** *Monday* and *March* and *Christmas* are names, so they are capitalized every time, in every position. *Spring*, *century*, *week*, *month*, *year* and *weekend* are categories, so they are not. Russian treats *понедельник* and *март* as categories too, which is why the capital letter is the first thing to fix and the last thing to stop forgetting.

The prepositions that go in front of all this — *on*, *in*, *at*, *by*, *through*, *until* — are [section 3](03-prepositions-of-time.md) and the [preposition catalog](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md); the clock is [section 1](01-telling-the-time.md). This section is the calendar.

## Days of the week

### The seven names

| Day | Said | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Sunday** | /ˈsʌndeɪ/ | the first day of the week on an American calendar |
| **Monday** | /ˈmʌndeɪ/ | |
| **Tuesday** | /ˈtuzdeɪ/ | American /tuz/, not British /tjuz/ |
| **Wednesday** | /ˈwɛnzdeɪ/ | the first *d* is silent — two syllables, not three |
| **Thursday** | /ˈθɜrzdeɪ/ | |
| **Friday** | /ˈfraɪdeɪ/ | |
| **Saturday** | /ˈsætərdeɪ/ | |

The ending `-day` is /deɪ/ in careful speech and /di/ in ordinary speech — *Mondi*, *Tuesdi*, *Sundi*. Both are standard American; the reduced one is what you will actually hear.

**The capital letter is not optional and not stylistic.** It survives in mid-sentence, in an attributive phrase, in a plural, and in a compound:

> We're closed **Monday**. &middot; I hate **Monday** mornings.  
> Three **Mondays** in a row. &middot; That's a **Tuesday**-afternoon problem.

> ✗ *See you on **monday**.* → *See you on **Monday**.*

Each day is also a noun with its own entry — see [Monday](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md#monday) and the rest in the [time and calendar vocabulary](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md).

### Abbreviations

| Day | In prose | Three letters | Two | One |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday | *Sun.* | Sun | Su | S / U |
| Monday | *Mon.* | Mon | Mo | M |
| Tuesday | *Tues.* | Tue | Tu | T |
| Wednesday | *Wed.* | Wed | We | W |
| Thursday | *Thurs.* | Thu | Th | R / Th |
| Friday | *Fri.* | Fri | Fr | F |
| Saturday | *Sat.* | Sat | Sa | S |

Three rules ride along with the table.

- **The period belongs to the prose form.** *Mon.*, *Tues.*, *Thurs.* take a period; the three-letter forms in a table, a calendar grid or a data file do not: *Mon Tue Wed Thu Fri Sat Sun*.
- ***Tues.*** **and** ***Thurs.*** **are the American preference**; *Tue.* and *Thu.* are the computing forms. And **abbreviations do not belong in a sentence** — they are for tables, schedules and notes: ✗ *I'll see you on Mon.* → *on Monday*.

American class schedules use a compressed code worth recognizing: **MWF** is Monday/Wednesday/Friday, **TR** or **TTh** is Tuesday/Thursday. *R* stands in for Thursday because *T* is already spoken for.

### *on Monday*, *Monday*, *Mondays*, *on Mondays*

| Form | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *on Monday* | one particular Monday | *The results come in **on Monday**.* |
| *Monday* (bare) | the same, preposition dropped — American | *The mayor resigned **Tuesday**.* |
| *on Mondays* | every Monday | *She works from home **on Mondays**.* |
| *Mondays* (bare) | the same — American | *We're closed **Sundays**.* |
| *every Monday* | every Monday, emphatic | *He calls **every Monday**.* |
| *every other Monday* | alternate weeks | *Payday is **every other Friday**.* |
| *on a Monday* | on some unspecified Monday | *He was born **on a Monday**.* |

**Dropping the preposition is American.** *I'll call you Monday*, *We're closed Sundays*, *The mayor resigned Tuesday* — all completely ordinary here, and all impossible in British English, which requires *on*. It is the house style of American journalism, where nearly every dateline sentence does it.

**The day name carries no time of its own. The tense does.**

> I saw him **on Monday**. — the Monday just past  
> I'll see him **on Monday**. — the Monday coming  
> I see him **on Mondays**. — every week

That is why the bare plural and the bare singular can look identical in a headline and still be read correctly: the verb settles it.

### *this*, *next*, *last* — and the ambiguity of *next Friday*

| Expression | What it usually means | Safe? |
|---|---|---|
| *this Monday* | the Monday of the current week, usually the one coming | mostly |
| *this coming Monday* | the next Monday to occur | yes |
| *this past Monday* | the most recent Monday | yes |
| *next Monday* | the Monday of next week — **or** the next Monday to occur | **no** |
| *last Monday* | the most recent Monday | mostly |
| *the Monday before last* | two Mondays back | yes |
| *a week from Monday* | Monday plus seven days | yes |
| *Monday the 9th* | the Monday that falls on the 9th | yes — and it is the real fix |

**The first rule is a grammatical one: *this*, *next*, *last* and *every* take no preposition.** They are determiners, and they replace *on* rather than following it.

> ✗ *on next Monday* → ***next Monday***  
> ✗ *in last March* → ***last March***  
> ✗ *on every Friday* → ***every Friday***  
> ✗ *on yesterday* → ***yesterday***

**And then the honest part about *next*.** Suppose today is Tuesday the 3rd. *Next Friday* means either Friday the 6th — the next Friday to come — or Friday the 13th, the Friday belonging to next week. Both readings are live in American English, neither is wrong, and native speakers get this wrong with each other constantly. The same ambiguity runs through *last Friday* on a Monday or Tuesday, though less badly.

There is no rule that resolves it, and any reference that gives you one is inventing it. What speakers actually do is repair it on the spot, and a learner should do the same:

> **Next Friday — the thirteenth**, not this week. &middot; Not this Friday, **the one after**.  
> **A week from Friday.** &middot; Let's say **Friday the 6th**.

**When it matters, say the date.** That one habit removes the whole problem, and it is what careful Americans do without thinking about it.

### *Monday through Friday*

**American English uses *through* to make the last day count.** This is the one place where American writing deliberately spends an extra word to buy precision, and it is worth adopting.

> We're open **Monday through Friday**. — five days, Friday included  
> The office is closed **December 24 through January 2**. — January 2 is closed too  
> The exhibit runs **March 3 through May 18**.

*Monday to Friday* is used in American English too, and nobody will misunderstand a work schedule. But *to* leaves the endpoint genuinely open — British writing has to add *inclusive* to get what *through* gives for free — so in anything that matters, use *through*. There is a fuller treatment in [through](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#through) and the [British forms table](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#british-time-forms-to-recognize-but-not-to-write).

In a table or a sign, the en dash does the same work: **Mon–Fri**, **Mar 3–May 18**. What you cannot do is mix the two systems:

> ✗ *from Monday–Friday* → *from Monday to Friday* / *Monday through Friday* / *Mon–Fri*

## Months and seasons

### The twelve names

| Month | Abbrev. | Said | Days |
|---|---|---|---|
| **January** | *Jan.* | /ˈdʒænjuɛri/ | 31 |
| **February** | *Feb.* | /ˈfɛbjuɛri/ — the first *r* is normally dropped | 28, or 29 |
| **March** | *Mar.* | /mɑrtʃ/ | 31 |
| **April** | *Apr.* | /ˈeɪprəl/ | 30 |
| **May** | — | /meɪ/ | 31 |
| **June** | — | /dʒun/ | 30 |
| **July** | — | /dʒʊˈlaɪ/ — stress on the second syllable | 31 |
| **August** | *Aug.* | /ˈɔgəst/ | 31 |
| **September** | *Sept.* | /sɛpˈtɛmbər/ | 30 |
| **October** | *Oct.* | /ɑkˈtoʊbər/ | 31 |
| **November** | *Nov.* | /noʊˈvɛmbər/ | 30 |
| **December** | *Dec.* | /dɪˈsɛmbər/ | 31 |

**Capitalized, always** — the same rule as the days, and the same error waiting for a Russian speaker.

Three notes on the abbreviations. **May, June and July are never abbreviated**, because there is nothing to cut. **Sept.** is the American four-letter form; *Sep.* belongs to software. And AP style abbreviates a month **only when a specific date follows it** — *Jan. 5*, but *last January* and *January 2026* spelled out. Chicago style spells the month out in running text and abbreviates only in tables and notes. Either is defensible; what is not defensible is switching between them in one document.

*February* deserves a line of its own. **/ˈfɛbjuɛri/ — "Feb-you-ary" — is the ordinary American pronunciation**, and it is not sloppy; the careful /ˈfɛbruɛri/ is the minority form even among educated speakers. Both are listed in every American dictionary.

Americans learn the lengths from a rhyme — *Thirty days hath September, April, June, and November; all the rest have thirty-one* — and February's extra day, added every fourth year except in centuries not divisible by 400, makes a [leap year](../../parts-of-speech/01-nouns/catalog/46-core-time-calendar.md#leap-year).

### *in March*, and the rest of the set

| Preposition | Example | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| *in* | *in March* | at some point inside the month |
| *by* | *by March* | at or before the start of it — a deadline |
| *since* | *since March* | from then until now |
| *until* / *till* | *until March* | up to it |
| *through* | *through March* | up to and including all of it |
| *from … to* | *from March to June* | a run with both ends named |
| *this* / *last* / *next* / *every* | *last March* | **no preposition** |

> The rent goes up **in March**.  
> I need it **by March** — the first week at the latest.  
> She's been there **since March**. &middot; We're booked solid **through March**.  
> ✗ *on March* → ***in March***  
> ✗ *in next March* → ***next March***

**Month plus year takes no comma and no *of*.** *In March 2026 the rules change.* You will hear *in March of 2026* constantly in speech, and it is not an error, but the *of* adds nothing and edited writing drops it.

### Early, mid, late

| Form | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *in early March* | *We closed in early March.* | no hyphen |
| *in mid-March* | *The report is due in mid-March.* | **hyphen required** |
| *in late March* | *in late March, when it finally warmed up* | no hyphen |
| *at the beginning of March* | | longer, more formal |
| *at the end of March* | | |
| *the first of the month* | *Rent is due the first of the month.* | a fixed phrase |

***Mid-* is a prefix, not a word.** It cannot stand alone in front of a month: ✗ *in mid March* → *in mid-March*. Before a capitalized month the hyphen is required; before an ordinary noun it usually disappears — *midweek*, *midmonth*, *midnight*, *midcentury* (though *mid-century* is also standard).

### Seasons: lowercase

**The four seasons are common nouns and take no capital letter.**

> spring &middot; summer &middot; fall &middot; winter

***Fall*** **is the American word**; *autumn* is available and sounds literary or formal. In British English the relationship is reversed.

> I'm going back to school **in the fall**. &middot; It rains here **in the spring**.  
> We met **in the summer of 1997**.  
> **Last winter** was brutal. — no preposition, as always with *last*  
> a **spring** morning &middot; **fall** weather &middot; **summer** clothes — attributive, still lowercase

*In the spring* and *in spring* are both correct. The article is the more common choice in American English and is the safer default for a Russian speaker, who will otherwise drop it everywhere.

A season is capitalized only when it is part of an actual name: *the **Spring** 2026 semester*, *the **Summer** Olympics*, *the **Winter** Palace*. And in poetry, where a personified season is a character. Everywhere else: ✗ *in the Spring* → *in the spring*.

## Ordinals in dates

### Write the digit, say the word

**This is the rule the page does not tell you.** The number in a date is an ordinal, even when it is written as a bare cardinal digit.

> **March 3** → say *March **third***  
> **July 4** → say *July **fourth***  
> **the 22nd** → say *the **twenty-second***  
> ✗ *March three* &middot; ✗ *July four*

There is no exception. If a digit is a date, it is read as an ordinal.

### The suffix

**`-th` on everything, except `-st` after 1, `-nd` after 2, and `-rd` after 3** — and except 11, 12 and 13, which take `-th` despite their last digits.

> **-st** after 1 — 1st, 21st, 31st &middot; **-nd** after 2 — 2nd, 22nd &middot; **-rd** after 3 — 3rd, 23rd  
> **-th** everywhere else, including **11th, 12th, 13th**, which ignore their last digits

| # | Ordinal | Spelled out | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1st | **first** | |
| 2 | 2nd | **second** | not built on *two* at all — ✗ *twoth* |
| 3 | 3rd | **third** | ✗ *threeth* |
| 4 | 4th | **fourth** | keeps the *u* — compare *forty*, which loses it |
| 5 | 5th | **fifth** | *five* → *fif-* |
| 6 | 6th | **sixth** | |
| 7 | 7th | **seventh** | |
| 8 | 8th | **eighth** | **one *t*** — *eight* + *h*, not ✗ *eightth* |
| 9 | 9th | **ninth** | **drop the *e*** — ✗ *nineth* |
| 10 | 10th | **tenth** | |
| 11 | 11th | **eleventh** | |
| 12 | 12th | **twelfth** | ***f***, from *twelve* — ✗ *twelvth*, ✗ *twelth* |
| 13 | 13th | **thirteenth** | *thir-*, not *three-* |
| 14 | 14th | **fourteenth** | |
| 15 | 15th | **fifteenth** | *fif-* again |
| 16 | 16th | **sixteenth** | |
| 17 | 17th | **seventeenth** | |
| 18 | 18th | **eighteenth** | one *t* here too |
| 19 | 19th | **nineteenth** | but the *e* **stays** — ✗ *ninteenth* |
| 20 | 20th | **twentieth** | ***-y*** **→** ***-ieth*** — ✗ *twentyth* |
| 21 | 21st | **twenty-first** | hyphen; only the last word is an ordinal |
| 22 | 22nd | **twenty-second** | |
| 23 | 23rd | **twenty-third** | |
| 24 | 24th | **twenty-fourth** | |
| 25 | 25th | **twenty-fifth** | |
| 26 | 26th | **twenty-sixth** | |
| 27 | 27th | **twenty-seventh** | |
| 28 | 28th | **twenty-eighth** | |

| 30 | 30th | **thirtieth** | *-y* → *-ieth* |

Two patterns cover nearly all of it. **A word ending in `-y` changes to `-ieth`** (*twenty → twentieth*, *thirty → thirtieth*), the same `-y` → `-ie` rule that runs *city → cities* and *study → studies*. And **in a compound, only the final element takes the ordinal ending**: *twenty-**first***, not ✗ *twentieth-first*. The full ordinal system, past the end of the month, is in [numerals and ordinals](../../parts-of-speech/09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#ordinals).

### Saying the hard ones

Four ordinals end in consonant clusters that English barely tolerates, and every American simplifies them in ordinary speech.

> **fifth** /fɪfθ/ → /fɪθ/ &middot; **sixth** /sɪksθ/ → /sɪkst/ &middot; **eighth** /eɪtθ/ → /eɪθ/ &middot; **twelfth** /twɛlfθ/ → /twɛlθ/

The reduced forms are standard spoken American English, not errors. What matters is that the **spelling** keeps every letter: *fifth*, *sixth*, *eighth*, *twelfth*.

### When to write the suffix

| Form | Register |
|---|---|
| *March 3* | the standard in edited American writing — AP and Chicago both |
| *March 3rd* | normal in email, texts, invitations, handwriting, signage |
| *the 3rd of March* | formal, ceremonial, and the form on a wedding invitation |
| *the third of March* | fully spelled out — legal and ceremonial |
| *See you on the 3rd.* | the month is already known, so the suffix comes back |
| ✗ *3rd March* | British order; not American |

**The one rule that surprises people: in edited American prose the suffix comes off when the month is present.** *March 3*, not *March 3rd*. The ordinal is still what you say; it is just not what you write. When the month is absent, the suffix returns, because *See you on the 3* is not a thing anyone writes.

## Dates, the American way

### The order

**Month, day, year.** That is the American sequence, and it is the one reversal a Russian speaker has to make deliberately every time until it stops costing anything.

> **March 7, 2026** &middot; **March 7** &middot; **Mar. 7, 2026**  
> **3/7/2026** &middot; **3/7/26** &middot; **Tuesday, March 7, 2026**

### The 3/7/2026 problem

An all-numeral date is not international. The same string means two different days depending on who is reading it, and there is nothing in the string to tell you which.

| Written | An American reads | Nearly everyone else reads |
|---|---|---|
| **3/7/2026** | March 7 | 3 July |
| **7/3/2026** | July 3 | 7 March |
| **1/12/2026** | January 12 | 1 December |
| **12/1/2026** | December 1 | 12 January |

The pattern is worth stating exactly: **an all-numeral date is ambiguous whenever both numbers are 12 or lower**, which is 144 of the 365 days in a year. When one number is above 12 the date solves itself, because only one reading is possible. That is a lot of days to leave to luck, and it is why every style guide that touches an international audience says to spell the month.

### The forms that cannot be misread

| Form | Example | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Month spelled out | *March 7, 2026* | the general-purpose fix |
| Month abbreviated | *Mar. 7, 2026* | tables, footnotes, tight columns |
| Day first, month spelled | *7 March 2026* | military, scientific, international |
| ISO 8601 | *2026-03-07* | filenames, databases, anything that sorts |

**ISO 8601** is the form to know even though nobody says it aloud. Four-digit year, two-digit month, two-digit day, hyphens, largest unit first: *2026-03-07*. Because the units descend, a list of ISO dates sorts correctly as plain text, which is why filenames are written *2026-03-07-notes.md*. Read aloud it becomes an ordinary date: *March seventh, twenty twenty-six*. **The practical rule:** if the reader might not be American, spell the month; if a machine will sort it, use ISO; the slash form is for Americans writing to Americans.

### Written form to spoken form

| Written | Said |
|---|---|
| *March 3* | March third |
| *March 3rd* | March third |
| *the 3rd of March* | the third of March |
| *March 3, 2026* | March third, twenty twenty-six |
| *Tuesday, March 3* | Tuesday, March third |
| *Tuesday, March 3, 2026* | Tuesday, March third, twenty twenty-six |
| *3/7/26* | March seventh, twenty twenty-six |
| *Mar. 7, 2026* | March seventh, twenty twenty-six |
| *2026-03-07* | March seventh, twenty twenty-six |
| *7 March 2026* | the seventh of March, twenty twenty-six |
| *March 2026* | March twenty twenty-six |
| *9/11* | nine eleven — the one date read as bare digits |
| *7/4* | July Fourth &middot; the Fourth of July |
| *March 3–7* | March third **to** seventh |

Notice the last two. **An en dash between dates is read aloud as *to***, never as a pause. And **a slash in a date is never read as "slash"** — except in *9/11*, which has become a name rather than a date and is said *nine eleven*.

### The comma rule

Five cases, and they are all one idea: **the comma exists to keep the year from colliding with the day.**

| Pattern | Comma? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| month day, year | **yes**, before the year | *March 7, 2026* |
| …and mid-sentence | **yes**, after it as well | *The lease began on March 7, 2026, and runs two years.* |
| month year | **no** | *In March 2026 the rules changed.* |
| weekday, date | **yes**, after the weekday | *Tuesday, March 7* |
| day month year | **no** | *7 March 2026* |
| the Nth of Month Year | **no** | *the 7th of March 2026* |

The error that survives longest is **dropping the second comma**. The year is a parenthetical, and a parenthetical is closed as well as opened:

> ✗ *On March 7, 2026 we signed the lease.*  
> ✓ *On March 7, 2026**,** we signed the lease.*

And the mirror error, a comma where the day is missing:

> ✗ *March, 2026 was unusually warm.* → *March 2026 was unusually warm.*

### Ranges

> The exhibit runs **March 3–May 18**.  
> Closed **December 24 through January 2**.  
> **March 3–7** &middot; **2026–2028** &middot; **the 2025–26 school year**  
> ✗ *from March 3–7* → *from March 3 to 7*

The dash is an **en dash** (–), not a hyphen, and it means *to*. Mixing it with *from* produces a sentence with two openings and one close, which is why *from March 3–7* is wrong in every style guide. Either write out both words or use the dash alone.

## Years

### The halves rule

**Split the four digits into two pairs and read each pair as a number.** That is the default for every year in English, and it accounts for most of the language's history.

```
   1066   →   ten | sixty-six          ten sixty-six
   1492   →   fourteen | ninety-two    fourteen ninety-two
   1776   →   seventeen | seventy-six  seventeen seventy-six
   1985   →   nineteen | eighty-five   nineteen eighty-five
   2026   →   twenty | twenty-six      twenty twenty-six
```

The complications are all about zeros.

| Year | Said | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1900** | nineteen hundred | second pair is 00 → *hundred* |
| **1905** | nineteen **oh** five | second pair starts with 0 → *oh*, the letter |
| **1800** | eighteen hundred | |
| **2000** | two thousand &middot; the year two thousand | the halves rule breaks down |
| **2001** | two thousand one &middot; two thousand and one | |
| **2005** | two thousand five | *twenty oh five* exists and is rare |
| **2010** | twenty ten | where the halves rule came back |
| **2026** | twenty twenty-six | now the dominant form |
| **800** | eight hundred | three digits — no pairing |
| **1000** | one thousand &middot; the year one thousand | |
| **44 BC** | forty-four BC | |

**The 2000s wrinkle is recent enough to be worth explaining.** From 2000 through 2009, Americans said *two thousand nine* rather than *twenty oh nine*, because the *oh* forms sounded stilted. Starting in 2010 the ordinary halves pattern reasserted itself — *twenty ten*, *twenty twelve*, *twenty twenty* — and it has held ever since. Today *twenty twenty-six* and *two thousand twenty-six* are both correct; the first is far more common in speech, the second is the one a lawyer reads out loud.

***And*** **is the British half of this.** British English says *two thousand **and** five*; Americans usually drop it — *two thousand five* — exactly as they drop it in *two hundred five*. Either is understood; the bare one is the American default.

### Years in sentences

> She was born **in 1985**.  
> **In 2026** the rules change.  
> the **1985** season &middot; a **1985** Buick — attributive, no preposition  
> **by 2030** &middot; **since 1985** &middot; **from 1985 to 1992** &middot; **March 2026** — no comma  
> **the year 1985** &middot; **in the year 2000** — for emphasis or clarity

> ✗ *in 1985 year* → *in 1985*

That last one is the Russian «в 1985 году» carried across intact. English has no noun after the year and no ordinal in it: the year is a plain cardinal number standing alone.

### Decades

Five written forms, all standard:

| Form | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| spelled out | *the eighties* | lowercase is the default |
| apostrophe + two digits | *the '80s* | the apostrophe replaces the dropped *19* |
| four digits | *the 1980s* | no apostrophe anywhere |
| spelled with the century | *the nineteen-eighties* | hyphenated, formal |
| attributive | *'80s music* &middot; *1980s architecture* | no hyphen needed before a noun |

**The apostrophe rule, which is the whole reason this is in a grammar book.** An apostrophe marks an omission or a possessive. **It never makes a plural.**

> ✓ *the **1980s*** — the plural of 1980  
> ✗ *the **1980's*** — that is a possessive, and there is nothing being possessed  
> ✓ *the **'80s*** — the apostrophe stands in for the missing *19*, then *s* makes the plural  
> ✗ *the **80s*** — the years are 1980–1989, so something is missing and must be marked  
> ✗ *the **80's*** — both errors at once  
> ✓ *the **'80s'** biggest hits* — now the second apostrophe is a genuine possessive

One typographic trap: the mark before *80* is an **apostrophe** (') and not an opening quotation mark. Every word processor guesses wrong and produces ‘80s, and it has to be fixed by hand or by typing the character twice and deleting the first.

Subdivisions: **the early eighties**, **the mid-eighties**, **the late eighties**, **the turn of the decade**. And two decades have naming problems. ***The 1900s*** **is ambiguous** — it means either the ten years 1900–1909 or the whole century — so if you mean the decade, say *the first decade of the twentieth century*. And **2000–2009 has no settled name**: *the 2000s*, *the two-thousands*, *the aughts* and *the oughts* are all in use and none of them won.

### Centuries, and why the number is off by one

**The twentieth century is the 1900s.** The century number is always one higher than the first two digits of the year, and that is a permanent, structural offset rather than a quirk.

**Why:** there is no year zero. The count starts at AD 1, so the first century ran from AD 1 through AD 100; the second from 101 through 200; and the twentieth from **1901 through 2000**. The twenty-first began on January 1, 2001 — which is the argument everybody had at the end of 1999, and the pedants were right and lost anyway.

| Years | Century | Strictly |
|---|---|---|
| 1–100 | the first century | 1–100 |
| 1400s | the fifteenth century | 1401–1500 |
| 1700s | the eighteenth century | 1701–1800 |
| 1800s | the nineteenth century | 1801–1900 |
| 1900s | the twentieth century | 1901–2000 |
| 2000s | the twenty-first century | 2001–2100 |

In ordinary use people ignore the strict column and treat *the twentieth century* as 1900–1999. For a learner the working rule is the arithmetic: **take the first two digits of the year and add one.** 1776 → 17 + 1 = the eighteenth century.

**Writing them.** *The twentieth century* spelled out and lowercase is the Chicago preference; *the 20th century* is AP style and is normal everywhere. Both are fine; consistency is not optional. And the hyphen appears only when the century modifies a noun:

> **twentieth-century** architecture &middot; a **19th-century** novel — hyphenated, attributive  
> the architecture of the **twentieth century** — no hyphen, it is a noun here  
> **mid-century** modern &middot; **turn-of-the-century** houses

*The turn of the century* is now ambiguous — 1900 or 2000 — so name the year when it matters. Above the century sits the **millennium**: the second millennium was 1001–2000, the third is 2001–3000, and *the turn of the millennium* means 2000.

### BC, AD, BCE, CE

| Label | Stands for | Where it goes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BC** | before Christ | **after** the year | *44 BC* |
| **AD** | *anno Domini*, "in the year of the Lord" | **before** the year; **after** a century | *AD 1066* &middot; *the second century AD* |
| **BCE** | Before the Common Era | **after** the year | *44 BCE* |
| **CE** | Common Era | **after** the year | *1066 CE* |

**AD comes first because it is a Latin prepositional phrase.** *AD 1066* reads as "in the year of the Lord 1066," and putting the phrase after its object is like writing *1066 in the year of the Lord*. In practice *1066 AD* is everywhere and most editors now accept it, but *AD 1066* is the form nobody will correct.

**With a century the Latin logic collapses**, because *anno Domini* modifies a year and not a hundred-year stretch. So the label moves to the back:

> the second century **AD** — ✓  
> ✗ *the AD second century*

**BCE and CE are the religiously neutral pair**, standard in academic, archaeological and museum writing. They map exactly onto the older pair — 1 BCE is 1 BC, 1066 CE is AD 1066 — so nothing is renumbered. What you cannot do is mix the systems in one document.

Three more points:

- **Periods.** *B.C.*, *A.D.*, *B.C.E.* and *C.E.* are the older punctuated forms; AP still uses them, Chicago prefers the bare capitals. Choose one.
- **BC counts backward.** *100 BC* is earlier than *44 BC*, and *the fifth century BC* runs from 500 to 401 BC. Everything in BC is upside down relative to intuition.
- **CE is usually left off** for years anyone would assume are recent — *1066 CE* in an ancient-history context, but just *1985* everywhere else.
- **An approximate year takes *c.*, *ca.* or *circa***, read aloud as *circa*: *c. 1450*, *around 1450*, and *c. 1450–1460* for a range of uncertainty.

## Time spans built on dates

Every one of these is a date with a boundary attached, and the boundaries differ in exactly one respect: whether the named day is inside or outside.

| Expression | The named day is… | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *from March to June* | unsettled — usually in | neutral | *The store is closed from March to June.* |
| *from March through June* | **in** | American, precise | *Open from March through June.* |
| *between March and June* | usually **out** | neutral | *It happened between March and June.* |
| *March–June* | **in** | tables, signs | *Season: March–June* |
| *through March 3* | **in** | American | *The offer runs through March 3.* |
| *until March 3* | unsettled | neutral | *We're waiting until March 3.* |
| *by March 3* | **in** — at or before | neutral | *I need it by March 3.* |
| *March 3 at the latest* | **in** | emphatic | *Friday, March 3 at the latest.* |
| *no later than March 3* | **in** | formal, legal | *Deliver no later than March 3.* |
| *on or before March 3* | **in** | legal | |
| *up to and including March 3* | **in** | legal, explicit | |
| *as of March 1* | the start point | business | *As of March 1, the rate is 6%.* |
| *effective March 1* | the start point | business, legal | *Effective March 1, the office moves.* |
| *starting / beginning March 1* | the start point | neutral | |

**The pair worth separating is *by* and *until*.** They are not interchangeable, and the difference is what kind of verb follows.

> Finish it **by** Friday. — one event, at any point up to Friday  
> Wait **until** Friday. — a state that continues up to Friday  
> ✗ *Wait by Friday.* &middot; ✗ *Finish it until Friday.*

***As of*** **points in two directions**, which confuses people. *As of March 1* can mean "starting on March 1" (*As of March 1, parking is $5*) or "measured at March 1" (*As of March 1, we had 400 subscribers*). Context settles it, and it always does.

In contracts and notices the date is load-bearing, and the language gets correspondingly explicit:

> This agreement is **effective March 1, 2026**, and terminates **on December 31, 2026**.  
> Payment is due **on or before the third day of each month**.  
> Applications will be accepted **from March 1 through March 31, inclusive**.

Every one of these prepositions has a full entry in [prepositions of time](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md) — see [as of](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#as-of), [effective](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#effective), [no later than](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#no-later-than) and [through](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#through).

## Holidays and recurring dates

The preposition depends on whether you mean the day itself, the season around it, or the stretch you spent inside it.

| Pattern | Example | Means |
|---|---|---|
| *on* + a named day | *on Christmas Day*, *on Thanksgiving*, *on New Year's Day* | that one day |
| *at* + a holiday season | *at Christmas*, *at Easter*, *at New Year's* | the general period |
| *over* + a multi-day stretch | *over Thanksgiving*, *over Christmas*, *over the holidays* | across the span |
| *for* + the occasion | *for Christmas*, *for my birthday* | the reason, not the time |
| *during* | *during Ramadan*, *during Lent* | throughout |

Americans say *on Thanksgiving* far more often than *at Thanksgiving*; *at Christmas* is the one that survives as a season. And *the holidays*, lowercase and plural, means the late-December stretch and nothing else: *What are you doing over the holidays?*

**The Fourth of July is the one date Americans habitually say day-first** — a fossil, and the only living trace of the British order in American speech. *The Fourth of July*, *July Fourth*, *the 4th of July*, or *Independence Day*; also *on the Fourth*, *over the Fourth*, and *the Fourth of July weekend*. The capital *F* belongs to the holiday. Written lowercase, *the fourth of July* is just a date on a calendar.

**Movable holidays are named by their rule**, and the rule is how Americans state them: *Thanksgiving is the fourth Thursday in November. Memorial Day is the last Monday in May. Labor Day is the first Monday in September.* *In November* and *of November* are both fine there.

**Weekends.** *On the weekend* is American; ✗ *at the weekend* is British and should not be written here. *Over the weekend* covers the whole stretch and is the most common of all. *This / last / next weekend* take no preposition, and *on weekends* or bare *weekends* is the habitual form.

> What are you doing **on the weekend**? &middot; We drove up **over the weekend**.  
> I work **weekends**. &middot; He's away **next weekend**.

**Birthdays and anniversaries** take *on* for the day and *for* for the occasion: *on my birthday*, *for my birthday*, *my thirtieth birthday*, *our tenth anniversary*, *on the anniversary of the fire*. And the verb is *turn*: *She turns thirty on Friday.*

**Holiday names are proper nouns** and take capitals throughout: *Christmas, Christmas Eve, Thanksgiving, Halloween, New Year's Eve, New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Memorial Day, Labor Day, Presidents' Day, Veterans Day, Easter, Passover, Hanukkah, Ramadan, Eid*. Watch the apostrophes, which are inconsistent by tradition rather than by rule: *New Year's Day*, *Mother's Day* and *Father's Day* are singular possessives; *Presidents' Day* is plural (and often written *Presidents Day*); *Veterans Day* has no apostrophe at all.

## Reading a date aloud

In a full sentence, nothing is added and nothing is skipped — the written form is simply pronounced by the rules above.

> The contract was signed **on March 3, 2026**. — "on March third, twenty twenty-six"  
> She was born **on July 4, 1985**. — "on July fourth, nineteen eighty-five"  
> The meeting is **Tuesday, March 3**. — "Tuesday, March third"  
> The photo is dated **10/12/99**. — "October twelfth, ninety-nine"

**Formal and legal English keeps the long forms**, because a date read aloud in a courtroom or written on a deed has to be unmistakable and hard to alter.

> **this 3rd day of March, 2026** — "this third day of March, two thousand twenty-six"  
> **the third day of March in the year two thousand twenty-six**  
> Dated **as of March 1, 2026**.

Note that legal reading takes *two thousand twenty-six*, not *twenty twenty-six*. The longer form has no possible second parse, and that is the whole point of it.

Two practical habits. **Over the phone, spell the month** — "March, M as in Mary" — because *March* and *May* collapse into each other on a bad line. And **for a year that must be exact, read the digits**: "two zero two six."

One convention from American broadcast and wire journalism, which AP style makes explicit: **within a week either side of today, use the day of the week; beyond that, use the date.** *The mayor resigned Tuesday* for something three days old; *The mayor resigned on March 3* for something three months old.

## For Russian speakers

Seven interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

**1. Capital letters.** Russian lowercases both «понедельник» and «март». English capitalizes both, everywhere, including in the middle of a sentence and in an attributive phrase.

> ✗ *on monday in march* → *on **M**onday in **M**arch*

Capitalized in English: days, months, holidays, their abbreviations. **Not** capitalized: seasons (*spring*, *fall*), and the words *century*, *decade*, *year*, *month*, *week*, *weekend*.

**2. The order is reversed, and the separator changes.** Russian writes «07.03.2026» for 7 March. American English writes **3/7/2026** for March 7. Same six digits, different day, and the periods become slashes.

| Russian | American |
|---|---|
| 07.03.2026 | 3/7/2026 &middot; March 7, 2026 |
| 3 марта 2026 г. | March 3, 2026 |
| 2026-03-07 | 2026-03-07 — ISO, the one form both sides read alike |

✗ *7.3.2026* in an American document will be read as July 3 by a machine and as nonsense by a person. When in doubt, spell the month.

**3. «в» splits into three prepositions.**

| Russian | English | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| в понедельник | **on** Monday | *on* a day |
| 3 марта | **on** March 3 | *on* a date |
| в марте | **in** March | *in* a month |
| в 1985 году | **in** 1985 | *in* a year |
| весной | **in** the spring | *in* a season |
| в XX веке | **in** the twentieth century | *in* a century |
| в три часа | **at** three o'clock | *at* a clock time |
| на выходных | **on** the weekend | |
| на Рождество | **at** / **on** Christmas | |

**One line to carry: *at* a point, *on* a day, *in* anything longer.** And no preposition at all after *this*, *next*, *last*, *every*, *tomorrow* or *yesterday*: ✗ *on next Monday*, ✗ *in last March*, ✗ *on yesterday*.

**4. The genitive date.** Russian puts the ordinal in the genitive and needs no preposition — «третьего марта». English needs a word in front of it, and offers two orders:

> «Третьего марта» → *(on) **March third*** — written *March 3*  
> → *(on) **the third of March*** — written *the 3rd of March*, formal

And «3 марта» reverses: *March 3*. The question and answer reverse too. Russian asks «Какое сегодня число?» and answers with a bare genitive; English asks *What's the date?* and answers *It's the third* or *It's March third*.

**5. A year in English is a cardinal number with nothing attached.** Russian says «в тысяча девятьсот восемьдесят пятом году» — one enormous ordinal ending in a noun. English has neither the ordinal nor the noun, and splits the number in half:

> ✗ *in 1985 year* &middot; ✗ *in the 1985th* &middot; ✗ *in 1985-th* → *in 1985*, said *nineteen eighty-five*  
> ✗ *March 3, 2026 year* → *March 3, 2026*

**6. Articles, which Russian does not have and dates are full of.** The names take none; the descriptions take *the*.

> **No article:** *in March* &middot; *on Monday* &middot; *in 1985* &middot; *at Christmas* &middot; *in spring*  
> ***The*:** *in **the** spring* &middot; *in **the** eighties* &middot; ***the** third of March* &middot; *on **the** 3rd* &middot; ***the** twentieth century* &middot; *on **the** weekend* &middot; ***the** Fourth of July* &middot; ***the** first Monday in September*

**7. «с … по …» is *through*, not *to*.** Russian «с понедельника по пятницу» includes Friday. The English form that keeps the inclusion is *Monday **through** Friday*; *Monday to Friday* leaves it open. Same for dates: «с 3 по 7 марта» → *March 3 through 7*.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *on **monday*** | *on **Monday*** | Day names are proper nouns. |
| ✗ *in **march*** | *in **March*** | So are month names. |
| ✗ *in the **S**pring* | *in the **s**pring* | Seasons are not. |
| ✗ *on next Monday* | *next Monday* | *This / next / last / every* replace the preposition. |
| ✗ *in Monday* &middot; ✗ *on March* | *on Monday* &middot; *in March* | Days take *on*; months take *in*. |
| ✗ *3 March 2026* | *March 3, 2026* | American order is month, day, year. |
| ✗ *7.3.2026* | *3/7/2026* &middot; *March 7, 2026* | Slashes, month first — or spell it out. |
| ✗ *On March 7, 2026 we signed.* | *On March 7, 2026**,** we signed.* | The year is a parenthetical; close it. |
| ✗ *March, 2026* | *March 2026* | No comma between a bare month and a year. |
| ✗ *the 1980's* | *the 1980s* | An apostrophe never makes a plural. |
| ✗ *the 80s* &middot; ✗ *the 80's* | *the '80s* | The apostrophe marks the dropped *19*. |
| ✗ *in 1985 year* | *in 1985* | English attaches no noun to a year. |
| ✗ *March three* | *March third* | A date digit is read as an ordinal. |
| ✗ *the nineth* | *the ninth* | *Nine* loses its *e*. |
| ✗ *the twelvth* &middot; ✗ *the twelth* | *the twelfth* | *Twelve* → *twelf-*. |
| ✗ *the eightth* | *the eighth* | One *t*: *eight* + *h*. |
| ✗ *the twentyth* | *the twentieth* | *-y* → *-ieth*. |
| ✗ *the twentieth-first* | *the twenty-first* | Only the last element is an ordinal. |
| ✗ *the AD second century* | *the second century AD* | *AD* precedes a year, follows a century. |
| ✗ *at the weekend* | *on the weekend* | British. |
| ✗ *Monday to Friday* (Friday must count) | *Monday through Friday* | American *through* is inclusive. |
| ✗ *from March 3–7* | *from March 3 to 7* &middot; *March 3–7* | Never mix *from* with a dash. |

## Quick reference

```
   WRITE                     SAY
   ---------------------------------------------------------
   March 3                   March third
   March 3, 2026             March third, twenty twenty-six
   3/7/2026                  March seventh      (US: month / day / year)
   2026-03-07                March seventh, twenty twenty-six
   1985                      nineteen eighty-five
   2000 / 2005               two thousand / two thousand five
   2026                      twenty twenty-six
   the '80s                  the eighties
   the 20th century          the twentieth century   ( = the 1900s )
   44 BC / AD 1066           forty-four BC / AD ten sixty-six
```

```
   at  a point on the clock ......  at 3:00, at noon, at midnight
   on  a day or a date ...........  on Monday, on March 3, on my birthday
   in  anything longer ...........  in March, in the spring, in 1985, in the '80s
   --  this / next / last / every .  next Monday, last March, every Friday
```

**Six rules that prevent almost every error.**

1. **Capitalize days, months and holidays. Lowercase seasons.**
2. **Month, day, year** — and spell the month out whenever a non-American might read it.
3. **Write the digit, say the ordinal.** *March 3* is *March third*.
4. **Split a year in half** and read each half as a number — except the *two thousand* years.
5. **No apostrophe in a plural:** *the 1980s*, *the '80s*.
6. **The century number is the year's first two digits plus one.**

## Practice

1. Capitalize: *we're closed on monday and tuesday in july, and all of march.*
2. Say aloud: *March 3, 2026* &middot; *July 4, 1776* &middot; *the 22nd*.
3. Say aloud: 1066, 1900, 1905, 2000, 2007, 2010, 2026.
4. Rewrite in American form: *7 March 2026.*
5. What does *3/7/2026* mean to an American? To a Londoner?
6. Fix: *The lease begins on March 1, 2026 and runs one year.*
7. Fix: *I grew up on music from the 1990's.*
8. Fix: *We're open Monday to Friday.* (Friday has to be included.)
9. Fix: *See you on next Friday.*
10. Spell out the ordinal: 2, 5, 8, 9, 12, 19, 20, 21, 30.
11. Which century is 1776 in? Which is 2026?
12. Place the era label: *44 ( BC )*, *1066 ( AD )*, *the second century ( AD )*.
13. Fill in the preposition, or leave it blank: *___ Monday &middot; ___ March &middot; ___ 1985 &middot; ___ the spring &middot; ___ Christmas Day &middot; ___ the weekend &middot; ___ next Tuesday.*
14. Fix: *In 1985 year I was born in march.*
15. What is ambiguous about *next Friday*, and how would you repair it?
16. Fix: *March, 2026 was unusually warm.*
17. Rewrite as ISO 8601: *December 1, 2026.*
18. Fix: *The exhibit runs from March 3–May 18.*

| # | Answer | Why |

| 2 | March third, twenty twenty-six &middot; July fourth, seventeen seventy-six &middot; the twenty-second | Date digits are read as ordinals; years split in half. |
| 3 | ten sixty-six &middot; nineteen hundred &middot; nineteen oh five &middot; two thousand &middot; two thousand seven &middot; twenty ten &middot; twenty twenty-six | The halves rule, with *hundred*, *oh*, and the *two thousand* decade. |
| 4 | **March 7, 2026** | American order is month, day, year. |
| 5 | An American reads **March 7**; a Londoner reads **3 July**. | Month/day/year against day/month/year. |
| 6 | …on March 1, 2026**,** and runs one year. | The year takes a comma on both sides. |
| 7 | …music from the **1990s**. | An apostrophe does not make a plural. |
| 8 | We're open Monday **through** Friday. | American *through* includes the last day. |
| 9 | See you **next Friday**. | *Next* takes no preposition. |
| 10 | second, fifth, eighth, ninth, twelfth, nineteenth, twentieth, twenty-first, thirtieth | The spelling traps: *eighth* one *t*, *ninth* no *e*, *twelfth* with *f*, *-y* → *-ieth*. |
| 11 | 1776 → the **eighteenth** century. 2026 → the **twenty-first**. | First two digits plus one. |
| 12 | **44 BC** &middot; **AD 1066** &middot; **the second century AD** | *BC* follows; *AD* precedes a year but follows a century. |
| 13 | **on** Monday &middot; **in** March &middot; **in** 1985 &middot; **in** the spring &middot; **on** Christmas Day &middot; **on** the weekend &middot; **—** next Tuesday | *at* a point, *on* a day, *in* anything longer; *next* takes nothing. |
