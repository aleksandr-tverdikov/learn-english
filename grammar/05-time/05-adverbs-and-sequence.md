# Time adverbs and sequence

> The words that place an event next to *now* — *just*, *already*, *still*, *soon* — and the words that place events next to each other — *first*, *then*, *meanwhile*, *in the end*.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

A tense says whether something is past, present, or future. It does not say **when**, and it does not say **in what order**. That work is done by a few dozen short words, and they are not a list: they fall into three groups, and the group a word belongs to predicts almost everything about it — which tense it takes, where in the sentence it can stand, and which Russian word it is not.

| Group | The question it answers | Members | Anchored to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deictic** | When, counting from now? | *now, just, today, tonight, recently, two days ago, soon, later* | the moment of speaking |
| **Sequential** | What came before it, and what after? | *first, then, next, after that, meanwhile, finally* | the previous event |
| **Phasal** | Has it started? Has it stopped? | *already, yet, still, anymore, no longer* | a change of state |

A deictic adverb is recomputed every time the clock moves, which is why *I'll call you in a minute* is useless in an email and *I saw him two days ago* has to be rewritten the moment it is reported. A sequential adverb is computed from whatever was mentioned last, which is why *then* and *after that* are meaningless in the first sentence of anything. A phasal adverb is not about a point on the line at all — it is about whether a situation has switched on or off, and it is the group that costs Russian speakers the most, because Russian handles all five with two words.

The individual entries — pronunciation, register, full sense lists — are in [adverbs of time](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/06-time.md) and [linking adverbs](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/13-linking.md). What follows is how they are chosen against each other.

## The now-words

### *now*, *right now*, *just now*

***Now*** is wider than a moment. It stretches from this instant (*Do it now*) to the present era (*Now we do everything online*), and it also does discourse work, meaning "at this point in what I am saying."

> I**'m** busy **now** — can I call you back? &middot; **Now** we wait.  
> He **now** lives in Portland. — mid position, slightly formal  
> **Now**, that's a good question. — discourse, not time

***Right now*** narrows *now* to this instant, and adds urgency after an imperative.

> She**'s** on the other line **right now**. &middot; Come here **right now**.  
> **Right now** we're short two people. — American; = for the time being, and it is expected to change

***Just now*** has **two meanings**, and the one a learner reaches for is usually the wrong one for American English.

| Sense | Position | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **a moment ago** — the normal American sense | end | past simple | *I **saw** her **just now**.* &middot; *Somebody **called just now**.* |
| **at this moment** — commoner in British English | end | present | *I**'m** rather busy **just now**.* |

Two cautions. The "a moment ago" sense takes the **past simple**, not the present perfect — ✗ *I have seen her just now*. And the South African and Indian sense, "in a little while," does not exist in American English: here *just now* points backward, never forward. Do not confuse it with mid-position *just*, a different structure with the same meaning: *I **just** saw her* = *I saw her **just now***.

### *at the moment*, *currently*, *presently*

All three mean roughly "as things stand," and all three imply the situation is temporary. They differ in register and in one dangerous ambiguity.

> She**'s** in a meeting **at the moment**. — conversational; end or front  
> The office is **currently** closed for renovations. — neutral to formal; mid or front

***Presently* is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is real.** Its older sense is **soon**; its newer sense, now dominant in American English, is **currently**.

> The doctor will see you **presently**. — soon, in a moment  
> She is **presently** serving as acting director. — currently

Nothing disambiguates the two except the tense of the verb, and with some verbs even that fails. Careful American writers avoid the word and write *soon* or *currently*; a learner loses nothing by doing the same. Recognize it, do not produce it — the full entry is at [*presently*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/06-time.md#presently). One neighbor is not a variant of *at the moment*: ***for the moment*** announces that an arrangement is provisional — *For the moment, we're using the conference room as an office.*

### *these days*, *nowadays*

Both contrast the present era with an earlier one, and the contrast is part of the meaning even when it is not stated. They sit at the end or at the front, and **never in mid position**.

> Nobody writes checks **these days**. &middot; **Nowadays** you can do the whole thing online. &middot; Kids **these days**.

*Nowadays* is slightly more written; *these days* is what people say. Neither works for a single moment, which is the error to watch for:

> ✗ *I**'m** reading a good book **nowadays**.* → *…**right now**.*  
> ✓ *I read a lot more **nowadays** than I used to.* — an era against an era

### *today*, *tonight*

These are nouns used as adverbs, and they take no preposition: *I saw him today*, never ✗ *on today*. End position is normal; front position sets a frame.

> The mail came late **today**. &middot; I'll call you **tonight**. &middot; **Today** we're starting with the budget.

*Tonight* covers this evening and the coming night; its past counterpart is ***last night***, and the parallel American forms are ***this morning***, ***this afternoon***, ***this evening***. The two shapes that do **not** exist are worth memorizing, because Russian builds both freely:

> ✗ *yesterday night* → ***last night*** &middot; ✗ *today morning* → ***this morning***

## The near past and the near future

### *just*, *recently*, *lately*

***Just*** puts an event a very short time before now, and takes **mid position** — before the main verb, after *be*, after the first auxiliary.

> I**'ve just** finished. &middot; She**'s just** left.  
> We **just** got back. — American past simple, entirely standard here  
> He was **just** about to call you.

The present perfect (*I've just eaten*) is correct everywhere; the past simple (*I just ate*) is the ordinary American form and at least as common in speech. The same American latitude applies to *already* and *yet*, and it is laid out in [the present perfect](../04-tenses/03-present-perfect.md).

***Recently*** and ***lately*** both mean "in the period leading up to now," and they are not interchangeable.

| | *recently* | *lately* |
|---|---|---|
| Position | mid or end | end almost always |
| Present perfect | ✓ *I've recently started running.* | ✓ *I've been tired lately.* |
| Past simple | ✓ *She recently moved to Tulsa.* | ✗ *She lately moved to Tulsa.* |
| Negatives and questions | ✓ | **preferred** — *Have you seen him lately?* |
| A single past event | ✓ | ✗ |

The short version: ***recently* can point at one past event; *lately* can only describe a stretch of time**, and it gravitates toward negatives, questions, and the continuous.

> I ran into her **recently**. — one occasion &middot; ✗ *I ran into her **lately**.*  
> I haven't been sleeping well **lately**. &middot; What have you been up to **lately**?

### *the other day*, *a moment ago*, *earlier*

***The other day*** means "a few days ago, and the exact day does not matter." Past simple, end or front. The same frame gives *the other night*, *the other week*.

> I **ran** into Dana **the other day**. &middot; **The other night** the power went out twice.

***A moment ago***, ***a minute ago***, ***a second ago*** — literal or not, all take the past simple and follow the *ago* rule below. ***Earlier*** means "at some point before now, usually today," or "before a past point already established."

> I **called** you **earlier**. &middot; **Earlier** this week the roof started leaking.  
> She had left two hours **earlier**. — counting back from a past moment

### *soon*, *shortly*, *in a minute*, *before long*, *any minute now*, *later*, *afterward*

Five ways of saying "not long from now," plus the two open-ended ones.

| Phrase | Register | Position | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **soon** | neutral | mid or end | gradable: *very soon*, *quite soon*, *how soon?* |
| **shortly** | slightly formal | mid or end | also *shortly after* / *shortly before* + noun |
| **in a minute** / **in a second** / **in a bit** | informal | end | *in a bit* is American and vague; *in a second* is rarely literal |
| **before long** | slightly literary | front or end | works in past narrative too: *Before long it started to rain.* |
| **any minute now** | conversational | front or end | imminent **and expected** |
| **later** / **later on** | neutral | end | also counts forward from a past point: *two days later* |
| **afterward(s)** | neutral | end or front | = *after that*; takes **no object** |

> I**'ll** be with you **shortly**. &middot; We should hear back **soon**.  
> Give me a minute — I**'ll** be down **in a bit**. &middot; The bus should be here **any minute now**.  
> We had dinner and went for a walk **afterward**.

**Note the preposition.** *In* + a length of time means "at the end of that length, counting from now" — *in an hour* is Russian «через час», not ✗ *after an hour*, which counts from somewhere else.

**And note that *afterward* is an adverb with no object**, while *after* is a preposition and a conjunction:

> ✗ *We went for a walk **afterward the dinner**.* → *…**after** dinner.*

The same three-way split applies to *before* — preposition (*before dinner*), conjunction (*before we ate*), and bare adverb (*I've seen it before*). Only the bare adverb belongs in this section.

## *ago* against *before* against *earlier*

One distinction with two halves: **where you are counting from**, and **which tense that forces**.

***Ago* counts back from now.** It follows its measurement, never precedes it, and it takes the **past simple** — never the present perfect.

> She left ten minutes **ago**. &middot; I bought it **two years ago**. &middot; How long **ago** did this happen?  
> ✗ *I have seen him **two weeks ago**.* → *I **saw** him two weeks ago.* &middot; ✗ ***ago** two weeks* → *two weeks **ago***

That first error is among the most persistent in advanced English, and the reason is structural: the present perfect refuses a finished-time expression, and *ago* is nothing but a finished-time expression. The rule is worked out in [the present perfect](../04-tenses/03-present-perfect.md).

***Before* counts back from a past point.** With a measurement in front of it, that past point has to be established already, and the tense is normally the **past perfect**.

> She had left ten minutes **before**. &middot; The storm hit Tuesday; the day **before**, the sky was clear.

Bare *before*, with no measurement, does a different job — "ever, at some earlier time." It takes the present perfect and sits at the end.

> Have we met **before**? &middot; I**'ve** never seen anything like it **before**.

***Earlier* and *previously* do the same work as measured *before*,** and both are more natural than *before* in American writing. *Earlier* additionally covers "earlier today."

| | Counts from | Usual tense | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***ago*** | **now** | past simple | *She left ten minutes **ago**.* |
| ***before*** (measured) | a **past point** | past perfect | *She had left ten minutes **before**.* |
| ***earlier*** | a past point, or earlier today | past perfect / past simple | *She had left ten minutes **earlier**.* &middot; *I called you **earlier**.* |
| ***previously*** | a past point; formal | past perfect | *He had **previously** worked in Tulsa.* |
| ***later*** | **forward** from a past point | past simple | *She called ten minutes **later**.* |

The cleanest demonstration is what happens inside reported speech. Once the anchor moves off *now*, the word has to change:

> "I saw her **two days ago**." → He said he**'d** seen her two days **before** / **earlier**.

Not ✗ *He said he'd seen her two days ago*, unless the report is made the same day and *ago* still counts from **now**. The mechanism is [backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md), where the full table of time-word substitutions lives.

## *Already*, *yet*, *still*, *anymore*, *no longer*

This is the set Russian collapses into **уже** and **ещё**. The five words are not five vocabulary items; they are the four corners of one small system, plus a formal variant.

### What the set actually tracks

Every one of them reports on a **situation and whether it has changed**. Two variables decide which word you need: whether the situation is on or off, and whether the sentence is affirmative or negative.

```
                    the change has NOT happened     the change HAS happened
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   affirmative      still                           already
                    He still works here.            He has already left.

   negative         not ... yet                     not ... anymore / no longer
                    He hasn't left yet.             He doesn't work here anymore.
```

Read the square diagonally and the oppositions come out clean. *Still* and *not … anymore* are opposites: one says the situation continues, the other says it stopped. *Already* and *not … yet* are opposites: one says the event has happened, the other says it has not happened **and is expected to**.

That last clause is doing real work. *Not yet* is not a plain negative. *He hasn't left yet* says his leaving is on the cards; *He hasn't left* says nothing of the sort.

### *already*

**Meaning:** the change has happened, often sooner than the hearer expected. **Position:** mid is neutral, end adds emphasis or surprise.

> I**'ve already** eaten, thanks. &middot; The movie had **already** started when we got there.  
> You**'re** leaving **already**? — end position, surprise &middot; It**'s** midnight **already**.

**Tense:** the present perfect is the traditional pairing, and American English uses the past simple just as freely — *I already ate* is not a mistake here.

**In questions**, *already* is not neutral. It asks with surprise: *Have you finished **already**?* means "so soon?" The neutral question uses *yet*.

**In negatives** it is simply wrong for Russian уже не: ✗ *He **already doesn't** work here.* → *He **doesn't** work here **anymore**.*

**The impatience use** is informal American, at the very end of a command or complaint: *Enough **already**! Just make up your mind **already**.*

### *yet*

**Meaning:** up to now — in a **negative** or a **question**. That restriction is the single most useful fact about the word. **Position:** end; a mid position after the negative is available and distinctly formal.

> I haven't finished **yet**. &middot; Has the mail come **yet**?  
> "Are we there **yet**?" — "**Not yet**."  
> We have **not yet** decided. — formal mid &middot; Did you eat **yet**? — American past simple

**Three more uses**, each of which looks like an exception and is not:

- ***have yet to*** has affirmative shape and negative meaning: *The winner **has yet to** be announced* = has not been announced yet. Also *The best **is yet to** come.*
- **After a superlative**, *yet* means "of those so far": *That was her best album **yet**.*
- ***as yet*** is a formal fixed phrase: *No decision has been made **as yet**.*

The conjunction *yet* meaning *but* (*simple **yet** effective*, *and **yet** he went anyway*) is a different word wearing the same spelling; it belongs with the [linking adverbs](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/13-linking.md).

> ✗ *I have **yet** finished.* → *I haven't finished **yet**.*  
> ✗ *He is **yet** working here.* → *He is **still** working here.*

### *still*

**Meaning:** the situation is continuing, usually longer than expected. **Position:** mid — and critically, **before a negative auxiliary**, not after it.

> She **still** lives with her parents. &middot; Are you **still** at work?  
> He **still hasn't** called. — ✓ &middot; ✗ *He **hasn't still** called.*

That position rule is the mechanical difference between the two words: ***still* goes to the left of the negative; *yet* goes to the right of everything.**

*Still* has two further senses that are not about time, both common enough to meet within a day: **nevertheless**, at the front of a sentence (*It rained all weekend. **Still**, we had a good time*), and **even**, with a comparative (*The next day was hotter **still**. Better **still**, call ahead*).

### *still* against *yet* in negatives

Both are grammatical in a negative sentence, they do not mean the same thing, and they do not stand in the same place.

| Sentence | What it reports | Attitude |
|---|---|---|
| *He hasn't called **yet**.* | the call is expected; it has not come | neutral |
| *He **still** hasn't called.* | the call is overdue | impatient, worried |
| *She isn't here **yet**.* | she is expected | neutral |
| *She **still** isn't here.* | it has been long enough | exasperated |
| *I haven't decided **yet**.* | the decision is open | neutral |
| *I **still** haven't decided.* | I have been at it a while | frustrated |

Both in one sentence — *He still hasn't called yet* — is redundant. It is heard in speech; pick one in writing.

### *anymore* and *no longer*

**Both mean "not now, as before."** They differ in shape, in register, and in where they can stand.

***Anymore*** (one word in American English) is **end position**, and needs a negative or a question.

> We don't go there **anymore**. &middot; She doesn't live here **anymore**.  
> Does anybody even use fax machines **anymore**?

***No longer*** is **mid position**, and carries its own negation — the sentence around it keeps affirmative shape.

> He **no longer** works here. &middot; She is **no longer** with the company.  
> ✗ *He **doesn't no longer** work here.* — double negative &middot; ✗ *He works here **no longer**.* — archaic

*No longer* is a step more formal than *not … anymore*, and it is what written English prefers. Fronting it forces inversion and is decidedly literary: ***No longer does** the mill run.*

**Two spelling traps.** ***Any more*** as two words is a **quantity**, not a time — *I don't want **any more** cake* and *I don't drink coffee **anymore*** are different sentences. And ✗ *He doesn't work here **no more*** is a nonstandard double negative; the positive *anymore* (*Anymore, everything costs a fortune*) is regional Midland American speech, worth recognizing and not worth copying.

### The decision, and the positions

```
   Has the event happened, sooner than expected?       →  already
   Is it expected and hasn't happened — neutrally?     →  not ... yet
   Is it expected, hasn't happened, and you're fed up? →  still + negative
   Is the situation continuing?                        →  still
   Has the situation stopped?                          →  not ... anymore   (everyday)
                                                          no longer         (formal)
```

| Word | Front | Mid | End |
|---|---|---|---|
| **already** | marked; only with a quantity — *Already three people have complained.* | ✓ neutral | ✓ surprise |
| **yet** | ✗ | formal only, after *not* | ✓ the normal slot |
| **still** | only in the "nevertheless" sense | ✓ **only** — before a negative auxiliary | ✗ |
| **anymore** | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ **only** |
| **no longer** | inversion; literary | ✓ **only** | ✗ |

## Sequencing a narrative or a set of instructions

### Opening: *first*, *at first*, *firstly*, *to begin with*

Four openers, and only one of them is safe in a set of instructions.

| Word | Means | Needs a contrast later? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***first*** | step one of a series | no | *First you sign it, then you mail it.* |
| ***first of all*** | step one, with the list announced | no | *First of all, turn off the water.* |
| ***at first*** | in the early stage, before things changed | **yes** | *At first it wouldn't start, but then it caught.* |
| ***firstly*** | item one of an argument | no | *Firstly, the cost. Secondly, the timing.* |
| ***to begin with*** | either of the above two | sometimes | *To begin with, we didn't have the money.* |
| ***initially*** | formal *at first* | **yes** | *Initially the plan was to fly.* |

***At first* is not a list opener.** It announces that things later changed, and using it for step one of a recipe is the classic error:

> ✗ ***At first**, brown the onions.* → ***First**, brown the onions.*  
> ✓ ***At first** I didn't like the job. Now I can't imagine doing anything else.*

***Firstly* is stiff in American English.** The natural American series is *first, second, third*, not *firstly, secondly, thirdly*, and the one thing never to do is mix them (✗ *first … secondly*); both are treated at [*firstly*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/03-ly-forms-that-changed-meaning.md#firstly) and [*lastly*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/03-ly-forms-that-changed-meaning.md#lastly). ***Originally*** belongs nearby but does something else: it names the state before a change, with no sequence implied — *The house was **originally** a barn.*

### The middle: *then*, *next*, *after that*, *subsequently*

***Then*** is the workhorse of English narrative and instruction. It normally sits at the front of its clause, very often after *and*.

> I finished the report, **and then** I went home. &middot; Brown the onions. **Then** add the garlic.  
> First it snowed, **then** it rained, **then** it froze.

*Then* is an **adverb, not a conjunction**, which matters when it joins two full clauses. *I finished the report, then I went home* is a comma splice; careful writing makes it *and then*, or a semicolon, or two sentences. In speech nobody notices.

***Next*** is the list word — front position, numbered order: ***Next**, check the oil.* Watch the noun phrase it hides inside: ***next week*** is the week coming, while ***the next week*** is the week after some point already named.

***After that*** is the narrative alternative to *then*, and it exists partly to block an error that Russian makes inevitable:

> ✗ ***After**, we went to the store.* → ***After that**, we went to the store.*

Bare *after* is a preposition (*after lunch*) or a conjunction (*after we ate*); it is not an adverb in American English. The adverb is *after that*, or *afterward*.

***Subsequently*** is the formal one, and it hides a genuine trap: **it means "after that," not "as a result."** Writers reach for it when they want *consequently*, and the sentence then says something they did not mean.

> The report was filed in March. The audit began **subsequently**. — later, no causal claim  
> The report was filed late. **Consequently**, the audit was delayed. — because of it

Both are in the [linking adverbs](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/13-linking.md#subsequently), where the whole causal set lives.

### Closing: *finally*, *at last*, *lastly*, *eventually*, *in the end*

Five words English keeps apart that most learners' first language does not.

| Word | Means | Emotion | Slot |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***finally*** | last in a list **or** after a long wait | depends on position | front = list, mid = relief |
| ***at last*** | after a long, frustrating wait | **relief** | front, end, or alone |
| ***lastly*** | last item in a list | none | front only; formal |
| ***eventually*** | after a long time and some obstacles | none | front or mid |
| ***in the end*** | after uncertainty, this was the outcome | none | front or end |
| ***ultimately*** | when everything is taken into account | none | front or mid; formal |

***Finally* changes meaning with its position**, and this is the most useful single fact in the group:

> **Finally**, don't forget to lock up. — the last item on my list  
> We **finally** ate at ten. — after a long wait, at last

***At last* is never a list marker.** It is pure relief, and it is emotional; *At last!* is a complete sentence.

> ✗ ***At last**, add the salt.* → ***Finally**, add the salt.* &middot; ✓ *The bus came **at last**.*

***Eventually* does not mean "possibly."** It is a false friend for speakers of several European languages, Russian included by way of «эвентуально»: German *eventuell* and French *éventuellement* mean *possibly*; English *eventually* means *in the end, after a while*.

> They argued for an hour and **eventually** gave up. — it did happen  
> ✗ *We will **eventually** come — we're not sure yet.* → *We **may** come.*

### *in the end* against *at the end*

The classic error pair, and the rule fits on one line: ***at the end* takes *of*; *in the end* takes nothing.** *In the end* is an adverb meaning "after everything, this was the outcome"; *at the end of X* names a **point** in something.

> **In the end**, we decided to drive. — after weighing it up  
> **At the end of** the movie she leaves town. &middot; There's a gas station **at the end of** this road.  
> We stayed till **the end**. — the *of* is understood: the end of the game

> ✗ ***At the end**, we decided to drive.* → ***In the end**, we decided to drive.*  
> ✗ ***In the end of** the film, she leaves.* → ***At the end of** the film, she leaves.*

### From a point onward: *from then on*, *ever since*

***From then on*** marks a permanent change dating from a past moment; *from that day on* and *from that point forward* are the same frame.

> He missed one payment. **From then on**, they called every week.

***Ever since*** is three parts of speech at once, and takes the present perfect whenever the effect reaches into now.

> I**'ve** hated flying **ever since**. — adverb  
> **Ever since** the accident, she takes the train. — preposition + noun  
> **Ever since** I moved here, the rent has gone up every year. — conjunction + clause

***Since then*** does the same work when the point has already been named; ***thereafter*** is the formal and legal version. Entries at [*ever since*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#ever-since) and [*since then*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#since-then).

## Simultaneity

### *while*, *as*, *just as*

***While*** takes a **clause**, and that clause often carries a continuous verb, because *while* wants a stretch rather than a point.

> **While** I was cooking, he set the table. &middot; She read the paper **while** the coffee brewed.  
> **While waiting** for the bus, I called my mother. — reduced; the subjects must match

The commonest error is supplying a noun instead of a clause. That is *during*'s job:

> ✗ ***While** the meeting, she took notes.* → ***During** the meeting…*  
> ✗ ***During** I was waiting…* → ***While** I was waiting…*

***As*** has two temporal readings, and context does all the sorting.

> **As** the sun went down, it got colder. — two things developing together  
> **As** I opened the door, the phone rang. — at that exact moment

*As* also means *because*, which makes some sentences genuinely ambiguous — *As she was leaving, I didn't ask* reads either way. Use *while* or *when* when the time reading has to be certain. ***Just as*** insists on the exact coincidence, and nearly always introduces an interruption: ***Just as** we sat down, the power went out.*

**The tense pattern** across all three: the longer background event takes the continuous, the interrupting one the simple — *I **was walking** home when it **started** to rain*. That pairing is the business of [the past continuous](../04-tenses/06-past-continuous.md).

### *meanwhile*, *in the meantime*, *all the while*, *at the same time*

***Meanwhile*** is an **adverb, not a conjunction.** It links two sentences; it cannot introduce a clause.

> I cooked dinner. **Meanwhile**, he set the table.  
> ✗ ***Meanwhile** I cooked dinner, he set the table.* → ***While** I cooked dinner, he set the table.*

***In the meantime*** narrows *meanwhile* to the interval **before an expected event**: *The part comes Thursday. **In the meantime**, use the loaner.*

***All the while*** means "throughout the whole time," and almost always sets up a contrast with what was visible on the surface: *He smiled and nodded, **all the while** planning to quit.*

***At the same time*** is literal simultaneity — and also a discourse connector meaning "on the other hand," which is a second life worth knowing.

> They both started talking **at the same time**. — literally  
> It's expensive. **At the same time**, we need it. — concession

***Simultaneously*** is the technical and formal one: at home in a lab report, stilted in conversation.

### The moment it happens

A cluster of subordinators for "the instant one thing happens, the other does."

> **As soon as** you land, call me. &middot; **The moment (that)** he walked in, everybody stopped talking.  
> **The minute** I said it, I regretted it. &middot; **The second** the bell rang, they were gone.  
> **Once** the paperwork clears, the money shows up in two days.

All of them take **the present for a future meaning** — ✗ *as soon as you will land* — the same rule that governs every time clause in English, set out in [the present simple](../04-tenses/01-present-simple.md#11-time-and-condition-clauses-where-english-refuses-the-future). The full inventory is in [subordinators of time](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md).

***No sooner … than*** and ***hardly / scarcely … when*** are the formal, emphatic versions. Both **invert** after the fronted adverb, and both put the earlier event in the past perfect.

> **No sooner had** we sat down **than** the phone rang.  
> **Hardly had** she started speaking **when** he interrupted.  
> **Scarcely had** the ink dried **before** they changed their minds.

> ✗ *No sooner we had sat down…* → *No sooner **had we** sat down…* — the inversion is obligatory  
> ✗ *No sooner had we sat down **when**…* → *…**than**…*

The memory hook: ***no sooner*** **is a comparative, so it takes *than*; *hardly* is not, so it takes *when*.** Entries at [*no sooner … than*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#no-sooner--than) and [*hardly … when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#hardly--when).

***On*** **+ *-ing*** is the compressed written version of *as soon as*; *upon* is the same thing one degree more formal. *On* + noun works identically.

> **On arriving**, he called the office. &middot; **Upon hearing** the news, she sat down. &middot; Payable **on receipt**. &middot; **On arrival**, proceed to the desk.

The subject of the *-ing* must be the subject of the main clause, or the participle dangles:

> ✗ ***On arriving**, the office was closed.* → ***When he arrived**, the office was closed.*

## Where time adverbs go

### The three positions

| Position | Where exactly | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Front** | before the subject | ***Yesterday** she called me.* |
| **Mid** | before the main verb, after *be*, after the first auxiliary | *She has **just** called me.* &middot; *She is **still** here.* |
| **End** | after the verb and its object | *She called me **yesterday**.* |

**End position is the default for time**, and it is the neutral choice. Front position sets a frame or contrasts with the sentence before it. Mid position is a narrow slot that only a short list of time adverbs can enter.

### Which adverb goes where

| Adverb | Front | Mid | End |
|---|---|---|---|
| *now* | ✓ | ✓ *(formal: I now see)* | ✓ |
| *just* | ✗ | ✓ **only** | ✗ — except the fixed phrase *just now* |
| *already* | marked | ✓ | ✓ |
| *yet* | ✗ | formal only | ✓ |
| *still* | ✓ *(= nevertheless)* | ✓ **only** | ✗ |
| *anymore* | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ **only** |
| *no longer* | inversion; literary | ✓ **only** | ✗ |
| *soon*, *shortly*, *recently* | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| *lately* | ✗ | rare | ✓ |
| *today*, *tonight*, *yesterday* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| *two days ago*, *last week* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| *at the moment*, *these days*, *nowadays* | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| *finally*, *eventually*, *immediately* | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| *then*, *next*, *after that* | ✓ | rare | ✓ |

The pattern behind the table: **one-word adverbs can go mid; phrases cannot.** *I've recently moved* is fine and ✗ *I've two days ago moved* is not, for the same reason ✗ *I every day check my email* is not.

### What moving one changes

The position never changes the facts. It changes what the sentence is **about** and what it **emphasizes**.

> **Finally**, we ate. — the last item in an account  
> We **finally** ate. — relief; it took forever

> **Suddenly** the door opened. — dramatic; the front slot is where narrative surprise lives  
> The door opened **suddenly**. — reads almost as manner: a sudden kind of opening

> I saw him **yesterday**. — answers *when did you see him?*  
> **Yesterday** I saw him. — answers *what happened yesterday?*, and contrasts with today

The principle: **English puts new information last.** Whatever sits at the end of the clause is what the sentence is telling you; whatever sits at the front is the frame it is being told inside.

### When several stack up

**Specific before general** — *at 7:30 on Tuesday morning*, *at eight last night*, *early in the spring of 1994*. **Duration, then frequency, then when** — *I worked **for two hours** **every morning** **last week**.* And across adverb types the end order is **manner — place — time**: *She sang **beautifully** **at the church** **on Sunday**.* The complete ordering rules are in [Adverbs §4](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/README.md).

## For Russian speakers

### 1. уже and ещё: two words into five

The block to learn by heart. Russian sorts the whole phasal set with two words plus negation; the English choice depends on **polarity** in a way Russian never asks about.

| Russian | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **уже** *(affirmative)* | ***already*** | *Он уже ушёл.* → *He**'s already** left.* |
| **уже** *(neutral question)* | ***yet*** | *Он уже ушёл?* → *Has he left **yet**?* |
| **уже** *(surprised question)* | ***already*** | *Ты уже уходишь?* → *You're leaving **already**?* |
| **ещё не**, **пока не** | ***not … yet*** | *Он ещё не пришёл.* → *He hasn't come **yet**.* |
| **(всё) ещё** | ***still*** | *Он всё ещё здесь.* → *He**'s still** here.* |
| **всё ещё не** | ***still*** + negative | *Он всё ещё не позвонил.* → *He **still hasn't** called.* |
| **уже не**, **больше не** | ***not … anymore*** / ***no longer*** | *Он здесь больше не работает.* → *He **doesn't** work here **anymore**.* |
| **ещё один**, **ещё пять минут** | ***another*** / ***one more*** | *Ещё пять минут.* → ***Another** five minutes.* |
| **ещё лучше**, **ещё холоднее** | ***even*** / ***still*** + comparative | *Ещё лучше.* → ***Even** better.* / *Better **still**.* |

The errors these produce, in order of frequency:

> ✗ *He **already doesn't** work here.* → *He **doesn't** work here **anymore**.* — уже не  
> ✗ *I **didn't finish still**.* → *I **haven't** finished **yet**.*  
> ✗ *Have you **already** eaten?* *(meaning a plain question)* → *Have you eaten **yet**?*  
> ✗ *He **hasn't still** called.* → *He **still hasn't** called.*  
> ✗ *Give me **still** five minutes.* → *Give me **another** five minutes.*  
> ✗ *This one is **still** better.* *(meaning ещё лучше)* → *This one is **even** better.*

That last pair is an ambiguity rather than a flat error: *still better* does mean *even better* in careful writing, but in speech it is far more often heard as "better in spite of everything." *Even better* is unambiguous.

### 2. больше не

Russian **больше** is a comparative of quantity, and translating it word by word produces ✗ *I don't work there **more***. English has two fixed answers and no third.

> *Я там больше не работаю.* → *I **don't** work there **anymore**.* — everyday &middot; *I **no longer** work there.* — formal  
> ✗ *I don't work there **no more**.* &middot; ✗ *I **no more** work there.*

Remember the spelling: ***anymore*** (one word) is time; ***any more*** (two words) is quantity.

### 3. наконец and the в конце концов trap

| Russian | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **наконец** *(relief)* | ***finally*** / ***at last*** | *Наконец-то!* → ***At last!*** |
| **наконец** *(last in a list)* | ***finally*** / ***lastly*** | ***Finally**, don't forget to sign it.* |
| **в конце концов** *(outcome)* | ***in the end*** | ***In the end**, we drove.* |
| **в конце концов** *(justification)* | ***after all*** | ***After all**, he's only nine.* |
| **в итоге**, **в конечном счёте** | ***in the end*** / ***ultimately*** | |
| **в конце фильма** | ***at the end of** the movie* | a point, not an outcome |

**The trap:** «в конце концов» is built out of «конец», so it comes out as ✗ *at the end* — and *at the end* is not an adverb in English. It is a prepositional phrase that needs *of* something.

> ✗ ***At the end**, we decided not to go.* → ***In the end**, we decided not to go.*

### 4. потом and тогда

| Russian | English | When |
|---|---|---|
| потом *(next in sequence)* | ***then***, ***next*** | *Сначала подпиши, потом отправь.* → *First sign it, **then** mail it.* |
| потом *(after an interval)* | ***later*** | *Поговорим потом.* → *We'll talk **later**.* |
| потом *(narrative)* | ***after that***, ***afterward*** | *Потом мы пошли домой.* → ***After that**, we went home.* |
| тогда *(at that time)* | ***then***, ***back then***, ***at the time*** | *Тогда я жил в Омахе.* → ***Back then** I was living in Omaha.* |
| тогда *(in that case)* | ***then*** | *Тогда позвони ей.* → ***Then** call her.* |

English *then* is ambiguous between "at that time" and "next," and it relies on the **tense** to sort them: *Then I **was living** in Omaha* is «тогда»; *Then I **moved** to Omaha* is «потом». When the ambiguity would bite, write *back then* or *at the time*. And the error that comes out of «потом» and «после» is bare *after*: ✗ ***After**, we went to the store* → ***After that**…*

### 5. сейчас

One Russian word, three English structures, sorted by the tense of the verb it sits with.

> *Я сейчас занят.* → *I**'m** busy **right now**.* — present  
> *Я сейчас его видел.* → *I **just** saw him.* / *I saw him **just now**.* — past  
> *Сейчас приду.* → *I**'ll** be right there.* — future

The past reading is the surprising one for an English ear, and it is why a Russian speaker's *now* sometimes lands in the wrong tense.

### 6. Word order

Russian places a time adverb almost anywhere and lets intonation carry the weight. English has one narrow mid slot and one default end slot, and stepping outside them is ungrammatical rather than merely odd.

> ✗ *I **yesterday** saw him.* → *I saw him **yesterday**.*  
> ✗ *He works **still** here.* → *He **still** works here.*  
> ✗ *She has **two weeks ago** left.* → *She left **two weeks ago**.*

### What transfers for free

Three things. Russian fronts a time expression to set a frame exactly as English does — «Вчера я его видел» is *Yesterday I saw him*, same function, same effect. «Тем временем» maps onto *meanwhile* with no adjustment, including the restriction that it links sentences rather than introducing a clause. And «как только» is *as soon as* sense for sense, with the single caveat that Russian puts the future in that clause and English will not: ✗ *as soon as he **will arrive*** → *as soon as he **arrives***.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **have seen** him two weeks **ago**.* | *I **saw** him two weeks ago.* | *Ago* is a finished-time expression; the present perfect refuses one. |
| ✗ ***Ago** two weeks…* | ***Two weeks ago**…* | *Ago* follows its measurement. |
| ✗ *He said he'd seen her two days **ago**.* | *…two days **before** / **earlier**.* | Inside a report the anchor is no longer *now*. |
| ✗ *He **already doesn't** work here.* | *He **doesn't** work here **anymore**.* | уже не is *not … anymore*, never *already* + negative. |
| ✗ *I **didn't finish still**.* | *I **haven't** finished **yet**.* | Negative + expected event takes *not … yet*, at the end. |
| ✗ *He **hasn't still** called.* | *He **still hasn't** called.* | *Still* goes to the left of the negative auxiliary. |
| ✗ *I have **yet** finished.* | *I **haven't** finished **yet**.* | *Yet* needs a negative or a question, and sits at the end. |
| ✗ *He doesn't live here **no more**.* | *…**anymore**.* | *No more* is a quantity; the double negative is nonstandard. |
| ✗ *He **doesn't no longer** work here.* | *He **no longer** works here.* | *No longer* carries its own negation. |
| ✗ *Give me **still** five minutes.* | *Give me **another** five minutes.* | ещё один is *another*, not *still*. |
| ✗ ***At first**, brown the onions.* | ***First**, brown the onions.* | *At first* announces a later change; it is not step one. |
| ✗ ***At last**, add the salt.* | ***Finally**, add the salt.* | *At last* is relief, never a list marker. |
| ✗ ***At the end**, we decided to drive.* | ***In the end**, we decided to drive.* | *At the end* needs *of* + something. |
| ✗ ***In the end of** the movie…* | ***At the end of** the movie…* | A point in the film, not an outcome. |
| ✗ ***After**, we went home.* | ***After that**, we went home.* | Bare *after* is not an adverb in American English. |
| ✗ *We will **eventually** come — we're not sure.* | *We **may** come.* | *Eventually* means "in the end," not "possibly." |
| ✗ ***Meanwhile** I cooked, he set the table.* | ***While** I cooked, he set the table.* | *Meanwhile* is an adverb, not a conjunction. |
| ✗ ***During** I was waiting…* | ***While** I was waiting…* | *During* takes a noun; *while* takes a clause. |
| ✗ *No sooner **we had** sat down **when**…* | *No sooner **had we** sat down **than**…* | Obligatory inversion, and *no sooner* is a comparative. |
| ✗ *I'll call you as soon as I **will get** home.* | *…as soon as I **get** home.* | Time clauses take the present for a future meaning. |
| ✗ *I **yesterday** saw him.* | *I saw him **yesterday**.* | Deictic phrases take front or end, never mid. |
| ✗ *She **lately** moved to Tulsa.* | *She **recently** moved to Tulsa.* | *Lately* cannot point at a single past event. |

## Quick reference

**The phasal square — the five words in one picture.**

```
                    NOT CHANGED YET                 CHANGED
   affirmative      still                           already
                    He still works here.            He has already left.

   negative         not ... yet                     not ... anymore / no longer
                    He hasn't left yet.             He doesn't work here anymore.

   positions        still → MID, before the negative      yet → END
                    already → MID neutral, END surprise   anymore → END only
                    no longer → MID only
```

**Counting back and forward.**

```
   from NOW ........ two weeks ago      + past simple
   from a PAST pt .. two weeks before / earlier   + past perfect
                     two weeks later    + past simple   (forward)
```

**Three pairs that decide a sentence.**

> ***first*** starts a list &middot; ***at first*** promises a "but"  
> ***finally*** ends a list &middot; ***at last*** expresses relief  
> ***in the end*** is an outcome &middot; ***at the end of*** is a point

**The emphatic simultaneity frames.**

> **No sooner had** we sat down **than** the phone rang. — a comparative, so *than*  
> **Hardly had** she spoken **when** he interrupted. — not a comparative, so *when*

**The Russian map, compressed.**

```
   уже (+) → already          уже (?) → yet           ещё не → not ... yet
   всё ещё → still            уже не / больше не → not ... anymore / no longer
   ещё один → another         ещё + comparative → even
   наконец → finally / at last     в конце концов → in the end     в конце X-а → at the end of X
   потом → then / next / later / after that     сейчас → right now / just now
```

## Practice

Choose the right word, fix the error, or translate — the instruction is in each item.

1. Choose: *He (still / yet) hasn't answered my email.*
2. Choose: *Have you finished (already / yet)?* — you want a neutral question.
3. Fix: *He already doesn't live here.*
4. Fix: *I have met her two months ago.*
5. Fix: *At first, preheat the oven to 375.*
6. Fix: *At the end, we decided to take the train.*
7. Fix: *In the end of the book, everything is explained.*
8. Fix: *Meanwhile I was on the phone, she made coffee.*
9. Fix: *No sooner we had left when it started raining.*
10. Fix: *After, we drove to my sister's.*
11. Choose: *She (recently / lately) sold the house.*
12. Choose: *I haven't been sleeping well (recently / lately).*
13. Report this: *"I saw him three days ago," she said.*
14. Translate: *Он здесь больше не работает.* — give the everyday and the formal version.
15. Translate: *Подожди ещё пять минут.*
16. Translate: *Тогда я жил в Чикаго.* — make the "at that time" reading unmistakable.
17. What is the difference between *Finally, we ate* and *We finally ate*?
18. What are the two meanings of *just now*, and which one is American?
19. Why is *presently* a word to avoid?
20. Put *yesterday* in every position it allows in *I called the bank*, and say which is neutral.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *He **still** hasn't answered my email.* | *Still* goes before the negative auxiliary and adds impatience; *yet* would go at the end and be neutral. |
| 2 | *Have you finished **yet**?* | *Already* in a question means "so soon?" |
| 3 | *He **doesn't** live here **anymore**.* / *He **no longer** lives here.* | уже не is *not … anymore*, not *already* + negative. |
| 4 | *I **met** her two months ago.* | *Ago* takes the past simple. |
| 5 | ***First**, preheat the oven to 375.* | *At first* announces a later contrast; it cannot open a recipe. |
| 6 | ***In the end**, we decided to take the train.* | An outcome, not a point. |
| 7 | ***At the end of** the book, everything is explained.* | A point in the book; *at the end* requires *of*. |
| 8 | ***While** I was on the phone, she made coffee.* | *Meanwhile* is an adverb and cannot introduce a clause. |
| 9 | *No sooner **had we** left **than** it started raining.* | Obligatory inversion; *no sooner* is a comparative, so *than*. |
| 10 | ***After that**, we drove to my sister's.* / ***Afterward**…* | Bare *after* is a preposition or conjunction, never an adverb. |
| 11 | *She **recently** sold the house.* | A single past event — *lately* cannot do that. |
| 12 | *I haven't been sleeping well **lately**.* | A stretch of time, negative, continuous — *lately*'s home ground. |
| 13 | *She said she**'d** seen him three days **before** / **earlier**.* | The anchor is no longer *now*, so *ago* has to change. |
| 14 | *He **doesn't** work here **anymore**.* &middot; *He **no longer** works here.* | End position for *anymore*, mid for *no longer*. |
| 15 | *Wait **another** five minutes.* / *Wait five **more** minutes.* | ещё один is *another*, never *still*. |
| 16 | ***Back then** I was living in Chicago.* / *I was living in Chicago **at the time**.* | Bare *then* is ambiguous between "at that time" and "next." |
| 17 | *Finally, we ate* = the last item in an account. *We finally ate* = after a long wait, with relief. | Position changes the meaning of *finally*. |
| 18 | "A moment ago," with the past simple — **the American sense**; and "at this moment," commoner in British English. | The "in a little while" sense is not American at all. |
| 19 | It means both *soon* and *currently*, and nothing in the sentence reliably sorts them out. | Write *soon* or *currently*. |
| 20 | ***Yesterday** I called the bank* (frame or contrast) &middot; *I called the bank **yesterday*** (**neutral**). Mid is impossible. | Deictic phrases take front or end, never mid. |
