# Schedules, appointments and deadlines

> The working adult's half of the time topic — making a time, confirming it, moving it, killing it, and the deadline vocabulary that runs from *EOD* to *grace period*.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

Everything else in this topic tells you how to **name** a moment. This section is about **negotiating** one. It is the part of the language a working adult uses most and the part a course usually skips, because it is made of collocations rather than rules: you cannot derive *pencil me in* from the grammar of *pencil*, and nothing about *push back* tells you which direction it pushes.

Three facts shape the whole section.

**American scheduling language is blunt.** *Does Tuesday work for you?* is not casual or rude; it is the neutral, professional way to ask, and it is what a vice president writes to a vice president. Russian politeness formulas — «Не могли бы вы сообщить, когда вам было бы удобно?» — translate into English that sounds either foreign or faintly sarcastic. The American move is to propose a specific time and let the other person counter. Vagueness is the discourtesy here, not directness.

**The vocabulary comes in opposed pairs that are easy to invert.** *Push back* and *move up* point in opposite directions; *by* and *until* attach to opposite kinds of verb; *hard* and *soft* deadlines differ in whether missing one costs anything; *on the clock* and *off the clock* decide whether you are being paid. Each pair is one word apart and produces a real-world consequence when reversed — a meeting at the wrong hour, work delivered a week late.

**Most of it is written, not spoken.** Meetings are arranged in email and calendar invitations, which have their own compressed register: no greeting flourishes, times in a fixed format, abbreviations that appear nowhere else in English. Three model texts at the end show what that looks like on the page.

## The words for the thing itself

### *Schedule*, and how to say it

The first problem with *schedule* is the pronunciation, and it is the one word in this entire topic where American and British English simply disagree.

| | Pronunciation | Spoken as |
|---|---|---|
| **American** | /ˈskɛdʒuːl/ | *SKEJ-ool* |
| **British** | /ˈʃɛdjuːl/ | *SHED-yool* |

Say **SKEJ-ool** in the United States. It is not a regional variant or an informal one: it is the only pronunciation most Americans have ever heard, and *SHED-yool* will be noticed every time. The derived words follow: *rescheduling* is /riˈskɛdʒuːlɪŋ/, *scheduler* is /ˈskɛdʒulər/.

*Schedule* is also a verb, and a very common one: *We scheduled it for Thursday. She's scheduled to present at two. I'll get it scheduled today.* Note the preposition — you schedule something **for** a time, never ✗ *on* a time.

> Can we **schedule** something **for** next week?  
> The surgery is **scheduled for** the ninth. &middot; He's **scheduled** to land at 6:40.  
> Nothing on my **schedule** moves before Thursday.  
> We're two weeks **behind schedule**, and the train is running **ahead of schedule**, which never happens.

*On schedule*, *behind schedule* and *ahead of schedule* take no article. *On time* is the everyday equivalent; the difference between *on time* and *in time* is in [Idioms and set phrases](07-idioms-and-set-phrases.md).

### *Schedule*, *agenda*, *itinerary*, *timeline*, *calendar*

Five nouns that Russian tends to cover with «расписание», «график» and «план», and that English keeps strictly apart.

| Word | What it lists | Typical home |
|---|---|---|
| **schedule** /ˈskɛdʒuːl/ | when things happen | a person's week, a train, a class |
| **agenda** /əˈdʒɛndə/ | what will be discussed, in order | one meeting |
| **itinerary** /aɪˈtɪnəˌrɛri/ | where you go and when, on a trip | travel |
| **timeline** /ˈtaɪmlaɪn/ | the phases of a project against dates | a project, a plan |
| **calendar** /ˈkæləndər/ | the grid itself, and by extension your commitments | the app, the wall, your availability |

> Can you send me the **agenda** before the call? — not ✗ *the schedule*, if you mean the topics  
> Our **itinerary** has us in Santa Fe by Thursday night.  
> What's the **timeline** on the rollout? — phases and dates, not a clock time  
> It's on my **calendar**. &middot; My **calendar** is a disaster this week.

Two traps. **An agenda is not a schedule**: an agenda without times is perfectly normal (*1. Budget. 2. Hiring. 3. Anything else.*). And ***agenda* has a second, political sense** — a hidden purpose — so *What's your agenda?* asked of a person is an accusation, while *What's the agenda?* asked of a meeting is a normal question.

### Slots, windows and openings

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **slot** | a fixed, named piece of a schedule | *I can give you a thirty-minute **slot** at four.* |
| **window** | a stretch inside which something can happen | *The delivery **window** is between ten and two.* |
| **opening** | a slot nobody has taken yet | *Do you have any **openings** on Thursday?* |

A fourth word, *availability* /əˌveɪləˈbɪləti/, is the standard one in scheduling email: *What's your availability next week?* It is uncountable — ✗ *my availabilities* — and the adjective is *available*: *Are you available at three?*

### What kind of meeting it is

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **standing meeting** | one that repeats at a fixed time and needs no new invitation |
| **recurring** | the calendar app's word for the same thing — *a recurring invite* |
| **one-off** | happening once, not part of a series |
| **ad hoc** /ˌæd ˈhɑk/ | called for a specific purpose as the need arises |
| **all-hands** | the whole company or department |
| **one-on-one** (**1:1**) | two people, usually a manager and a report, usually recurring |

> We have a **standing** Monday sync at nine. &middot; Is this **recurring**, or a **one-off**?  
> It was an **ad hoc** thing — somebody noticed the numbers were wrong.

### *Back-to-back* and *double-booked*

> I'm **back-to-back** until four. — meetings with no gap between them  
> I'm **double-booked** at two. — two things claim the same slot, and one has to go  
> I'm **booked solid** through Thursday. &middot; I'm **slammed**. &middot; I'm **swamped**.

These are not the same state. *Back-to-back* is about density and is nobody's fault; *double-booked* is a conflict that has to be resolved, and admitting it is the normal way to ask somebody to move: *I'm double-booked at two — any chance we could do three?*

## Making an appointment

### Asking whether somebody is free

From most casual to most formal. The middle of this list is where most working email lives.

| Register | Question |
|---|---|
| Casual | *Are you around Tuesday?* &middot; *Free Thursday?* &middot; *You got a minute?* |
| Neutral spoken | *Are you free on Tuesday?* &middot; *Do you have time this week?* &middot; *What does your week look like?* |
| Neutral written | *Does Tuesday work for you?* &middot; *Are you available Tuesday afternoon?* &middot; *When works for you?* |
| Formal | *Would you be available on Tuesday the 14th?* &middot; *Please let me know a time that works for you.* |
| Very formal | *I am writing to request an appointment to discuss…* &middot; *Kindly advise when it would be convenient.* |

*When works for you?* is a complete, ordinary question, subject and all — not a fragment, and not casual. Note also the preposition and its absence. *Free **on** Tuesday*, *free **at** three*, *free **in** the morning* — but American English deletes *on* freely before a bare weekday: *Are you free Tuesday?* is completely standard. What is never possible is ✗ *free at Tuesday*. The whole system is in [Prepositions of time](03-prepositions-of-time.md).

### Proposing a specific time

Asking about availability in the abstract usually produces a slow answer. Proposing a time produces a fast one, and that is the American default.

> **Does 3 p.m. work for you?**  
> **How does Thursday at two sound?**  
> **Would Tuesday at ten be okay?** &middot; **Can we say Thursday?**  
> **How about** Friday morning? &middot; **What about** the 14th?  
> **I was thinking** Tuesday afternoon, if that's not terrible for you.

*Work for you* is the workhorse phrase of the whole section, and it takes a time as its subject: *Does Tuesday work? Does two work? Would next week work better?* Its negative is equally standard: *Tuesday doesn't work for me — Wednesday does.* *How does X sound?* is warmer and slightly more casual, and it can take a whole plan as its subject: *How does lunch on Thursday sound?*

### The verbs that put it on a calendar

| Verb | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **make an appointment** | a professional who sees people one at a time | *I need to **make an appointment** with the dentist.* |
| **schedule** | anything, neutral and slightly formal | *Let's **schedule** it for Thursday.* |
| **book** | a resource: a room, a flight, a table, a service | *I'll **book** the conference room.* |
| **set up**, **arrange** | informal work register; *arrange* is the formal one | *Can we **set something up** for next week?* |
| **get on the calendar** | the thing exists but has no time yet | *Let's **get it on the calendar** before it slips.* |
| **pencil in** | tentatively, subject to change | ***Pencil me in** for Thursday.* |
| **block off** | reserve time for yourself, not a meeting | *I **blocked off** an hour to write it.* |
| **send an invite** | the calendar object, not the meeting | *I'll **shoot you an invite**.* |

> I'd like to **make an appointment** with Dr. Okafor.  
> Can we **set something up** for next week?  
> I'll **book** a room and **shoot you an invite**.  
> Let me **pencil you in** for Thursday at two and we'll confirm Monday.  
> I **blocked off** two to four so nobody could take it.  
> Can you **put it on my calendar**?

**Pencil in** earns its own note. The metaphor is pencil against ink: a penciled appointment can be erased. *Pencil me in* means "yes, tentatively, but I am not promising," and Americans hear it as exactly that soft. **Shoot me an invite** — also *shoot me an email*, *shoot me a text* — is informal but entirely ordinary in American offices; it is the register of a Slack message, not risky slang.

### *Appointment* or *meeting*?

Russian «встреча» covers both, and choosing wrong is one of the most audible non-native touches in office English.

| Word | Who it is with |
|---|---|
| **appointment** | a professional whose time you book: doctor, dentist, lawyer, hairdresser, mechanic, the DMV |
| **meeting** | colleagues, clients, partners — two or more people with equal claim on the time |
| **call** | any meeting held by phone or video, however many people |
| **interview**, **consultation** | a job candidate or a journalist's subject; a paid first meeting with a specialist |

> ✗ *I have an **appointment** with my manager at three.* → *I have a **meeting** with my manager at three.*  
> ✗ *I have a **meeting** with the dentist.* → *I have a dentist **appointment**.*  
> I have a **doctor's appointment** at four, so I'll be out.

*Doctor's appointment*, *dentist appointment*, *hair appointment* — the possessive sticks to *doctor's* and *dentist's* and drops elsewhere, and both are heard. Nobody will notice either way.

### Two registers, side by side

| Move | Informal | Formal |
|---|---|---|
| Open | *Hey — got time this week?* | *I hope this finds you well. I would like to arrange a time to speak.* |
| Propose | *Does Thursday at 2 work?* | *Would Thursday, March 12, at 2:00 p.m. be convenient?* |
| Confirm | *Thursday at 2 it is.* | *Thursday at 2:00 p.m. is confirmed. I have sent a calendar invitation.* |
| Decline | *Can't do Thursday — how's Friday?* | *Unfortunately I have a conflict on Thursday. Would Friday be possible?* |

Neither column is more correct. The left is what a colleague writes to a colleague, and what most Americans use most of the time; the right is for a first contact, an outside counterpart, or anyone senior enough that you are still guessing.

## Saying yes, saying no, and saying "how about Thursday"

### Yes

> **That works for me.** — the default confirmation  
> **That works.** &middot; **Works for me.** &middot; **Two works.** &middot; **Sounds good.** &middot; **Perfect.**  
> **Thursday it is.** — a confirmation with a small flourish  
> **I'm free then.** &middot; **I'm open at two.** &middot; **Put me down for Thursday.**  
> **See you then.** — closes the exchange; nothing further is needed

*That works for me* is the one to learn first. It is neutral, it is professional, it cannot be too casual for any office, and it does not sound enthusiastic when you do not feel enthusiastic.

### No, and the vocabulary of being busy

A flat *I can't* is rare. The American convention is to name a reason, however thin, and immediately offer an alternative — and the reasons are almost all idiomatic.

| Phrase | Literally | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **I'm booked.** | my calendar is full | any conflict |
| **I have a conflict.** | two things overlap | the neutral office word |
| **I'm tied up.** | I am occupied | a stretch of time you cannot leave |
| **I'm slammed / swamped.** | overwhelmed | a whole busy period |
| **I'm back-to-back.** | no gaps | a specific day |
| **Something came up.** | an unforeseen thing | canceling, usually last-minute |
| **I'm out that week.** &middot; **I'm OOO Thursday.** | away, on leave | vacation, travel; *OOO* is written only |

> **I'm booked** Tuesday, but Wednesday is wide open.  
> **I have a conflict** at two — could we do three?  
> **I'm tied up** until about four.  
> Sorry, **something came up** and I have to push our one-on-one.  
> **I won't be able to make it** — can we find another time?

*Something came up* is the standard, almost formulaic excuse for a late cancellation. It is vague on purpose, nobody asks what came up, and asking would be odd. What it should always be followed by is a replacement time; canceling without one is what turns an inconvenience into a slight.

### *Rain check*

An Americanism, common enough that a learner will meet it in the first month and opaque enough that nobody guesses it.

**The origin is baseball.** When a game was rained out, the stadium gave ticket holders a *rain check* — a stub good for admission to a later game. The phrase spread to retail (a *rain check* on a sale item that is out of stock, redeemable at the sale price when it comes back in) and from there into ordinary speech.

**What it means now:** *no this time, yes another time* — a decline that explicitly preserves the invitation.

> **Can I take a rain check?** — the standard form  
> I'll have to **take a rain check** on dinner — I'm buried this week.  
> "Drinks after work?" "**Rain check.** I've got my kid tonight."

Two notes. It is **social, not corporate** — you take a rain check on drinks, a dinner, a weekend trip, not on a quarterly review. And it is **spelled as two words** in American usage, though *raincheck* appears and nobody objects.

### Counter-proposing

Declining and proposing are one move, not two, and the alternative comes in the same sentence.

> **Can we do Thursday instead?** &middot; Tuesday's tough — **how about Wednesday?**  
> **Would Friday be any better?** &middot; **Same time Thursday?**  
> I can't do mornings. **Any chance of an afternoon?**  
> That's cutting it close for me — **could we say four instead of three?**

*Instead* goes at the end of the clause: *Can we do Thursday instead?*, not ✗ *Can we instead do Thursday?* And *Any chance…?* is an extremely useful softener that takes either a noun phrase or an *of* + *-ing*: *Any chance of a later slot? Any chance we could move it?*

## Changing the time

### The neutral verbs

| Verb | Direction | Register | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **reschedule** /riˈskɛdʒuːl/ | either | neutral | the safe general word |
| **move** | either — needs a target | neutral | *move it to Friday*, *move it an hour* |
| **push back** | **later** | neutral to casual | separable: *push it back* |
| **move up** | **earlier** | neutral to casual | separable: *move it up* |
| **postpone** /poʊstˈpoʊn/ | later | slightly formal | a deliberate decision |
| **delay** | later | neutral | usually caused, not chosen |
| **put off**, **bump** | later | casual | *put it off*, *they bumped us to Thursday* |
| **defer** | later | formal | decisions and payments, not meetings |

> Can we **reschedule** for Friday? &middot; They've **rescheduled** twice already.  
> Let's **move** it **to** two. &middot; We **postponed** the launch **until** spring.  
> The flight was **delayed** three hours.  
> I keep **putting off** that conversation. &middot; They **bumped** our review to next month.

**Reschedule** takes *for* or *to* with a new time and needs no object repetition: *Can we reschedule for Friday?* is complete. ✗ *Can we reschedule the meeting on Friday?* mixes two patterns; write *reschedule the meeting for Friday*.

**Postpone versus delay.** *Postpone* is a decision somebody made about a scheduled event; *delay* is something being made late, usually by circumstances. A flight is *delayed*; a wedding is *postponed*. ✗ *We delayed the meeting to Friday* is odd — you **moved** or **pushed** it. And *postpone* takes *until* in careful writing (*postponed until March*), though *postponed to March* is common in speech. Full entries: [work and business verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/28-core-work-business.md), and [Phrasal verbs of time](06-phrasal-verbs-of-time.md) for the rest.

### *Push back* and *move up*: the pair that reverses

This is the single most consequential pair in the section, because getting it backwards puts you in a room on the wrong day.

> ***Push back*** **= later.** *Can we **push** the meeting **back** to four?* — it was at three  
> ***Move up*** **= earlier.** *Can we **move** the meeting **up** to two?* — it was at three

The metaphor is a calendar page, where earlier is higher up and later is further along. *Up* is toward the top of the page — earlier. *Back* is against the direction of travel — later.

| Phrase | New time is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **push back** | later | *Pushed back to Friday.* |
| **move back** | later | *Moved the call back an hour.* |
| **put off**, **bump** | later | *Put it off until next quarter.* &middot; *Bumped to the 14th.* |
| **move up** | **earlier** | *Moved the deadline up a week.* |
| **bump up**, **advance** | **earlier** | *They bumped up the launch date.* |
| **move forward** | **ambiguous — avoid** | could be either |

**Two things to be careful about.** First, ***move forward* is genuinely ambiguous** and native speakers disagree about it: some hear *move the meeting forward* as earlier (forward toward now), others as later (forward along the timeline). Americans argue about this in real offices, and the result is a missed meeting. Do not use it — say *push it back*, *move it up*, or, safest of all, **give the new time**: *Let's move it to Thursday at two.*

Second, the amount versus the destination. Both verbs take either.

> **Push it back an hour.** — by one hour, to four &middot; **Push it back to four.** — to that time  
> **Move it up two days.** — two days earlier &middot; **Move it up to the 12th.** — to that date

Confirming the actual clock time afterward is not fussy; it is standard practice, and the reason calendar invites exist.

> ✗ *Can we **push up** the meeting to four?* → *Can we **push back** the meeting to four?* — *push up* is not a scheduling verb at all  
> ✗ *They **moved back** the launch to an earlier date.* → *They **moved up** the launch.*

### Canceling, and not showing up

| Word | What it is |
|---|---|
| **cancel** /ˈkænsəl/ | decide it will not happen |
| **call off** | cancel something planned, often at short notice |
| **scrap** | cancel and abandon, informal |
| **fall through** | collapse on its own — plans, deals, not people |
| **no-show** | somebody who did not come, and did not say |
| **stand somebody up** | fail to show for a date or a personal meeting |
| **blow off**, **flake** | skip deliberately, or back out at the last minute; *a flake*, *flaky* |

> They **canceled** the whole conference. &middot; The game was **called off** because of lightning.  
> Our plans **fell through**, so I'm free after all.  
> We had three **no-shows** this morning.  
> He **stood her up**. — a social, personal failure, not a corporate one  
> Don't **blow off** the orientation; they take attendance.

American spelling keeps one *l*: **canceled**, **canceling** — but the noun is **cancellation** with two, everywhere in the world. Memorize the pair. And **no-show** is a noun, an adjective (*a no-show fee*) and now a verb (*two people no-showed*); clinics and restaurants charge for it, which is why it turns up on paperwork.

## Deadlines

### The words

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **deadline** /ˈdɛdlaɪn/ | the moment by which something must be finished |
| **due date** | the same thing, softer — also a pregnancy and a library book |
| **due** (adjective) | required by then: *the report is due Friday* |
| **cutoff** | the moment after which something is not accepted at all |
| **drop-dead date** | the absolute last possible date; nothing moves past it |
| **target date**, **milestone** | what you are aiming at, and a dated checkpoint inside a longer project |

> The **deadline** is Friday at five. &middot; We missed the **deadline** by two hours.  
> The report is **due** Friday. — *due* takes a bare date with no preposition  
> When is it **due**? &middot; It's **due** the 15th. &middot; It's **due by** noon.  
> The **cutoff** for registration is March 1.

The verbs that go with *deadline* are worth listing because they do not translate: you **meet** a deadline, **make** one, **miss** one, **hit** one, **blow** one (informal), **set** one, **extend** or **push back** one, and a deadline **slips**. You do not ✗ *fulfill* or ✗ *realize* one.

### *By*, *until*, *through*, *within*

The *by* / *until* contrast is worked out in general in [Prepositions of time](03-prepositions-of-time.md). In the deadline register it narrows to one rule, and the rule is about the verb.

**A deadline is a boundary, so it takes *by*. A span of availability is a stretch, so it takes *until*.**

| Preposition | What it attaches to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **by** | a **completed** action — a single event that must be done no later than | *Get it to me **by** Friday.* |
| **until** / **till** | a **continuing** state or action that stops at that point | *You have **until** Friday.* |
| **through** | a span **including** the last day (American) | *The offer runs **through** Friday.* |
| **within** | a stretch measured from now | *We'll respond **within** 24 hours.* |
| **no later than** / **as of** | formal *by*; a state beginning at a point | *Submit **no later than** 5:00 p.m.* &middot; ***As of** Monday…* |

The confusing part is that both prepositions appear in perfectly ordinary deadline sentences — because they attach to different verbs:

> The report is due **by** Friday. ✓ — *due* is a boundary  
> You have **until** Friday to finish it. ✓ — *have* is a state that continues  
> ✗ *Finish it **until** Friday.* → *Finish it **by** Friday.*  
> ✗ *You have **by** Friday.* → *You have **until** Friday.*  
> I'll be in meetings **until** three, so I'll get to it **by** four.

That last sentence is the whole rule in one line: *be in meetings* continues and takes *until*; *get to it* completes and takes *by*.

**American *through* is inclusive.** *Monday through Friday* includes Friday; *the sale runs through the 15th* includes the 15th. British English writes *Monday to Friday*, and for an American reader *to the 15th* is unclear about whether the 15th counts — which is why forms and contracts write *through*, or spell it out as *to and including*.

### Hard and soft deadlines

An American office distinction with no grammar behind it and real consequences attached to it.

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **hard deadline** | missing it has a consequence — money, a lost slot, a legal problem |
| **soft deadline** | a target; missing it is noticed but survivable |
| **drop-dead date** | the hardest possible deadline, usually contractual — while a **hard stop** is not a deadline at all, but the moment you must *leave* |

> Is the 15th a **hard deadline** or a **soft** one?  
> The filing date is a **hard deadline** — the court doesn't take late submissions.  
> We have a **drop-dead date** of June 1 in the contract.

Asking *Is that a hard deadline?* is not a challenge and is not rude. It is a standard, expected clarifying question, and an American manager will usually answer it honestly.

### *EOD*, *COB*, and the rest of the alphabet

Abbreviations that appear in email, in chat, and almost never in speech — with two exceptions noted below.

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Means | Said aloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EOD** | end of day | ambiguous — see below | /ˌi oʊ ˈdi/, spelled out |
| **COB** | close of business | 5:00 p.m. in the recipient's time zone | /ˌsi oʊ ˈbi/ |
| **EOB** | end of business | the same as COB | spelled out |
| **EOW**, **EOM**, **EOQ** | end of week, month, quarter | Friday; the last business day; the fiscal quarter's end | spelled out |
| **ASAP** | as soon as possible | no fixed time — see below | /ˈeɪsæp/ "AY-sap", or spelled out |
| **TBD**, **TBC** | to be determined / confirmed | no time set; a time set but not final | spelled out |
| **ETA** | estimated time of arrival | when it will land, literally or not | /ˌi ti ˈeɪ/ |
| **OOO** | out of office | away | usually read as *out of office* |
| **PTO** | paid time off | vacation and personal leave | /ˌpi ti ˈoʊ/ |
| **NLT** | no later than | a hard boundary; military and government | spelled out |

> Can you get me the draft **by EOD**?  
> I'll have it to you **by COB Thursday**.  
> The invoice needs to go out **by EOM**.  
> I'm **OOO** next week — **ETA** back in the office is the 14th.

**EOD is genuinely ambiguous, and this causes real problems.** In some offices it means the end of the *workday* — five or six in the evening. In others, especially where work crosses time zones, it means the end of the *calendar* day, midnight. The two readings are seven hours apart. If the difference matters, **do not use it**: write *by 5 p.m. Thursday* or *by midnight Thursday*. And if somebody else uses it, asking *EOD as in 5, or as in midnight?* is a normal question that marks you as careful rather than as a beginner.

**ASAP has a register problem.** It is genuinely urgent in some mouths and a verbal tic in others, and it gives the recipient nothing to plan around. A named time is always better — *by 2 today* instead of *ASAP* — and the safe reply to one names a time back: *I can have it to you by 3, does that work?* **COB and EOB**, meanwhile, assume a time zone, usually the recipient's; across zones, say it: *by 5 p.m. Eastern*. The zones are in [Time zones and American conventions](09-time-zones-and-american-conventions.md).

### Lead time, turnaround and buffer

Three nouns that measure the space around a deadline rather than the deadline itself.

| Noun | Measures | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **lead time** | how far in advance you need the request | *We need two weeks' **lead time** on custom orders.* |
| **turnaround time** | how long the work takes once it starts | *Our **turnaround** is 48 hours.* |
| **buffer** | slack built in against surprises | *I built in a two-day **buffer**.* |
| **runway** | how much time remains before something runs out | *We have three months of **runway**.* |
| **bandwidth** | capacity, not time — but used as if it were | *I don't have the **bandwidth** this week.* |

> How much **lead time** do you need? &middot; What's your usual **turnaround**? — *time* is regularly dropped  
> Give yourself a **buffer**; the printer always takes longer than they say.  
> I don't have the **bandwidth** to take that on right now. — a polite refusal, very common

Watch the apostrophe in the measure phrase: **two weeks' notice**, **a week's lead time**, **three days' warning**. The noun is possessive and the apostrophe follows the *s* in the plural. ✗ *two weeks notice* is an error common enough to have named a movie.

### Late

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **overdue** | past its deadline — general |
| **past due** | past its payment date — billing |
| **late** / **running late** | after the appointed time; on the way and going to arrive late |
| **extension** | more time, granted |
| **grace period** | a span after the due date during which no **penalty** or **late fee** applies |
| **slip** (verb) | to move later without anybody deciding it would |

> The report is two weeks **overdue**. &middot; Your account is **past due**. — the invoice wording  
> I'm **running late** — start without me.  
> Can I ask for an **extension**? &middot; They gave us a two-day **extension**.  
> There's a ten-day **grace period** before the late fee kicks in.  
> The timeline **slipped** again.

***Running late* versus *late*.** *I'm running late* is said on the way, before you arrive, and it is the phrase to text. *I'm late* is said once you are there, or about a habit (*he's always late*). Both are correct English; only one is the right thing to send from the car. And ***overdue* has a second, positive life** as an idiom: *This conversation is long overdue* means it should have happened sooner and is welcome now — a compliment, not a complaint.

## Duration at work

### The hyphen rule, which is not optional

When a duration sits **in front of** a noun and modifies it, it becomes a compound adjective: **hyphenated, and singular**.

| Before the noun (hyphen, singular) | After the verb (no hyphen, plural) |
|---|---|
| *a **half-hour** meeting* | *The meeting lasted **half an hour**.* |
| *a **30-minute** slot* | *The slot is **30 minutes** long.* |
| *a **two-hour** delay* | *We were delayed **two hours**.* |
| *a **three-day** extension* | *They gave us **three days**.* |

> ✗ *a **30-minutes** meeting* → *a **30-minute** meeting*  
> ✗ *a **two hours** delay* → *a **two-hour** delay*  
> ✗ *a **three days** weekend* → *a **three-day** weekend*

The same word can be either: *a half hour* is a noun phrase (*I waited a half hour*), *a half-hour* a modifier (*a half-hour show*). Both *half an hour* and *a half hour* are standard American and mean the same thing, and *an hour and a half* is the usual way to say ninety minutes.

### Blocking, running long, hard stops

> I'll **block off** an hour Thursday morning. &middot; Can you **block** two to four on my calendar?  
> The meeting **ran long**. — it went past its end time  
> We **ran over** by twenty minutes. — the same, with an amount  
> Let's try to **wrap up on time** today. &middot; Let's **wrap this up** — I have another call.  
> I have a **hard stop** at four. &middot; Sorry, **hard stop** — I'll follow up by email.

***Hard stop*** is the single most useful phrase in this section for a non-native speaker in American meetings. It means: I must leave at this time, it is not negotiable, and I am telling you now so nobody is offended later. Announcing it at the start (*Heads up, I have a hard stop at four*) is courteous, not abrupt — and once announced, leaving at four needs no apology.

***Run long*** and ***run over*** both mean exceeding an allotted time, and they differ in what follows: *run long* stands alone, *run over* takes an amount or nothing. ✗ *The meeting ran long by ten minutes* is not idiomatic; use *ran over*. ***Wrap up*** means finish, transitive (*wrap up the presentation*) or not (*let's wrap up*), and *wrap* alone works in speech.

## The hours themselves

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **business hours**, **office hours** | when a business is open — typically 9:00 to 5:00, Monday through Friday |
| **after hours** | outside business hours; also an adjective (*an after-hours call*) |
| **off hours** | the quiet parts of the day, when nothing is busy |
| **peak** / **off-peak** | the busy and quiet parts of a pricing schedule |
| **on the clock** / **off the clock** | being paid for this time, or not |
| **overtime** | hours past the standard week, usually paid at a higher rate |
| **time and a half** | the usual overtime rate — 1.5× |
| **flextime** | freedom to choose your start and end times |
| **core hours** | the hours a flextime employee must still be present |
| **full-time** / **part-time** | roughly 40 hours a week, or fewer |
| **shift** | a block of hours a worker covers |
| **graveyard shift** | the overnight shift, roughly 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. |
| **swing shift** | the evening shift, roughly 3 p.m. to 11 p.m. |
| **nine-to-five** | a conventional salaried job, with a faint sigh attached |
| **on call** | reachable and obliged to respond outside normal hours |
| **PTO** | paid time off — vacation and personal days in one pool |
| **work-life balance** | how much of your life the job leaves you |

> Call the **after-hours** line if it's an emergency.  
> Try to go **off hours** — the line is an hour long at noon.  
> I'm **off the clock**, but I'll take a look tonight anyway.  
> They pay **time and a half** past forty hours, and we have **flextime** with **core hours** ten to three.  
> He works the **graveyard shift** at the hospital.  
> I'm **on call** this weekend, and I'm taking **PTO** the week of the 20th.  
> It's a **nine-to-five** — decent money, no surprises.

Three notes an American would assume you already know. **PTO is one pool:** most American employers no longer separate vacation days from sick days, so *I'm taking PTO* is the neutral way to say you will be away, and giving no reason is not rude. **Overtime is a legal category:** non-exempt hourly employees are owed time and a half past forty hours, salaried exempt employees are not, so *I worked a lot of overtime* from a salaried person means "long hours," not "extra pay." And ***off the clock* has a second sense** — "unofficially, not for the record": *Off the clock, what do you actually think of the proposal?*

## Three model texts

These are what the register looks like on the page. Each is short on purpose; American work email is shorter than a Russian equivalent and puts the request in the first line.

**1. A meeting invitation.**

```
Subject: 30 min this week — Q3 numbers

Hi Marta,

Do you have 30 minutes this week to go over the Q3 numbers
before they go to the board? Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon
both work on my end — Tuesday at 2 would be ideal.

Let me know what works and I'll send an invite.

Thanks,
Ray
```

Notice what is missing: no apology for writing, no inquiry after the recipient's health, no closing formula longer than one word. The shape — **duration, purpose, two options, one preference, a request to reply** — is close to fixed.

**2. A deadline reminder.**

```
Subject: Reminder — expense reports due Friday 5 p.m.

Quick reminder that Q3 expense reports are due by 5 p.m.
Friday, October 3. This one is a hard deadline — anything
that comes in after Friday goes into the next quarter.

If you need an extension, email me before Thursday and
I'll see what I can do.

Thanks,
Priya
```

The pattern: **what**, **by when in full**, **hard or soft**, **what happens if you miss it**, **how to ask for more time**. Note *by 5 p.m. Friday, October 3* — day name and date together, because one of them is always misread.

**3. A reschedule email.**

```
Subject: Re: Thursday 2 p.m. — need to move

Hi Dan,

Something came up Thursday afternoon and I need to push our
call back. Would Friday at 2 work, same length? If Friday is
bad, I'm fairly open Monday morning.

Sorry for the short notice — I'll send an updated invite as
soon as you confirm.

Ray
```

The pattern: **a one-line reason**, **the verb that says which direction it moves**, **a specific alternative**, **a fallback**, **a short apology**, **who will send the new invite**. The apology is one clause. Extending it past that reads as either anxious or insincere.

## For Russian speakers

**1. «Записаться» fans out.** Russian uses one reflexive verb for every act of putting your name in somebody's book; English picks a different verb depending on what is being booked.

| Russian | English | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| записаться к врачу, на приём | **make an appointment**, **book an appointment** | a professional's time |
| записаться на курс | **sign up for**, **enroll in**, **register for** | a class, a course |
| записаться в очередь | **get on the list**, **put my name down** | a waiting list |
| записаться в спортзал | **join** (a gym), **sign up** | a membership |
| записать что-то в календарь | **put it on the calendar** | your own schedule |

> ✗ *I want to **write myself** to the doctor.* → *I want to **make an appointment** with the doctor.*  
> ✗ *I **recorded** for the course.* → *I **signed up** for the course.*  
> ✗ *Please **write me** for Tuesday.* → *Please **put me down** for Tuesday.*

**2. «Перенести» splits into two verbs that point in opposite directions.** This is the error with the largest practical cost in the whole topic. Russian «перенести» is neutral about direction and lets «на» plus the new date supply it. English has no neutral common verb: *push back* means later and *move up* means earlier, and a learner who has met only one of them will use it for both.

| Russian | English | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| перенести на более поздний срок, отложить | **push back**, **postpone**, **put off**, **defer** | later |
| перенести на более ранний срок | **move up**, **bump up** | **earlier** |
| перенести (на другое время, нейтрально) | **reschedule**, **move** | either — needs a target |
| отменить | **cancel**, **call off** | gone |

> ✗ *Can we **transfer** the meeting to Friday?* → *Can we **move** the meeting to Friday?*  
> ✗ *They **carried over** the interview to Monday.* → *They **pushed** the interview **back** to Monday.*  
> ✗ *We **moved up** the deadline to next month.* → *We **pushed back** the deadline to next month.*

The reliable strategy when you are unsure: use ***reschedule*** and then **name the new time**. *Can we reschedule for Friday at two?* cannot be misunderstood in either direction, and it is perfectly good English at any level of formality.

**3. «Срок» is three different English nouns.**

| Russian | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| срок (сдачи) | **deadline**, **due date** | *The **deadline** is Friday.* |
| срок (действия) | **term**, **period**, **validity** | *a five-year **term*** |
| сроки (проекта) | **timeline**, **time frame** | *What's the **timeline**?* |
| в срок | **on time**, **by the deadline** | *We finished **on time**.* |
| в кратчайшие сроки &middot; продлить срок | **as soon as possible** &middot; **get an extension** | |

> ✗ *What is the **term** of the report?* → *When is the report **due**?* &middot; ✗ *We finished **in term**.* → *We finished **on time**.*

**4. The prepositions: «в» before a day, «до» before a deadline.** «Во вторник» maps onto *on Tuesday*, but the *at* of «в три часа» leaks onto the day and produces the commonest error in this section. And «до пятницы» does two jobs with one word, where English splits them by what the verb is doing.

> ✗ *I will be free **at** Tuesday.* → *I will be free **on** Tuesday.* / *I'll be free Tuesday.*  
> ✗ *The meeting is **in** Monday.* → *The meeting is **on** Monday.*  
> ✗ *Let's meet **at** the next week.* → *Let's meet **next week**.* — no preposition at all  
> ✗ *Please send it **until** Friday.* → *Please send it **by** Friday.*  
> ✗ *I'm busy **by** Friday.* → *I'm busy **until** Friday.*

*At* takes a clock time, *on* takes a day or a date, *in* takes a month, season or year, and *next*, *last*, *this* and *every* take **nothing** (*next week*, *last Friday*, *every Tuesday*). The whole system, including the zero-preposition rule, is in [Prepositions of time](03-prepositions-of-time.md).

**5. American scheduling language is more direct than Russian politeness leads you to expect.**

Russian arranges a time through conditional and impersonal formulas — «Не могли бы вы сказать, когда вам было бы удобно?», «Если вас не затруднит…» — and translating them produces English too heavy for the situation. A native speaker reading *Would you perhaps be so kind as to inform me when it might be convenient for you to meet?* concludes one of three things: that you are being sarcastic, that something is wrong, or that English is not your first language.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| *Would you be so kind as to inform me of your availability?* | *When works for you?* |
| *I would be extremely grateful if you could find the time.* | *Do you have 30 minutes this week?* |
| *If it is not difficult for you, could we possibly meet on Tuesday?* | *Does Tuesday work?* |
| *I apologize profusely for the inconvenience caused.* | *Sorry for the short notice.* |

Two transfers to watch. **Do not stack softeners** — *Could you possibly maybe just see if perhaps Tuesday might work?* is less polite in English, not more, because it makes the reader work. One is plenty: *Any chance Tuesday works?* And **do not apologize more than once**; the convention is a single short apology, then the fix.

**6. Three calques worth naming.**

> ✗ *I will be **on** the meeting.* → *I'll be **in** a meeting.* / *I'll be **at** the meeting.*  
> ✗ *I **have no time** this week.* → *I **don't have time** this week.* — the first is grammatical but dramatic  
> ✗ *I didn't **succeed** to finish.* → *I didn't **have time** to finish.* / *I didn't **get to** it.* — «успеть» has no single English verb; the everyday equivalents are ***make it***, ***have time to***, ***get to***, and ***make the deadline***

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I'll be free **at** Tuesday.* | *I'll be free **on** Tuesday.* | *At* takes a clock time; a day takes *on*, or nothing. |
| ✗ *Send it **until** Friday.* | *Send it **by** Friday.* | A completed action takes *by*; *until* needs a continuing one. |
| ✗ *You have **by** noon.* | *You have **until** noon.* | *Have* is a continuing state, so it takes *until*. |
| ✗ *We **moved up** the deadline to next month.* | *We **pushed back** the deadline to next month.* | *Move up* means earlier; *push back* means later. |
| ✗ *Can we **push up** the meeting?* | *Can we **push back** the meeting?* / *…**move** it **up**?* | *Push up* is not a scheduling verb. |
| ✗ *Let's **move** the meeting **forward**.* | *Let's **move it to Thursday at two**.* | *Move forward* is ambiguous even to Americans. Name the time. |
| ✗ *Can we reschedule the meeting **on** Friday?* | *Can we reschedule the meeting **for** Friday?* | *Schedule* and *reschedule* take *for*. |
| ✗ *I have an **appointment** with my boss.* | *I have a **meeting** with my boss.* | *Appointment* is for a professional whose time you book. |
| ✗ *a **30-minutes** meeting* | *a **30-minute** meeting* | A duration before a noun is hyphenated and singular. |
| ✗ *two **weeks** notice* | *two **weeks'** notice* | The measure phrase is possessive. |
| ✗ *The meeting **ran long by** ten minutes.* | *The meeting **ran over by** ten minutes.* | *Run long* takes no amount; *run over* does. |
| ✗ *We **delayed** the meeting **to** Friday.* | *We **moved** the meeting **to** Friday.* | *Delay* is something done to you, not a rescheduling verb. |
| ✗ *I am late, I will be there in ten minutes.* | *I'm **running late** — I'll be there in ten.* | *Running late* is said on the way; *late* once you have arrived. |
| ✗ *I will be **on** the meeting.* | *I'll be **in** a meeting.* | *In a meeting* is the fixed phrase. |
| ✗ *They **cancelled** it.* (US context) | *They **canceled** it.* | American spelling keeps one *l* — but *cancellation* keeps two. |
| ✗ *Can we **transfer** the call to Friday?* | *Can we **move** the call to Friday?* | «Перенести» is not *transfer* when it is about time. |
| ✗ *I **recorded** for the class.* | *I **signed up** for the class.* | «Записаться» chooses a different verb for each thing booked. |
| ✗ *What is the **term** of the report?* | *When is the report **due**?* | «Срок» is *deadline* or *due date* here, not *term*. |

## Quick reference

**The direction words, once more.**

```
   EARLIER  ←────────────────────────────────→  LATER
   move up                                      push back / move back
   bump up                                      put off / postpone
   advance                                      bump / defer

                  reschedule / move  — neutral; name the new time
                  move forward       — ambiguous. Do not use it.
```

**The deadline preposition, in one question.**

```
   Does the verb describe something that FINISHES,
   or something that CONTINUES?

   FINISHES ──── by      Send it by Friday.  It's due by noon.
   CONTINUES ─── until   You have until Friday.  I'm tied up until noon.
   A SPAN ─────── through  The offer runs through Friday. (Friday included)
```

**The five phrases to learn first.**

> **Does Tuesday work for you?** — the standard way to propose a time  
> **That works for me.** — the standard way to accept one  
> **I have a conflict — can we do Thursday instead?** — decline and counter in one line  
> **Can we push it back to four?** — move it later, unambiguously  
> **I have a hard stop at four.** — I am leaving then, and this is not rude

**Abbreviations, shortest to longest span:** ASAP &middot; EOD &middot; COB / EOB &middot; EOW &middot; EOM &middot; EOQ. When the hours matter, write the hour: *by 5 p.m. Thursday* beats *by EOD* every time.

**Register in one line:** propose a specific time, give one reason if you decline, offer one alternative, apologize once.

## Practice

Fix, complete, or choose. Some items have more than one good answer.

1. ✗ *I'm free at Tuesday afternoon.* — fix the preposition.
2. The report is due ____ Friday, and you have ____ Friday at five to finish it. [*by* / *until*]
3. The meeting was at three. They *pushed it back* an hour — and in another version *moved it up* an hour. Give both new times.
4. ✗ *Can we reschedule the call on Thursday?* — fix the preposition.
5. ✗ *We had a two hours delay, and we need two weeks lead time.* — fix both measure phrases.
6. Your colleague writes: *Can you get it to me by EOD?* It matters whether that means 5 p.m. or midnight. What do you write back?
7. Give the one-line American reply that declines Tuesday and proposes Thursday.
8. ✗ *Sorry, I have no time — I am late for the meeting, I will come in ten minutes.* — rewrite it as a text message from the car.
9. What is a *rain check*, and would you take one on a performance review?
10. ✗ *The presentation ran long by fifteen minutes.* — fix the phrasal verb.
11. Translate the intent: «Не могли бы вы сообщить, когда вам было бы удобно встретиться?»
12. ✗ *They cancelled the flight, and the cancelation cost us a day.* — fix the spelling. Both words.
13. A meeting invitation arrives for a time you cannot commit to but do not want to refuse. What do you reply?
14. ✗ *I recorded myself to the Spanish course.* — fix the verb.
15. What is the difference between a *hard deadline* and a *hard stop*?
16. Name the direction: *put off*, *bump up*, *defer*, *advance*, *postpone*.
17. ✗ *I have an appointment with the marketing team at four.* — fix the noun.
18. You are chairing a meeting and must leave at four no matter what. What do you say at the start, and is it rude?

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *I'm free **on** Tuesday afternoon.* / *I'm free Tuesday afternoon.* | *At* takes a clock time. A day takes *on*, and American English drops it freely. |
| 2 | *due **by** Friday … you have **until** Friday at five* | *Due* is a boundary; *have* is a continuing state. |
| 3 | *Pushed back* → **four o'clock**. *Moved up* → **two o'clock**. | Back is later, up is earlier. |
| 4 | *Can we reschedule the call **for** Thursday?* | *Schedule* and *reschedule* take *for*. |
| 5 | *a **two-hour** delay* &middot; *two **weeks'** lead time* | Before a noun: hyphenated and singular. Standing alone: possessive apostrophe. |
| 6 | *EOD as in 5, or as in midnight?* / *Can you give me a time? I can do 5 p.m. today or first thing tomorrow.* | *EOD* is ambiguous by seven hours; asking is normal and careful. |
| 7 | *I have a conflict Tuesday — does Thursday work?* | Decline, reason, alternative, one line. |
| 8 | *Running late — be there in ten.* | *Running late* is the on-the-way phrase; *I have no time* is the wrong idiom entirely. |
| 9 | A decline that preserves the invitation — *no this time, yes another time*, from the baseball ticket stub. **No** — it is social, not corporate. | Register, not grammar. |
| 10 | *The presentation **ran over** by fifteen minutes.* | *Run long* takes no amount; *run over* does. |
| 11 | *When works for you?* / *Do you have time to meet this week?* | The Russian formula overtranslates. One softener at most. |
| 12 | *They **canceled** the flight, and the **cancellation** cost us a day.* | American *canceled* has one *l*; *cancellation* has two everywhere. |
| 13 | ***Pencil me in.*** | A tentative yes, and Americans hear it as tentative. |
| 14 | *I **signed up** for the Spanish course.* / *I **enrolled in**…* | «Записаться» chooses its English verb by what is booked. |
| 15 | A **hard deadline** is when work must be finished; a **hard stop** is when you must leave the room. | Two unrelated things that share an adjective. |
| 16 | *put off* — later &middot; *bump up* — **earlier** &middot; *defer* — later &middot; *advance* — **earlier** &middot; *postpone* — later | Only two of the five go backward on the calendar. |
| 17 | *I have a **meeting** with the marketing team at four.* | Colleagues get meetings; professionals you book get appointments. |
| 18 | *Heads up — I have a hard stop at four.* **Not rude**; announcing it is the courteous move. | It lets everybody plan the agenda around it. |

### One more pass

Write the three model emails again, changing only the times: a 45-minute invitation for Monday morning, a soft deadline reminder for the 20th, and a reschedule that moves a Wednesday call **up** to Tuesday. The third is the test — the verb has to be *move up*, and the confirmation has to name the new day and hour.
