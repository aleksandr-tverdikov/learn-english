Telling the time#

Every way English has of saying what the clock says — the digital reading and the analog one, a.m. and p.m., noon and midnight, military time, and the half hour that Russian speakers get backwards.

← Time · All grammar topics

English gives you two completely different ways to read a clock, and a fluent speaker uses both without noticing the switch.

It's six forty-five. — read the numerals It's a quarter to seven. — read the face

The first is the digital reading: say the hour, then say the minutes as a number. The second is the analog reading: say how far the minute hand is from the nearest hour, and in which direction. They describe the same instant, they are equally correct, and they do not mix — ✗ a quarter to six forty-five is not a sentence.

One fact shapes everything below: in American English the digital reading is the default. Where a British speaker reaches for a quarter to seven, an American is more likely to say six forty-five, and where a British speaker says half past six, an American almost always says six thirty. The analog forms are alive and completely standard in the US — nobody will blink at a quarter after three — but they are the marked choice, not the neutral one. A learner who masters the digital reading first can say every time on the clock; a learner who masters only the analog one cannot say 6:37 at all.

The rest of this section works through the asking, the two systems position by position, the a.m. / p.m. apparatus, noon and midnight, military time, approximation, punctuation, and the verbs that go with clocks. Where the clock meets a preposition — at six, by six, until six, in an hour — the entries are in prepositions of time.

The polite forms almost always carry an opener: Excuse me — do you have the time? Walking up to a stranger with a bare What time is it? is not wrong, but it lands abruptly.

the time and time are two different questions#

This one is worth isolating, because the error is invisible to the learner and very visible to the listener.

Do you have the time? — What time is it? Do you have time? — Are you free? Can you spare a moment?

The definite article carries the whole difference. The time is the reading on the clock, a specific thing; time is the uncountable stuff, a quantity. So:

✗ Excuse me, do you have time? — if you meant the clock, you have just asked a stranger whether they are busy ✓ Excuse me, do you have the time?

And in the other direction: Do you have a minute? / Do you have a second? ask for a moment of someone's attention and never for the time of day. The uncountable / countable split behind this is in time as a noun.

Word order in the polite version#

Do you know what time it is? is an embedded question, and an embedded question keeps statement word order. The inversion happens once, on do you know, and not again inside.

✓ Do you know what time it is? · ✗ Do you know what time is it? ✓ Could you tell me what time it is? · ✗ Could you tell me what time is it?

Said with a particular intonation, Do you know what time it is? can also be a reproach — Do you know what time it is? to a teenager coming home at two in the morning is not a request for information.

Answering#

The answer begins with It's. English has no way to state a time without a subject, and it is the only subject available.

It's ten after six. · It's almost noon. ✗ Is six o'clock. · ✗ Now six o'clock. ✗ The time is six o'clock now. — grammatical, but it sounds like a recorded announcement

In speech the It's is very often dropped altogether, and the bare time is the most natural answer of all:

"What time is it?" — "Six forty-five." "Got the time?" — "Almost seven." "Do you have the time?" — "Sorry, I don't — my phone's dead."

Sorry, I don't have a watch and Sorry, I don't know are the standard refusals. Note that the short answer repeats the auxiliary, exactly as it does everywhere else: Do you have the time? — No, I don't.

The two systems#

The digital reading#

Hour, then minutes, as two numbers side by side. No preposition, no minutes, nothing between them.

It's six thirty. · It's nine fifteen. The train gets in at eleven forty. Two twenty-five — we've got time.

Two mechanical rules. Minutes under ten take oh. The digit 0 is read /oʊ/, the name of the letter, never zero and never nothing at all.

6:05 → six oh five · 9:01 → nine oh one · 12:09 → twelve oh nine ✗ six five · ✗ six zero five · ✗ six and five

A whole hour is not read with oh oh. 6:00 is six or six o'clock, never ✗ six oh oh — that is military time, and it is read differently again.

The digital reading has one enormous advantage, and it is why Americans default to it: it covers every minute on the clock. 6:37 has no analog reading anyone says. Twenty-three to seven is arithmetic, not English.

6:37 → six thirty-seven · 10:52 → ten fifty-two · 3:08 → three oh eight

The analog reading#

Minutes, then a direction word, then an hour. The direction word tells you which side of the hour you are on.

It's ten after six. — 6:10, past the hour It's twenty to seven. — 6:40, short of the hour

The pivot is the half hour. Up to :30 you count forward from the hour that has just gone; from :31 you count backward to the hour that is coming. The hour named therefore changes halfway around the face, which is the part that has to be internalized rather than worked out.

6:20 — twenty after six · 6:40 — twenty to seven

Both of those are the six o'clock hour on a digital display, and only one of them says six.

The analog reading is restricted to multiples of five, plus the quarters and the half. Nobody says seventeen after six. If the minute number is not a multiple of five, the digital reading is the only option.

Every position on the clock#

The whole hour: o'clock#

O'clock is a contraction of of the clock, and the apostrophe is the surviving mark of the missing the. It is one word, it takes exactly one apostrophe, and it is pronounced /əˈklɑk/ — the o' is a schwa, never /oʊ/.

It's six o'clock. The meeting's at two o'clock. ✗ six oclock · ✗ six o clock · ✗ six aclock · ✗ six o'clocks

Four restrictions, and they are absolute:

1. Whole hours only. O'clock means the hand is on the twelve. Any other minute reading rules it out.

✗ six thirty o'clock · ✗ half past six o'clock · ✗ a quarter to seven o'clock · ✗ 6:30 o'clock

2. The numbers one through twelve only. ✗ eighteen o'clock does not exist in English. Eighteen hundred is military time and is read another way entirely.

3. Never with a.m. or p.m. The two are alternative ways of doing the same job, and using both is like saying the hour twice.

✗ six o'clock p.m. → six p.m. or six o'clock in the evening

4. It is optional, and usually omitted. It's six is the ordinary spoken form; It's six o'clock is a shade more deliberate, and is what you say when the hour itself is the point — I'll be there at six o'clock, not six fifteen.

O'clock survives in a handful of fixed phrases where no time is meant at all: bogey at two o'clock (a direction, from aviation), five o'clock shadow (a man's stubble by late afternoon), the five o'clock news.

past and after#

For minutes between :01 and :30, English offers two words and American English prefers the second.

FormWhereExample
pastuniversal; the default in Britain, common in the USten past six
afterAmerican, and the American defaultten after six

It's five after six. · It's ten past six. Twenty after three — I'm on my way.

Neither is more correct: after sounds slightly more American, past never sounds wrong. What is wrong is pluralizing them — ✗ five afters six, ✗ ten pasts six. The word minutes is normally left out, but it can go back in for precision: It's twenty-two minutes past six.

a quarter past / a quarter after#

Fifteen minutes is a quarter of an hour, and the fraction is the normal way to say it.

It's a quarter after six. — 6:15 It's a quarter past six. Quarter after six. — the article dropped, which is very common in American speech

The article is genuinely optional in the US; in careful writing, keep the a. Quarter is /ˈkwɔrtər/ — two syllables, the vowel of war rather than of car, both r's pronounced.

✗ a quarter past of six — there is no of ✗ fifteen past six — grammatical, and nobody says it ✗ one quarter past six — the fraction takes a, not one

half past — and why Americans say six thirty#

Thirty minutes is half past, and the hour named is the hour that has already struck.

It's half past six. = 6:30 It's half past ten. = 10:30 ✗ half past of six · ✗ half of past six · ✗ half to seven · ✗ a half past six

But the American default is the digital reading. Six thirty outnumbers half past six in American speech by a wide margin. Half past six is correct and instantly understood, and it sounds a little formal or a little British. If you learn one form for :30, learn six thirty.

British half six is not American. In Britain it is a casual clipping of half past six and means 6:30. In the United States it is simply not said, and an American hearing it will either fail to parse it or guess wrongly.

And half past is not half an hour. Half past six is a point in time; half an hour is a length of one. I'll be there in half an hour has nothing to do with :30.

to, of, and till#

For minutes between :31 and :59, English counts down to the next hour, and offers three words for the job.

All three are standard American English within their regions. a.m. and p.m.#

What they abbreviate#

They are Latin, and the full forms explain the whole system:

AbbreviationLatinLiterallyCovers
a.m.ante merīdiembefore midday12:00 midnight → 11:59 in the morning
p.m.post merīdiemafter midday12:00 noon → 11:59 at night

The word is merīdiem, the accusative of merīdiēs, "midday" — not ✗ meridian, which is a line of longitude. Nobody ever says the Latin out loud; a.m. and p.m. are read as two letter names, /ˌeɪ ˈɛm/ and /ˌpi ˈɛm/, with the stress on the second letter.

Punctuation#

American practice has settled on lowercase with periods, and the alternatives are style choices rather than errors.

FormStatus
6:45 p.m.The American standard. Chicago, AP, and most edited prose.
6:45 P.M.Correct, more formal, older. Common in small caps in typeset books.
6:45 pmCorrect, and the British default. Increasingly common in American informal writing.
6:45 PMCommon on signs, in email, and on digital displays. Not wrong, not the style of careful prose.
✗ 6:45p.m.Missing the space.
✗ 6:45 P.m.Mixed case.

Two mechanical points. There is a space between the time and the abbreviation: 6:45 p.m., not ✗ 6:45p.m. And at the end of a sentence, the abbreviation's period doubles as the sentence's: The store closes at 9 p.m. — one period, not two.

When Americans actually use them#

In writing, constantly: schedules, invitations, appointment cards, email — anything where the reader is not in the room to infer the rest. In speech, only when the ambiguity is real.

The flight's at six a.m. — worth saying; six in the evening is just as plausible Dinner's at six. · I get up at seven. — nobody needs the abbreviation

Where a speaker does want to disambiguate out loud, the ordinary choice is not a.m. / p.m. but a phrase:

Instead ofAmericans say
6 a.m.six in the morning
2 p.m.two in the afternoon
7 p.m.seven in the evening
11 p.m.eleven at night
3 a.m.three in the morning — even in the dark

The rough boundaries: morning runs until noon, afternoon from noon to about five or six, evening from then until about nine, and night from there through the small hours. Note that the dead of night is in the morning in English once midnight has passed — three in the morning, never ✗ three at night. The prepositions themselves — in the morning but at night, at noon, at midnight — are catalogued at prepositions of time.

What not to do with them#

Do not combine them with o'clock, and do not combine them with in the morning / at night. Each pair is two solutions to one problem, and the second error is extremely common.

✗ five o'clock p.m. → five p.m. / five o'clock in the afternoon ✗ at 6 a.m. in the morning → at 6 a.m. / at six in the morning ✗ at 11 p.m. at night → at 11 p.m. / at eleven at night ✗ 18:00 p.m. → 18:00 — the 18 already says which half of the day it is

In a range, one abbreviation is often enough when both ends are in the same half: The store is open 9 to 5 p.m. is loose but idiomatic; 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. is the careful version and is never wrong.

Noon and midnight#

These two points are where the a.m. / p.m. system breaks, and the breakage is worth understanding rather than memorizing.

Merīdiēs is noon. Noon is therefore neither before midday nor after it, so strictly speaking 12:00 is neither a.m. nor p.m. Convention has assigned them anyway, and the convention is the opposite of what most people guess:

TimeMeansWhy it confuses
12 p.m.noon — the middle of the dayPeople expect p.m. to mean "evening"
12 a.m.midnight — the start of the dayPeople expect a.m. to mean "daytime"

Because roughly half of any audience gets this wrong, careful American writing avoids the numbers entirely — noon, twelve noon, 12:00 noon; midnight, twelve midnight, 12:00 midnight. AP style drops the 12 as redundant; 12 noon is nevertheless everywhere, because redundancy is exactly what the reader wants here. Both are defensible; ✗ 12 p.m. noon is not.

Midday means noon and is a perfectly good word, but it is much less common in the US than in Britain. Americans say noon, and use midday mostly as a modifier — the midday news, a midday flight.

Why schedules write 11:59 p.m.#

A deadline of "midnight Friday" is ambiguous in a way that costs money. Does it mean the midnight that begins Friday or the midnight that ends it? Both readings are defensible, so contracts, submission portals, coupons and ballots sidestep the question:

Entries must be received by 11:59 p.m. on March 3. Coverage begins at 12:01 a.m. on the first of the month.

Those two minutes are unambiguously inside the day named, and that is the whole reason they are chosen. If you must use midnight in writing, anchor it: midnight on Friday night, midnight between Friday and Saturday.

Fixed phrases: at noon, at midnight — no article, ✗ at the noon. High noon is twelve exactly, with a whiff of the Western. The stroke of midnight is the moment the clock finishes striking twelve. Burn the midnight oil is to work very late. The noun entries are at noon, midnight and midday.

The 24-hour clock: military time#

What Americans call it and where it lives#

The United States is one of the few places where the 24-hour clock is not the civilian default. Americans call it military time, and the name says where it is used:

  • the armed forces, aviation, and air traffic control
  • hospitals, EMS, and medical charting — where a mis-read 6:00 can kill someone
  • police, fire, and dispatch; lab logs, computer systems, and anything internationally facing

It is not used in ordinary conversation, on storefronts, in invitations, on TV listings, or in most American writing. An American who says I'll see you at nineteen hundred about dinner is either in uniform or making a joke.

How it is written#

Four digits, no colon, no a.m. / p.m. The leading zero is obligatory.

0600 · 0915 · 1200 · 1345 · 2359

Civilian and ISO-style writing keeps the colon — 06:00, 13:45 — and that is what you see on a European timetable or in a computer log. Military writing drops it.

The rules behind the table:

  • A whole hour is read as hundred, not o'clock and not oh oh: 1300 is thirteen hundred, never ✗ thirteen o'clock.
  • Hours is often tacked on — thirteen hundred hours. Optional, commoner in speech, and the part civilians imitate when they are being funny.
  • A leading zero is read oh or zero. The Navy tends to zero, the Army to oh; both are ordinary.
  • Minutes are a plain number, exactly as in the civilian digital reading: 1845 is eighteen forty-five.

Converting#

After noon, add 12 to the civilian hour; to go back, subtract 12. 3 p.m. + 12 = 1500. 1900 − 12 = 7 p.m. Before noon the two systems agree, except that military time pads the hour to two digits.

In practice an American reading a 24-hour display converts on the spot and then says the civilian time out loud: It says nineteen forty-five — so, quarter to eight. That is the reflex to imitate.

Four of these repay a closer look.

-ish attaches to any time and makes it casual: sixish, seven-ish, six-thirtyish, noonish. Hyphenate it when the run of letters gets hard to read — seven-ish beats sevenish — and leave it bare otherwise; both spellings are current. It is speech and informal writing only. And around sixish is technically redundant and completely ordinary.

Going on says the hour is close enough to feel as if it has arrived, usually with a note that time has got away from the speaker: It's going on eleven and he still isn't back. The same idiom does ages: She's going on forty.

Sharp comes after the time and exactly comes before it: at six sharp, at exactly six. ✗ at sharp six is not English.

On the dot is the dot on a clock face that marks the minute; on the nose comes from broadcasting hand signals. Both mean exactly, both are informal. The prepositions themselves are at about and around.

o'clock in writing#

Only with whole hours, and normally with the number spelled out: at six o'clock. Never with a.m., never with a minute count, and never abbreviated to ✗ 6 o'c.

Clocks, watches, and the verbs that go with them#

A clock face carries three hands — the short hour hand, the long minute hand, the thin second hand. A clock with hands is analog; one with numerals is digital. Half past and a quarter to describe a hand's position, which is exactly why those readings lose ground every year that fewer clocks have hands.

What it says, and whether it's right#

The clock says ten to six. · What does your phone say? The microwave reads 12:00 — it's been blinking since the power went out. My watch is five minutes fast. — it shows a later time than the real one That clock is slow. — it shows an earlier one It gains a minute a week. · It loses two minutes a day. A good watch keeps good time.

✗ My watch is five minutes quick. → five minutes fast ✗ My watch hurries. → My watch is fast. / It gains time. ✗ My watch is late. → My watch is slow.

For Russian speakers#

Russian and English both read a clock face, and they read it in opposite directions on the second half of the hour. That single fact generates most of the errors below.

1. The half hour is inverted. This is the commonest time error a Russian speaker makes.

«Полседьмого» — «половина седьмого» — is literally "half of the seventh [hour]", meaning half of the stretch between six and seven has gone. It is 6:30. English names the hour that has already struck, not the one being filled:

«Полседьмого» = 6:30 = half past six = six thirty ✗ half past seven — that is 7:30, a full hour late

The two numbers are never the same: Russian says seven, English says six. A mnemonic that works — half past six keeps the six you can still see on the clock, because the hour hand has not reached seven yet. Better still, avoid the construction: say six thirty, which is the American default anyway and cannot be got backwards. British half six is no help either — it is not American, and it removes the past that at least pointed in a direction.

2. Counting down to the next hour transfers for free.

This is the good news. Russian «без» + genitive maps directly onto English to:

RussianEnglishClock
без пяти семьfive to seven6:55
без десяти семьten to seven6:50
без двадцати семьtwenty to seven6:40
без четверти семьa quarter to seven6:45

Same direction, same hour named, same logic. What does not transfer is the word: «без» is without, and ✗ without twenty seven is not English.

3. Russian uses an ordinal and a genitive. English uses a cardinal and nothing.

«Половина седьмого» has an ordinal in the genitive — of the seventh. Learners carry both across, and both are wrong:

✗ half past of six → half past six · ✗ a quarter past of six → a quarter past six ✗ half of the seventh → six thirty · ✗ half past the seventh → half past six

English clock hours are plain cardinals — six, seven, twelve — with no article, no ordinal, and no preposition attached.

4. «В шесть часов» → at six o'clock, with no of and no plural.

«Часов» is a noun in the genitive plural, and it tempts learners into producing a noun in English too:

✓ at six o'clock · ✓ at six ✗ at six of clock · ✗ at six a clock · ✗ at six o'clocks · ✗ at six hours · ✗ at six of the clock

O'clock is not a noun and does not pluralize, take an article, or take a preposition. And the preposition in front is at, always: «в шесть» → at six, never ✗ in six and never ✗ on six. The point-in-time at is at at.

5. Russian has no dummy subject. English requires one.

«Сейчас шесть часов» has no subject at all, and «Шесть часов» on its own is a complete sentence. English needs it:

✗ Now six o'clock. · ✗ Now is six o'clock. · ✗ Is six o'clock. ✓ It's six o'clock. / ✓ It's six.

The same empty it runs the weather (It's raining) and distance (It's two miles). It means nothing; it is there because an English sentence must have a subject.

7. The 24-hour clock is normal in Russia and abnormal in American speech.

Russian writes 19:00 and says «в девятнадцать ноль-ноль» in an announcement, «в семь вечера» in conversation. American English does neither in daily life:

✗ The movie starts at nineteen o'clock. → at seven p.m. / at seven in the evening ✗ I'll be there at nineteen hours. → at seven

Read a 24-hour display the way an American does: convert, then say the civilian time.

8. The four Russian times of day do not match the four English ones.

RussianEnglishCareful
в девять утраat nine in the morning

The boundary that moves is the one at midnight. Russian «ночи» stretches to dawn; English night effectively stops at twelve, and 1 a.m. through 4 a.m. are in the morning however dark it is.

9. Prepositions around the clock.

«в шесть» → at six · «к шести» → by six · «до шести» → until / till six «с шести до восьми» → from six to eight · «после шести» → after six «через час» → in an hour — ✗ after an hour, ✗ through an hour «час назад» → an hour ago · «за час» → in an hour (it took me an hour)

«Через час» is the one that catches everybody: the English word is in. I'll call you in an hour.

10. «Ровно в шесть» has three English equivalents.

at six sharp · at exactly six · at six on the dot

Note the position: sharp and on the dot follow the time, exactly precedes it. ✗ at sharp six.

Quick reference#

The face, and where the pivot is.

                         12
                  :55           :05
             :50                     :10
                     ← to | past →
         :45          of  |  after        :15
                    till  |
             :40                     :20
                  :35           :25
                         :30

    :00        six o'clock  /  six
    :01–:29    ... past / after SIX      the hour behind you
    :30        half past SIX  /  six thirty
    :31–:59    ... to / of / till SEVEN  the hour ahead of you

The three rules that cover everything.

  1. Hour, then minutes. Six forty-five. This reading works for all sixty minutes, is the American default, and can never be got backwards. Learn it first and the clock is solved.
  2. The analog reading pivots at :30. Before the half hour you count past / after the hour behind you; after it you count to / of / till the hour ahead. It works only for multiples of five.
  3. Say each thing once. O'clock or a.m., not both. a.m. or in the morning, not both. from… to… or a dash, not both.

Choosing a reading in one question.

   Are the minutes a multiple of five?

   NO ──── digital only              6:37 → six thirty-seven

   YES ─── Is it :00, :15, :30 or :45?

           NO  ──  digital is safer   6:20 → six twenty
           YES ──  either one         6:45 → six forty-five
                                            a quarter to seven
