# Duration and frequency

> How long something lasts and how often it comes around — *for* against *since*, *during* against *for*, the *It takes me twenty minutes* construction, and the whole scale from *always* to *never*.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

Two questions, and almost nothing in common between the answers. **How long?** asks for a stretch — a span with a length, or with a beginning it can be measured from. **How often?** asks for a count — how many times the same thing comes back around. English keeps the two sets of words so strictly apart that mixing them produces sentences no American would write, and it keeps three slots inside the first set apart just as strictly.

Three facts organize everything below.

**A length is not a point, and a named period is neither.** *Three hours*, *two weeks*, *a long time* are **lengths**, and they take *for*. *Monday*, *2019*, *noon*, *the election* are **points**, and they take *since*. *The meeting*, *the summer*, *the war* are **named periods**, and they take *during*. Three slots, three words, and not one of them covers two slots. That single fact kills ✗ *since three years*, ✗ *during three hours*, and ✗ *for the meeting* (when you meant *during*) in one stroke.

**Duration drags a tense along with it.** *Since* is almost a tense marker: put it in a sentence and the verb goes into the perfect, because *since* opens a span that reaches the present. *For* is neutral — it sits in any tense, and the tense then carries the whole meaning. *I lived there for ten years* says I moved out; *I've lived here for ten years* says I am still here. The expression did not change; the tense did. That contrast is worked out from the tense side in [Tenses](../04-tenses/README.md).

**Frequency comes in two halves with two different rules of position.** **Definite** frequency — *twice a week*, *daily*, *every other day* — is a phrase, and phrases go at the **end** of the clause. **Indefinite** frequency — *always*, *usually*, *never* — is a one-word adverb, and those go in the **middle**, before the main verb and after *be*. ✗ *I go twice a week to the gym* and ✗ *I take always the bus* are the same error made from opposite directions.

The words themselves — pronunciation, Russian glosses, and six examples each — are in the dictionaries: **[adverbs of frequency and duration](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/07-frequency-duration.md)** and **[prepositions of time](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md)**. This section is about choosing between them and building sentences out of them.

## The whole set in one table

Read the third column as a hard limit. Most errors in this section are the wrong kind of thing put after the right word.

| Expression | Means | What follows it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *for* | a length of time | a **length** — *two hours*, *ages* | *We waited **for** twenty minutes.* |
| *since* | a starting point, running to now | a **point** or a past-simple clause | *I've been up **since** five.* |
| *during* | inside a named period | a **noun phrase** naming a period | *He called **during** the meeting.* |
| *in* + period | after that much time from now; or elapsed time to finish | a length | *I'll be back **in** ten minutes.* &middot; *She read it **in** an hour.* |
| *within* | at some point inside that span | a length | *You'll hear back **within** two weeks.* |
| *over* | across a span; also "more than" | a period, or a quantity | *We talked it over **over** the weekend.* |
| *throughout* | continuously, the entire span | a period | *He coughed **throughout** the concert.* |
| *all day / night / week long* | the whole of it, emphatically | nothing — it is complete | *The phone rang **all day long**.* |
| *the whole time* | the entire span just referred to | nothing | *She knew **the whole time**.* |
| *from start to finish* | end to end, nothing left out | nothing | *It was a mess **from start to finish**.* |
| *around the clock* | twenty-four hours a day | nothing | *The crews worked **around the clock**.* |
| *on end* | one after another, exhaustingly | follows a **plural** length | *He'd read **for hours on end**.* |
| *straight* | with no break | follows a length | *She slept ten hours **straight**.* |
| *solid* | with no break (before the noun) | precedes a length | *I spent **two solid hours** on hold.* |
| *up to* | a maximum | a length | *Delivery takes **up to** six weeks.* |
| *as long as* | a surprisingly large maximum | a length | *It can take **as long as** a year.* |
| *How long does it take…?* | asking the time a task costs | *to* + infinitive | ***How long does it take to** get downtown?* |
| *It takes* + person + time | that is the cost in time | *to* + infinitive | ***It takes me** twenty minutes **to** get there.* |

| *every so often*, *once in a while* | occasionally | nothing | *Check the oil **every so often**.* |

| *sooner or later*, *eventually* | at some unspecified later point | nothing | *He'll find out **sooner or later**.* |

## Duration: how long

### *For* + a length of time

***For*** **measures.** It takes a length and nothing else, and it fits every tense in the language.

> We waited **for** twenty minutes.  
> She's lived in Tulsa **for** six years.  
> I'll be gone **for** about a week.  
> They talked **for** hours.  
> He was out of work **for** most of 2019.  
> I haven't slept properly **for** ages.

Three practical notes.

**American English drops *for* freely** in the affirmative, when the length comes right after the verb: *We waited twenty minutes. She's lived here six years. I'll be gone a week.* The bare version is the normal spoken form. Keep *for* in negatives and where the phrase is separated from the verb: *I haven't seen her **for** two years* is better than the bare version, and *For six years he refused to speak to them* needs it.

**The tense, not the phrase, says whether it is over.** This is the most useful single contrast in the section:

| Sentence | Means |
|---|---|
| *I lived in Denver **for** ten years.* | I don't live there now. |
| *I've lived in Denver **for** ten years.* | I live there now. |
| *I'd lived in Denver **for** ten years when they transferred me.* | ten years up to that past moment |
| *I'll have lived here **for** ten years in March.* | the span completes in the future |

**A *for* phrase is not a *since* phrase.** ✗ *since ten years* is the single commonest error anyone makes with this pair, and it comes straight out of Russian and half of Europe. *Ten years* is a length. Lengths take *for*.

### *Since* + a starting point

***Since*** **locates a beginning.** It takes a point in time — a clock time, a date, a year, an event, a stage of life — and it opens a span that runs from that point to now.

> I've been up **since** five.  
> She's worked there **since** 2018.  
> We haven't heard from him **since** the funeral.  
> He's been like this **since** he was a kid.  
> Nothing's changed **since** last March.  
> **Since** graduation, she's had four different jobs.

*Since* also takes a whole clause, and the clause goes into the **past simple** while the main clause stays in the perfect, because the clause names a finished starting event:

> I've felt better **since I quit** coffee.  
> They've been closed **since the pipe burst**.  
> **Since she moved out**, the place has been silent.

**The tense *since* forces.** With almost no exceptions, *since* pulls the main verb into a perfect — present perfect, present perfect continuous, or past perfect. ✗ *I am here since Monday* and ✗ *I work here since 2018* are errors of tense, not of preposition, and they are the second half of the same problem that produces ✗ *since three years*.

> ✗ *I **am** here **since** Monday.* → *I**'ve been** here **since** Monday.*  
> ✗ *She **works** there **since** 2018.* → *She**'s worked** there **since** 2018.*  
> ✗ *We **know** each other **since** college.* → *We**'ve known** each other **since** college.*

One genuine exception: *since* also means *because*, and that *since* has nothing to do with time and no tense requirement at all — *Since you're already up, could you get the door?* Nothing but the meaning of the clause separates the two.

### *For* and *since* side by side

The same stretch of time can be described either way. Which one you reach for depends on whether you have measured it or dated it.

| | ***for*** | ***since*** |
|---|---|---|
| Answers | *How long?* | *Since when?* |
| Takes | a **length** | a **point**, or a past-simple clause |
| Typical objects | *two hours, six years, a while, ages, as long as I can remember* | *Monday, noon, 2018, March, the election, I was a kid* |
| Tense it prefers | any | a **perfect**, nearly always |
| Can the span be over? | **yes** — *I worked there for a year* | no — *since* runs to now |

| The situation | With *for* | With *since* |
|---|---|---|
| Started Monday; today is Friday | *I've been here **for** four days.* | *I've been here **since** Monday.* |
| Moved in 2019 | *We've lived here **for** six years.* | *We've lived here **since** 2019.* |
| Met in college | *We've known each other **for** twenty years.* | *We've known each other **since** college.* |
| Woke at five | *I've been up **for** three hours.* | *I've been up **since** five.* |

The test is mechanical, and it never fails: **if you can put a number and a unit on it, it is a length and takes *for*; if you could answer "when?" with it, it is a point and takes *since*.**

### *During* + a named period

***During*** **names a period and never measures one.** It takes a noun phrase that is already a stretch of time — an event, a season, a named era — and it says the main event happened somewhere inside it.

> The lights went out **during** the second act.  
> She reads **during** her commute.  
> We don't take calls **during** lunch.  
> **During** the war, the factory made parachutes.  
> Nobody said a word **during** dinner.

✗ ***during two hours*** **is the error to burn out of your English.** *Two hours* is a length; *during* cannot take one. The repair is *for*:

> ✗ *We waited **during** two hours.* → *We waited **for** two hours.*  
> ✗ *He talked **during** twenty minutes.* → *He talked **for** twenty minutes.*  
> ✗ *It rained **during** three days.* → *It rained **for** three days.*

**The exception that confuses everyone.** *During the two hours we waited* is perfectly good English, and so is *during those three days*. The difference is the article: a **definite** noun phrase names a particular period, and *during* is happy with it. A **bare** measurement is a length, and it is not. Compare ✗ *during three days* with *during **the** three days **of the hearing***.

Two more limits worth knowing. *During* never takes a clause — ✗ *during he was speaking* — and the repair is *while*. And it never takes an *-ing* form either — ✗ *during eating* — where English wants *while eating* or *in the middle of eating*.

| Wanted | Word | Example |
|---|---|---|
| inside a named period | *during* + noun | *during the meeting* |
| inside a measured length | *for* + length | *for an hour* |
| at the same time as a clause | *while* + clause | *while she was speaking* |
| at the same time as an action | *while* + **-ing** | *while eating* |

### *Over* and *throughout*

***Over*** spreads an event across a span, usually with the sense that it happened in pieces rather than continuously. It takes periods, and it is at home with *the weekend*, *the summer*, *the past year*, *the course of*.

> We talked about it **over** the weekend.  
> Enrollment has doubled **over** the past decade.  
> **Over** the course of the evening, four people asked me the same question.

*Over* has a second, unrelated job: **more than**. *It took over three hours* does not mean "across three hours"; it means the total exceeded three. The two readings rarely collide, because the quantity reading needs a bare number and the span reading a definite period — but *over the three hours* and *over three hours* are different sentences, and the article is doing all the work.

***Throughout*** is the continuous one: the whole span, with no gaps. Where *over* allows episodes, *throughout* insists there were none. It also stands alone as an adverb, which *during* cannot do.

> He coughed **throughout** the concert.  
> The policy stayed in place **throughout** the 1990s.  
> She was polite **throughout**, which made it worse.

### *All day long*, *the whole time*, *from start to finish*

***All*** **+ period** is the plain version, and ***long*** is an optional intensifier that goes at the end: *all day*, *all night*, *all week*, *all year*, *all morning*, *all summer* — and *all day **long***, *all night **long***, *all week **long***, which add exasperation or endurance. No preposition, ever: ✗ *for all day*, ✗ *during all night*.

> The phone rang **all day**.  
> He was up **all night** with the baby.  
> I've been on my feet **all day long**.

***The whole*** **+ period** and ***the whole time*** point back at a span already established, and carry a note of "and you didn't notice": *She knew **the whole time**. He slept **the whole way** to Phoenix.*

***From start to finish***, ***from beginning to end***, and the daylight pair ***from dawn to dusk*** and ***from sunup to sundown*** frame a span by its two ends. So does the plain office version, *from nine to five*.

> The project was mismanaged **from start to finish**.  
> They were in the fields **from sunup to sundown**.  
> I'm at a desk **from nine to five**, so evenings are better.

### *Around the clock*, *on end*, *straight*, *solid*

Four intensifiers of duration, each with its own grammar. Getting the slot wrong is what makes them sound foreign.

| Intensifier | Slot | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *around the clock* | alone; hyphenated before a noun | twenty-four hours a day | *The crews worked **around the clock**.* &middot; ***around-the-clock** care* |
| *24/7* | alone | the same, conversational | *That gym is open **24/7**.* |
| *on end* | **after a plural** length | one after another, exhaustingly | *He'd sit there **for hours on end**.* |
| *straight* | after a length | with no break | *She slept ten hours **straight**.* |
| *solid* | **before** the length | with no break, resentfully | *I spent **two solid hours** on hold.* |

✗ *for an hour on end* is not English — the noun has to be plural. *Straight* also works in front of the noun in the fixed frame *the fourth **straight** week of rain*, and *round the clock* is the British form of the first one.

> It rained **for days on end**.  
> I worked six days **straight** and then collapsed.  
> He didn't speak to me for **three solid days**.

### Caps, floors, and approximations

| Frame | Says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *up to* + length | a maximum, often a warning | *Delivery takes **up to** six weeks.* |
| *as long as* + length | a maximum, and it surprises | *Approval can take **as long as** a year.* |
| *no more than* + length | a cap, usually a promise | *This will take **no more than** five minutes.* |
| *at least* + length | a floor | *Give the paint **at least** four hours.* |
| *a good* / *a solid* + length | more than you'd expect | *We waited **a good** forty minutes.* |
| *about*, *around*, *roughly* | an approximation | *It runs **about** two hours.* |
| *or so*, *give or take* | an approximation, trailing | *Twenty minutes **or so**.* &middot; *An hour, **give or take**.* |
| *anywhere from … to* | a stated range | ***Anywhere from** two **to** six weeks.* |

*As long as* is worth a second look, because the same three words are also a conditional connector (*You can stay **as long as** you pay rent*) and a comparison of two durations (*It took **as long as** the first one*). A length after it is a maximum, a clause about a requirement is a condition, and a clause with a second duration in it is a comparison. The conditional one is in [Alternatives to *if*](../03-conditionals/03-alternatives-to-if.md).

## Asking how long

### *How long…?*

***How long*** is the default question for duration, and it works with any tense. The word order is an ordinary question: *how long* + auxiliary + subject + verb.

> **How long** have you been waiting?  
> **How long** did you live in Austin?  
> **How long** is the flight?  
> **How long** will you be gone?  
> **How long** has she known about this?

Four relatives sit close enough to be confused with it.

| Question | Asks for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *How long…?* | the length of a span | *How long did the meeting run?* |
| *How long ago…?* | the distance back to a point | *How long ago did you move here?* |
| *How long until / till…?* | the distance forward to a point | *How long until the doors open?* |
| *How long before…?* | the same, often with impatience | *How long before somebody notices?* |
| *How often…?* | frequency | *How often do the buses run?* |
| *How many times…?* | a count of occasions | *How many times have you seen it?* |

*How long is it?* is genuinely ambiguous between time and physical length, and only the context settles it: *How long is the movie?* is time, *How long is the table?* is inches. English does not distinguish them, and neither does the answer.

### *How much time…?*

***How much time*** asks about time as a **resource** — how much of it you have, need, or have left — rather than about the length of an event. It takes a noun-phrase slot in the sentence, which *how long* does not.

> **How much time** do we have?  
> **How much time** do you need?  
> **How much time** is left on the parking meter?  
> **How much time** did you put into this?

The two overlap in exactly one frame, and there *how long* is the idiom: *How long does it take?* is what people say; *How much time does it take?* is correct, heavier, and rarer. When in doubt about an event, use *how long*; when the question is about a supply of hours, use *how much time*.

✗ *How much time is the movie?* and ✗ *How many time do you need?* are the two errors that come out of this pair. *Time* in this sense is uncountable, so it takes *much*, never *many*.

### *How long does it take?* and the *take* construction

This is the highest-value construction in the section, and it is worth building from the ground up, because English has no other way to say how much time a task costs.

**The skeleton:**

```
   It  takes   (someone)   [a length of time]   to + verb
   It  takes      me        twenty minutes       to get there.
```

Four slots, and each one has a rule.

***It*** **is a dummy subject.** It refers to nothing. You cannot leave it out: ✗ *Takes twenty minutes* is not a sentence in writing, though it is a normal clipped reply in speech.

**The person slot is a bare object pronoun — no preposition.** *It takes **me*** an hour, *it takes **us*** a week, *it took **her*** three tries. ✗ *It takes **to me*** twenty minutes and ✗ *It takes **for me*** twenty minutes are both wrong, and both come from languages that mark the experiencer with a case or a preposition. The slot is also **optional**: leave it out and the sentence becomes a general statement about the task rather than about you.

> **It takes me** twenty minutes to get downtown. — for me, specifically  
> **It takes** twenty minutes to get downtown. — for anybody

**The time slot is a length**, with all the usual modifiers: *about an hour*, *up to six weeks*, *no more than a minute*, *forever* (which is a joke, and extremely common).

**The last slot is a *to*-infinitive, never a gerund.** This is where learners lose the sentence:

> ✗ *It takes me twenty minutes **for getting** there.* → *…**to get** there.*  
> ✗ *It takes me twenty minutes **getting** there.* → *…**to get** there.*  
> ✗ *It takes me twenty minutes **that I get** there.* → *…**to get** there.*

**Every tense, in one table.** The construction inflects normally, and *it* stays *it* throughout.

| Tense | Example |
|---|---|
| Present simple | *It **takes** me twenty minutes to get there.* |
| Past simple | *It **took** us three hours to find the place.* |
| Future — *will* | *It **will take** about a week to clear.* |
| Future — *going to* | *This **is going to take** all afternoon.* |
| Present perfect | *It**'s taken** me six months to get an answer.* |
| Modal | *It **could take** up to six weeks.* &middot; *It **shouldn't take** long.* |
| Negative | *It **doesn't take** long.* &middot; *It **won't take** a minute.* |
| Question | ***How long does it take** to get downtown?* |
| Yes/no question | ***Does it take** long?* |

**The other subjects.** *It* is the default, but any noun that names a task or a journey can take its place, and then the meaning is subtly different — the sentence is about that thing rather than about the abstract cost of doing it.

> The drive **takes** about two hours.  
> The paperwork **took** a month.  
> Getting a passport **takes** six to eight weeks.  
> That recipe **takes** forever.

A **person** can be the subject too, and then the sentence says the person controlled the pace rather than that the task demanded it:

> She **took** an hour to answer my email. — she chose to sit on it  
> **It took** her an hour to answer my email. — the answering was slow  
> **Take your time.** — the imperative, meaning "don't rush"

**Two fixed idioms** ride on the same verb: *take time* (meaning "not be quick," with no length at all — *These things take time*) and *take some doing* (meaning "require effort" — *That took some doing*).

**The question form is the one to memorize whole:** ***How long does it take to*** *get downtown?* Say it enough times that the word order stops being a decision. The commonest broken version is ✗ *How long it takes to get downtown?*, which forgets that a *wh*-question needs *do*-support and inversion — the same rule that runs every question in [the present simple](../04-tenses/01-present-simple.md).

## Spending time

### The gerund rule

***Spend*** **+ a length of time + *-ing*.** No preposition, no infinitive. This is the frame, and it is fixed.

> I **spent** three hours **writing** that email.  
> She **spends** most of her weekends **fixing** up the house.  
> We **spent** a week **arguing** about the font.  
> Don't **spend** too long **thinking** about it.

> ✗ *I spent three hours **to write** that email.* → *…**writing** that email.*  
> ✗ *I spent three hours **for writing** that email.* → *…**writing** that email.*  
> ✗ *I spent three hours **on writing** that email.* → *…**writing** that email.*

With a **noun** rather than a verb, *spend* takes *on*: *She spends too much time **on** her phone. I spent all morning **on** that report.* So the rule has two branches — a verb takes the bare *-ing*, a noun takes *on* — and mixing them (✗ *spend time on writing*) is the classic half-learned version.

*Spend* also takes the money frame, identically: *I spent forty dollars on it*, *I spent an hour on it*. Time is money, grammatically speaking.

### *Waste*, *kill*, and the rest of the gerund group

Three verbs share *spend*'s frame exactly: a length of time, then an *-ing* form.

| Verb | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***spend*** | neutral — this is where the time went | *He **spent** an hour **looking** for his keys.* |
| ***waste*** | and it was not worth it | *Don't **waste** your time **arguing** with him.* |
| ***kill*** | filling an empty gap on purpose | *We **killed** an hour **wandering** around the mall.* |
| ***pass*** | filling a gap, gently; more literary | *She **passed** the afternoon **reading**.* |

> He **wasted** two years **chasing** that degree.  
> I **killed** twenty minutes **reading** the bulletin board.  
> They **spent** the whole flight **complaining** about the seats.

### The infinitive group: *make*, *find*, *take*, *have*

The other half of the vocabulary is about **acquiring** the time to do something, and every verb in it takes a ***to*-infinitive**. This split is the thing to learn: *spend* and *waste* look backward at time already gone and take *-ing*; *make* and *find* look forward at time not yet used and take *to*.

> I'll **make time to** look at it this week.  
> I never **find time to** read anymore.  
> **Take** a minute **to** read the whole thing.  
> Do you **have** time **to** talk?  
> It **takes** patience **to** teach that age group.

> ✗ *I'll make time **looking** at it.* → *…**to look** at it.*  
> ✗ *I never find time **for reading**.* — grammatical, but it means something narrower; with a verb, use *to read*.

***Save*** sits between the two groups. It takes a person and a length directly (*That **saves** me twenty minutes*), and the action that produces the saving attaches with *by* + *-ing* or a whole clause:

> Booking online **saves** you about twenty minutes.  
> You'll **save** time **by taking** the toll road.  
> It **saves** time **if** you fill out the form beforehand.

***Give*** takes two objects, like any other giving verb, and has two idiomatic uses worth knowing: *Give me five minutes* (a request for delay) and *Give it time* (an instruction to stop worrying).

> **Give me** ten minutes and I'll have it ready.  
> **Give** the medication a few days to work.  
> **Give it time.** It's only been a month.

### Hours as a countable thing: *put in*, *log*, *clock*

Working time is measured in hours, and American English has a small set of verbs for counting them. All of them take *hours* as a plain object.

| Verb | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***put in*** | everyday | *He **put in** twelve hours yesterday.* |
| ***log*** | workplace, billing, aviation | *She **logged** forty hours on the case.* |
| ***clock*** | payroll, hourly work | *I **clocked** sixty hours last week.* |
| ***rack up*** | informal, usually too many | *He's **racked up** a hundred hours of overtime.* |
| ***bill*** | professional services | *The firm **billed** eleven hours to that client.* |
| ***pull*** | informal, of a punishing shift | *She **pulled** a double.* &middot; *I **pulled** an all-nighter.* |

*Clock in* and *clock out* are the separate phrasal verbs for starting and ending a shift, and *put in* also takes years: *He **put in** thirty years at the plant.*

## Frequency: definite

### Counting occasions

**Once, twice, three times.** English has one-word forms for the first two counts and then switches to *number* + *times*.

| Count | Form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***once*** | *one time* exists but is marked — used for contrast (*just one time*) |
| 2 | ***twice*** | ***two times*** is child's English; adults say *twice* |
| 3+ | ***three times***, ***four times*** | the regular pattern from here up |
| many | *a dozen times, a hundred times, countless times* | exaggerations, all of them |

> I've seen it **twice**.  
> He called **three times** before nine.  
> I've told you that **a hundred times**.  
> **Once** is enough.

*Once* and *twice* also do non-frequency jobs — *once* meaning "at one time in the past" (*I once lived in Reno*) and "as soon as" (*Once the paperwork clears…*), and *twice* meaning "double" (*twice the price*, *twice as long*). Only the count reading belongs to this section.

### Per period: *a*, *per*, and the *-ly* adverbs

Three ways to say "in each period," differing in register rather than in meaning.

| Frame | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|
| count + ***a*** / ***an*** + period | the everyday form | *twice **a** week* &middot; *three times **a** year* &middot; *once **an** hour* |
| count + ***per*** + period | technical, contractual, scientific | *forty hours **per** week* &middot; *two doses **per** day* |
| the ***-ly*** adverb | compact; also an adjective | *The report runs **weekly**.* &middot; *a **weekly** report* |

The *a* here is not the indefinite article doing its usual job; it is an old preposition meaning "each," which is why it survives in *sixty miles an hour* and *five dollars a pound*. ✗ *twice in a week* and ✗ *twice on week* are the two ways it goes wrong.

> The bus runs every twenty minutes, so that's three **an hour**.  
> Rent is due once **a** month.  
> The committee meets **quarterly**.

**The *-ly* series**, with the caution flags marked. Every one of them is both an adverb and an adjective with no change of form — *The report runs **weekly*** and *a **weekly** report* — which is unusual in English and worth noticing.

| Adverb | Means | Safe? |
|---|---|---|
| *hourly* | once an hour | yes — also *an hourly rate*, meaning "by the hour" |
| *daily*, *nightly* | once a day, once a night | yes |
| *weekly* | once a week | yes |
| *biweekly* | **ambiguous** | **no** — see below |
| *monthly* | once a month | yes |
| *bimonthly* | **ambiguous** | **no** — see below |
| *semimonthly* | twice a month | unambiguous, and standard in payroll |
| *quarterly* | once every three months | yes |
| *semiannually* | twice a year | yes |
| *biannually* | **ambiguous** | **no** — usually twice a year, sometimes every two |
| *biennially* | every two years | yes, and constantly confused with *biannually* |
| *annually*, *yearly* | once a year | yes |

### *Biweekly* and *bimonthly*: say something else

Here is the honest picture, and it is not a rule you can learn your way out of. **In American usage *biweekly* means both "every two weeks" and "twice a week," and *bimonthly* means both "every two months" and "twice a month."** American dictionaries record both senses for both words, because both are genuinely in use. There is no correct answer; there is a coin flip and a reader who guesses wrong.

The one place the ambiguity has partly resolved is **payroll**, where the terms are treated as technical: *biweekly* means every two weeks, which is 26 paychecks a year, and *semimonthly* means twice a month, which is 24. Outside payroll, nothing has resolved.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| *biweekly* | **every two weeks**, or **twice a week** |
| *bimonthly* | **every two months**, or **twice a month** |
| *biannual* | **twice a year**, or **every two years** |

American English also has no ordinary word for a two-week period — *fortnight* is British and sounds like a costume drama to an American ear — which is exactly the gap that pushed *biweekly* into doing two jobs. *Every two weeks* is four syllables longer, and worth every one of them.

### *Every*: the interval frames

*Every* + a period is the most flexible frequency frame in English, and it reaches ground the *-ly* adverbs cannot.

| Frame | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *every* + singular period | once in each | *every day*, *every Tuesday*, *every summer* |
| *every* + number + plural | at that interval | *every three days*, *every six months* |
| *every other* | alternating | *every other Friday*, *every other row* |
| *every few* | short, irregular intervals | *every few minutes*, *every few weeks* |
| *every single* | emphatic — no exceptions | *every single day* |
| *every so often* | **not an interval at all** — occasionally | *Check it every so often.* |

> The medication is one tablet **every four hours**.  
> We get the gutters cleaned **every other spring**.  
> She has called me **every single day** this week.

***Every other*** is the trap for Russian speakers, and it gets its own line below; ***every so often***, despite the shape, names no interval and belongs with the indefinite adverbs. All of these are phrases, so they sit at the **end** of the clause, or at the front for emphasis — never in the middle: ✗ *I **every morning** check my email* → *I check my email **every morning**.*

## Frequency: indefinite

### The scale

The percentages are approximate by definition — that is what *indefinite* means — but they are the shared intuition, and they are close enough to choose by.

| Roughly | One-word adverbs | Phrases at the same strength |
|---|---|---|
| 100% | **always** | *without fail*, *every single time*, *invariably* |
| ~95% | **almost always**, **nearly always** | *nine times out of ten*, *as a rule* |
| ~80% | **usually**, **normally**, **generally**, **typically**, **ordinarily** | *most of the time*, *more often than not*, *by and large* |
| ~70% | **often**, **frequently**, **regularly** | *a lot*, *time and again* |
| ~50% | **sometimes** | *at times*, *now and then*, *half the time*, *as often as not* |
| ~30% | **occasionally**, **periodically**, **sporadically**, **intermittently** | *from time to time*, *every so often*, *once in a while*, *on and off* |
| ~10% | **rarely**, **seldom**, **infrequently** | *not often*, *not much* |
| ~5% | **hardly ever**, **scarcely ever**, **almost never** | *once in a blue moon*, *next to never* |
| 0% | **never** | *not once*, *never in my life*, *not in a million years* |

Four of the phrases carry more than a percentage. ***On and off*** (and ***off and on***, identical) means intermittently across a long stretch, and implies the thing kept restarting: *They dated on and off for six years.* ***Once in a blue moon*** is an exaggeration, not a measurement. ***As often as not*** means about half, though plenty of Americans use it loosely for "frequently," which makes it a poor choice in writing. ***More often than not*** is the safe one: it means more than half, and nobody misreads it.

### Where they go

The one-word adverbs take **mid-position**, and the rule has three parts:

1. **Before the main verb.** *She **always** takes the stairs. He **never** calls first.*
2. **After *be*.** *He **is always** late. They **are never** on time.*
3. **After the first auxiliary.** *I **don't usually** eat breakfast. She **has never** been to Texas. **Do** you **ever** go back?*

> ✗ *I take **always** the bus.* → *I **always take** the bus.*  
> ✗ *He **always is** late.* → *He **is always** late.*

The **phrases** go at the end, or at the front for emphasis — the same split that runs through definite frequency, and through [the present simple](../04-tenses/01-present-simple.md), where the position rule is set out in full.

> It works, **most of the time**.  
> **Nine times out of ten**, it's the router.  
> **Every once in a while** she'll send a postcard.

**Fronting a one-word adverb** is allowed for *sometimes*, *usually*, *normally*, *often*, *occasionally*, *frequently* and *generally* (*Sometimes I walk to work*), and forbidden for *always*, *never*, *rarely* and *seldom* in a plain statement — front one of those four and the main clause **inverts**, which is literary. **End position** is available for *sometimes*, *often*, *occasionally* and all the phrases, and for nothing else on the scale.

> **Rarely does he** call first.  
> **Never have I seen** anything like it.

### Negative frequency, and *ever*

***Never***, ***rarely***, ***seldom***, and ***hardly ever*** are already negative. They take no *do*-support negation of their own:

> ✗ *She **doesn't never** call.* → *She **never calls**.* / *She **doesn't ever** call.*  
> ✗ *He **doesn't rarely** answer.* → *He **rarely answers**.*

***Ever*** is the negative-and-question partner of *always*, and it almost never appears in a plain affirmative. Its homes are questions (*Have you **ever** been to Alaska?*), negatives (*I don't **ever** want to do that again*), comparisons (*the best I've **ever** had*), and *if*-clauses (*If you're **ever** in Denver…*). ✗ *I ever go there* is not English; the affirmative word is *always* or *sometimes*.

And ***not always*** is not ***always not***. *He's **not always** right* means he is sometimes wrong; ✗ *He's always not right* is not a sentence anyone says. The word for the 0% end is *never*.

## Repetition and continuation

### Doing it again

| Expression | Sense | Position |
|---|---|---|
| *again* &middot; *once again* &middot; *yet again* | one more time, with exasperation at *yet* | end, or front |
| *again and again* &middot; *over and over (again)* | many times, wearingly | end |
| *time after time* &middot; *time and (time) again* | the same, more rhetorical | end or front |
| *repeatedly* | many times, neutral and written | mid or end |
| *re-* + verb | do it over — *reread*, *redo*, *rewrite*, *resend* | prefix |

> Say that **again**.  
> **Yet again**, the flight was delayed.  
> He asked me the same question **over and over**.  
> **Time and again** she covered for him, and we warned her **repeatedly**.

The *re-* prefix is usually closed up (*reread*, *reopen*, *resend*) and takes a hyphen only to prevent a misreading — *re-sign* a contract is not *resign* from a job, and *re-cover* a chair is not *recover* from the flu.

### *Keep (on) doing*

***Keep*** **+ *-ing*** is the everyday way to say something will not stop. It takes a gerund and only a gerund: ✗ *keep to do* is one of the most persistent errors in the language. *Keep on* + *-ing* is the emphatic version, and it usually carries annoyance.

> He **keeps calling**.  
> The car **keeps making** that noise.  
> She **kept on talking** through the whole movie.  
> **Keep going** — you're almost there.

*Go on*, *carry on*, and *continue* are the near neighbors. *Go on* and *carry on* take *-ing*; *continue* takes either *-ing* or *to* + infinitive, with no difference in meaning — one of the very few verbs in English where the choice is genuinely free.

Do not confuse this *keep* with *keep* + object + adjective (*Keep the door closed*) or *keep* + object + from + *-ing* (*That kept me from sleeping*). Same verb, different frames, unrelated meanings.

### *Still*, *yet*, *no longer*, *anymore*

Four words that say whether a situation is running, not started, or finished.

| Word | Says | Position | Polarity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***still*** | it is going on, later than expected | mid | affirmative, and negative for impatience |
| ***yet*** | it has not started, and is expected | end | negatives and questions |
| ***no longer*** | it has stopped | mid | affirmative in form, negative in sense |
| ***anymore*** | it has stopped | end | needs a negative or a question |

> She's **still** at the office. &middot; He **still** hasn't called — *still* before the negative marks impatience.  
> Has the mail come **yet**? — *Did the mail come yet?* is equally American.  
> He **no longer** works here. &middot; He doesn't work here **anymore**. — the formal version and the everyday one.

Two American notes. **Spelling:** *anymore* is one word in American English, where British writing keeps *any more*, and it is distinct from *any more* meaning an additional quantity — *I don't want **any more** coffee* is two words and a different sentence. **Positive *anymore*:** in parts of the Midland and the lower Midwest you will hear *Gas is so expensive anymore*, with *anymore* meaning "nowadays" and no negative anywhere. It is a real regional feature, not an error in those dialects, and not standard written English.

These four, along with *already* and *just*, are the words Russian collapses into *уже* and *ещё*, and they are worked out in full in the next section.

## Speed and pace

The same grammatical slot — an adverbial of time — also carries how *fast* something happens. These are not durations, but they answer questions about time and they cluster with everything above.

| Group | Members | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **No delay at all** | *right away*, *right now*, *at once*, *immediately*, *this minute*, *first thing*, *ASAP* | *Send it **right away**.* |
| **Very fast** | *in no time*, *in a flash*, *in seconds*, *before you know it* | *We'll have you out of here **in no time**.* |
| **Soon** | *soon*, *shortly*, *before long*, *any minute now*, *momentarily*, *in a bit*, *in a minute* | *We'll be boarding **shortly**.* |
| **After a gap** | *in a while*, *after a while*, *later on*, *down the road* | *Try again **in a while**.* |
| **Unspecified later** | *sooner or later*, *eventually*, *in the end*, *ultimately*, *in due course* | *He'll find out **sooner or later**.* |

Three honest notes. ***At once*** has two unrelated senses — "immediately" (*Come at once*) and "at the same time" (*Don't all talk at once*) — and only the context separates them. ***Momentarily*** is genuinely split in American usage: the older sense is "for a moment" (*the power flickered momentarily*), the everyday airplane sense is "in a moment" (*We'll be landing momentarily*), and both are current, so write *in a moment* or *briefly* when the difference matters. ***In a minute*** is almost never a minute, neither is *in a second*, and *in due course* usually means "not soon."

***In*** **+ a period versus *within* + a period** is the distinction worth being exact about. *In two weeks* points at a moment two weeks from now; *within two weeks* covers the whole span up to it.

> You'll hear back **in** two weeks. — around the two-week mark  
> You'll hear back **within** two weeks. — any time between now and then

And *in* has a second, backward-looking sense with a finished action, meaning elapsed time: *She read it **in** an hour*. The tense tells you which sense is running — a future verb gives the "from now" reading, a past verb the "elapsed" reading. That ambiguity is the first Russian note below.

## For Russian speakers

**1. *За*, *в течение*, and *через* all land on English words that are not each other.** This is the largest single source of duration errors, because Russian marks the difference with aspect and a preposition where English marks it with a preposition and a tense.

| Russian | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| «Я сделал это **за** два часа» | *I did it **in** two hours.* | elapsed time to complete — *in*, with a past verb |
| «Я делал это **в течение** двух часов» | *I did it **for** two hours.* | a length I spent on it — *for*, never *during* |
| «Я приду **через** два часа» | *I'll be there **in** two hours.* | measured forward from now — *in*, with a future verb |
| «**В течение** встречи» | ***During** the meeting* | a named period — *during* takes the noun |
| «**В течение** двух часов встречи» | ***During the** two hours **of** the meeting* | definite noun phrase, so *during* is fine |

Note what happens to *in*: it translates both «за» and «через», and English tells them apart by the tense of the verb rather than by a second preposition. *I finished it in an hour* is «за», *I'll finish it in an hour* is «через».

**2. ✗ *during two hours* comes directly from «в течение двух часов».** The Russian phrase is built on a noun («течение»), so it takes a genitive length and sounds like it should map to *during*. It does not. A bare length takes *for*: ✗ *We waited during two hours* → *We waited **for** two hours*.

**3. The Russian instrumental of duration has no English case to land in.** «Часами», «днями», «неделями», «годами», «целыми днями» all become prepositional phrases, and the emphatic ones become idioms:

| Russian | English |
|---|---|
| «Он читал **часами**» | *He read **for hours**.* / *He read **for hours on end**.* |
| «Мы ждали **днями**» | *We waited **for days**.* |
| «Она **целыми днями** сидит дома» | *She sits at home **all day long**.* |
| «Я не видел его **годами**» | *I haven't seen him **in years**.* |

The last row is the useful one: after a negative, American English prefers *in years* to *for years* — *I haven't seen him **in** years*, *We haven't spoken **in** months*.

**4. «Раз в неделю» → *once a week*, with no preposition.** The Russian «в» has no English counterpart here; the *a* already means "per."

> ✗ *once **in a** week* → *once **a** week*  
> ✗ *two times **in a** month* → ***twice a** month*  
> ✗ *three times **on** year* → *three times **a** year*

**5. «Через день» means *every other day*, not "in a day."** This is a genuine false friend, because «через» elsewhere really is *in*. «Через день» = *every other day*; «через два дня» = *in two days* if you are counting forward, *every two days* if you are naming an interval. Only the context separates them in Russian too, and English forces you to choose.

**6. *Spend time* takes a gerund, and Russian gives you no reason to expect it.** «Я провёл три часа, **читая**» has a participle; «я потратил время **на** чтение» has a preposition plus a verbal noun. English has neither: ✗ *I spent three hours **to read*** and ✗ *I spent three hours **on reading*** both fail, and the answer is *I spent three hours **reading***.

**7. «Уже три года» takes the present perfect, not the present.** Russian says «Я живу здесь уже три года» with a present-tense verb, because the state is going on now. English measures the span from its beginning and therefore needs a perfect: *I**'ve lived** here for three years.* ✗ *I live here three years* and ✗ *I am here since Monday* are the two shapes this comes out in. The tense side of it is in [the present perfect](../04-tenses/03-present-perfect.md).

**8. «Сколько времени это займёт?» → *How long will it take?*** Two things go wrong at once. Russian needs no *do* and no inversion, so ✗ *How long it will take?* is the usual output; and «сколько времени» invites the heavier *how much time*, where the idiom is *how long*.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I've been here **since** three hours.* | *I've been here **for** three hours.* | *Three hours* is a length, so it takes *for*. |
| ✗ *We waited **during** two hours.* | *We waited **for** two hours.* | *During* takes a named period, never a bare length. |
| ✗ *I **work** here **since** 2018.* | *I**'ve worked** here **since** 2018.* | *Since* forces a perfect tense. |
| ✗ *During he was speaking, the lights went out.* | ***While** he was speaking, the lights went out.* | *During* never takes a clause. |
| ✗ *It takes **to me** twenty minutes.* | *It takes **me** twenty minutes.* | The person slot is a bare object — no preposition. |
| ✗ *It takes me an hour **for getting** there.* | *It takes me an hour **to get** there.* | The last slot is a *to*-infinitive. |
| ✗ *How long it takes to get there?* | *How long **does it take** to get there?* | A *wh*-question needs *do* and inversion. |
| ✗ *I spent two hours **to write** it.* | *I spent two hours **writing** it.* | *Spend*, *waste*, *kill* and *pass* take *-ing*. |
| ✗ *I'll make time **looking** at it.* | *I'll make time **to look** at it.* | *Make*, *find*, *take* and *have* take the infinitive. |
| ✗ *How much time is the movie?* | *How long is the movie?* | *How much time* asks about a supply, not an event. |
| ✗ *once **in a** week* | *once **a** week* | The *a* already means "per." |
| ✗ *I go **twice a week** to the gym.* | *I go to the gym **twice a week**.* | Frequency phrases go at the end. |
| ✗ *I take **always** the bus.* | *I **always take** the bus.* | One-word frequency adverbs take mid-position. |
| ✗ *He **always is** late.* | *He **is always** late.* | After *be*, the adverb follows the verb. |
| ✗ *She **doesn't never** call.* | *She **never calls**.* | *Never* is already negative. |
| ✗ *He **keeps to call**.* | *He **keeps calling**.* | *Keep* takes a gerund only. |
| ✗ *He doesn't work here **no more**.* | *He doesn't work here **anymore**.* | Standard English does not double the negative. |
| ✗ *Let's meet **biweekly**.* | *Let's meet **every two weeks**.* | *Biweekly* is genuinely ambiguous in American usage. |

## Quick reference

**The three duration slots, and the two verb patterns.**

```
   a LENGTH   ──►  for      for two hours, for six years, for ages
   a POINT    ──►  since    since noon, since 2018, since she left
   a PERIOD   ──►  during   during the meeting, during the summer

   time gone       spend / waste / kill / pass  + TIME + V-ing
   time to find    make / find / take / have    + TIME + to + V
   the cost        It takes (me) [length] to + verb
```

**The question to memorize whole:** *How long does it take to get downtown?* — *About twenty minutes.*

**Position, in one line.** One-word frequency adverbs go in the **middle** — before the main verb, after *be*, after the first auxiliary. Everything else, every phrase of duration, frequency or pace, goes at the **end**, or at the front for emphasis.

**Four things to write instead of a word that is ambiguous:** *every two weeks* for *biweekly*, *every two months* for *bimonthly*, *twice a year* for *biannual*, *in a moment* for *momentarily*.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **for** forty minutes | a length |
| 2 | **since** she graduated | a past-simple clause naming a starting point |
| 3 | I waited **for** two hours. | *During* cannot take a bare length. |
| 4 | It takes **me** half an hour to get to work. | The person slot takes no preposition. |
| 5 | **How long does it take** to get a passport? | *Wh*-question: *do* plus inversion. |
| 6 | I spent the whole weekend **painting** the kitchen. | *Spend* + time + *-ing*. |
| 7 | I never find time **to read** anymore. | *Find time* takes the infinitive. |
| 8 | He goes to the gym **three times a week**. | Frequency phrases go at the end, not in mid-position. |
| 9 | He **keeps asking** me the same question. | *Keep* takes a gerund only. |
| 10 | Either **every two weeks** or **twice a week** — both are current American usage, so ask, and write the unambiguous phrase yourself. | *Biweekly* has two live senses. |
| 11 | *I**'ve lived** here **for** five years.* — present perfect. | Russian uses the present; English measures the span from its start and needs a perfect. |
