Common mistakes with time#

Every time-related error a Russian speaker actually makes — the wrong sentence, the repair, and the reason the mistake was the natural one to make.

← Time · All grammar topics

Time errors are the most persistent errors in a Russian speaker's English, for a reason that has nothing to do with difficulty. Nobody misunderstands ✗ I live here two years or ✗ on next Monday. The meaning lands, the conversation continues, and the error is reinforced by its own success. A learner can spend fifteen years in the United States saying ✗ I'll call you through an hour and never once be corrected, because correcting it would be rude and unnecessary.

Almost everything below comes from one of five sources.

  1. Russian requires a preposition where English forbids one. На прошлой неделе, в следующий понедельник, в этом году — every one carries a preposition, and every one loses it in English. §1.1 is entirely this.
  2. One Russian word covers two English ones. До is until and by; последний is last and latest; часы is watch and clock; вовремя is on time and in time. Each split has to be learned as a split, because no amount of care with the Russian word will reveal it.
  3. Russian counts the hour forward; English counts it backward. Полседьмого is 6:30 because Russian names half of the seventh hour. English names the hour that has already finished. §2 follows from that one fact.
  4. Russian uses the present tense for a stretch reaching the present. Я живу здесь два года is present in Russian, and no English present tense can do that job. §6 is this error and its consequences.
  5. English marks the future once per sentence. The rule that governs conditionals governs every time clause. §10 sends you there rather than repeating it.

1. Preposition errors#

1.1 The zero-preposition rule#

The most frequent time error in the language, and the easiest to fix permanently.

✗ I saw her in last week. → ✓ I saw her last week.

Why it happens. Russian marks these phrases with a preposition and a case ending — на прошлой неделе, в следующем месяце, в этом году — and the learner supplies an English equivalent out of habit. English treats them as adverbs already: they are not prepositional phrases at all.

The rule. No preposition before a time expression beginning with last, next, this, that, every, each, all, some — or before yesterday, today, tomorrow, tonight.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
We met in last week.We met last week.
I'll see you on next Monday.I'll see you next Monday.
I'm busy on this week.I'm busy this week.
He runs in every morning.He runs every morning.

The honest exception. In the last week — with the — is correct, and means during the seven days just past: I've called him three times in the last week, against I called him last week (the calendar week before this one). The article does the work: without the, the phrase is an adverb; with the, last is an ordinary adjective inside a real noun phrase and the preposition returns. Same for on the last day of the month, in the next few hours, at this time of year.

1.2 In, on, and at#

✗ The meeting is in Monday. → ✓ …on Monday. ✗ I work best at the morning. → ✓ …in the morning.

Why it happens. Russian uses в for nearly all of these — в понедельник, в марте, в 2019 году, в три часа — so there is no distinction to transfer, only one preposition that has to be split three ways. English divides by size of container.

PrepositionTakesExamples
atclock times, noon, midnight, night, mealtimesat 6:15, at noon, at night, at lunch
ondays, dates, holidays, a day plus a part of iton Monday, on March 3, on Friday night
inmonths, seasons, years, decades, parts of the dayin March, in the summer, in 2019, in the morning
✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
in Mondayon Mondaydays take on
on July · at 2019in July · in 2019months and years take in
at the morningin the morningparts of the day take in — except night
in Friday eveningon Friday eveninga day plus a part of it stays on

Two American notes. Americans say on the weekend, not the British at the weekend. And in speech they routinely drop on before a day: I'll see you Tuesday. That is standard spoken American English — but not a license to write ✗ The report is due Friday in a contract, where by Friday is wanted.

1.3 The in an hour trap#

✗ I'll call you through an hour. → ✓ I'll call you in an hour.

Why it happens. Через has a spatial meaning — через мост, across the bridge — and the dictionary offers through. Applied to time it produces a phrase that means nothing in English. The English word for "a stretch of time from now" is in.

EnglishMeansExample
in an houran hour from now, counted from the moment of speakingThe bus leaves in twenty minutes.
within an hourany time before an hour is upYou'll get a reply within an hour.
after an hourfollowing an hour already passed — a past narrativeAfter an hour, he gave up.
✗ Wrong✓ Right
He'll be back through ten minutes.He'll be back in ten minutes.
I'll text you after five minutes. (from now)I'll text you in five minutes.

In has a second job, and it is a real ambiguity: in an hour also means taking one hour. A future verb means "from now"; a past verb means "it took that long." And через means every other as well: через день is every other day, not ✗ through a day.

1.4 Until and by#

Russian до covers both, and this is the split that costs learners actual deadlines.

✗ I'll finish the report until Friday. → ✓ …by Friday. ✗ Please stay by six. → ✓ Please stay until six.

Why it happens. До пятницы is one phrase for two ideas. Until describes something that continues up to a point and then stops; by describes something that happens once, at or before a deadline.

The test: is the verb a stretch or a moment? Wait, stay, work, sleep, be open, last are stretches and take until. Finish, send, arrive, pay, decide, call are moments and take by.

✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
Send it until Monday.Send it by Monday.one act, with a deadline
The store is open by nine.The store is open until nine.a stretch ending at nine
I have to decide until tomorrow.I have to decide by tomorrow.a single decision

Three extras. Not … until is the ordinary way to say "not before": The results won't be out until Thursday. Writing ✗ won't be out by Thursday says the opposite. By the time takes a clause, by takes a noun: ✗ By I get there → By the time I get there — and that clause takes a present form for future time (§10). And до is also before when the point is order rather than duration: Let's talk before lunch.

1.5 For, during, and while#

✗ We waited during three hours. → ✓ We waited for three hours.

Why it happens. Во время and в течение both come back from the dictionary as during, so a measurement gets attached to it. English sorts these by what follows the word, not by meaning.

WordFollowed byExample
fora length — a number, a measurementfor three hours, for a long time
duringa named period — an event or a named stretchduring the meeting, during the war
whilea clause — subject and verbwhile I was waiting
✗ Wrong✓ Right
He slept for the whole movie.He slept during the whole movie.
During I was cooking, the phone rang.While I was cooking, the phone rang.

1.6 Since and for#

✗ I've worked here since three years. ✓ …for three years. / …since 2023.

Why it happens. Russian marks both the same way — уже три года, с 2023 года — and the difference is not felt as one. English is strict: since takes a starting point, for takes a length.

Since + a pointFor + a length
since Monday, since 2019, since I moved here, since thenfor two days, for a month, for a while, for ages
✗ Wrong✓ Right
since two weeksfor two weeks · since two weeks ago
since a long timefor a long time

Ago belongs here. Three years ago is a point counted backward from now, and it goes with the past simple: ✗ I have seen him three years ago → I saw him three years ago. It is never ✗ before three years, a literal rendering of три года назад through до. And since forces a perfect tense — that half of the error gets §6.2.

2. Telling the time#

Everything here traces to one structural fact: Russian names the hour that is coming; English names the hour that has passed.

2.1 The half-hour inversion#

✗ Let's meet at half seven. — said by a Russian speaker meaning 6:30

Полседьмого is half of the seventh hour — the seventh hour is under way and half of it is gone, so the time is 6:30. English works from the other end: half past six is half an hour past six. The two languages agree on the clock reading and disagree completely on which number to say.

The danger is the British colloquial half seven, which means 7:30. A Russian speaker who picks it up and reads it through the Russian pattern will be exactly one hour off, permanently.

ClockRussian✓ American English✗ The error
6:30полседьмогоsix thirty · half past sixhalf seven (= 7:30)
7:30полвосьмогоseven thirty · half past sevenhalf eight (= 8:30)

Americans rarely say half past at all — six thirty is the default. And half seven is not American English in any meaning. Say the digits and the problem disappears.

2.2 O'clock, a.m., and p.m.#

✗ The flight leaves at five o'clock p.m. ✓ …at five p.m. / at five o'clock in the afternoon

O'clock is short for of the clock, and it exists to say "this number is a clock time." Once p.m. is there, that job is done, and the two cannot stack.

The rules for o'clock: only with a whole hour (✗ five thirty o'clock), only with one to twelve (✗ seventeen o'clock), never with a.m. or p.m., never with a digit-and-colon time (✗ at 5:00 o'clock), and optional everywhere — I'll be there at five is complete.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
at 17 o'clockat five p.m.
at eight o'clock amat eight a.m. · at eight in the morning

The 24-hour clock is not spoken in American civilian life. Written 17:00 is read aloud as five p.m., and outside the military, transit, and medical charts it is not written either. ✗ The meeting is at 15:00 reads as foreign in an American office email.

Format. American edited prose writes a.m. and p.m. lowercase with periods; informal writing uses AM / PM. Do not write ✗ 8 p.m. in the evening — the marker already says it. And 12 p.m. is noon while 12 a.m. is midnight, which half of all readers get backward: in anything that matters, write noon and midnight.

2.3 Past, to, and the shape of the phrase#

✗ at half past of six → ✓ at half past six

Russian builds these with a genitive — половина седьмого, четверть седьмого — and the genitive comes out as of. English uses no preposition between past and the hour.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
half past of sixhalf past six
quarter to the sevena quarter to seven
it is half pastit's six thirty
The shape
[minutes] + past / after / to / of + [hour]. Past and to are the neutral pair; after and of are American regional variants — ten after six (6:10), ten of seven (6:50). Minutes is optional with multiples of five (ten past six) and required otherwise (twenty-three minutes past four).

The digital reading is always safe. Six fifteen, six thirty, six fifty-five. In the first ten minutes of an hour, say oh: six oh five, not ✗ six zero five.

2.4 Counting toward the next hour#

The half-hour is only the most famous case. Russian counts every minute of the first half-hour toward the hour that is coming, and every literal translation produces a real English time that is wrong by thirty to fifty minutes.

ClockRussian, literally✗ The literal English✓ English
6:05пять минут седьмогоfive of seven (= 6:55)five past six · six oh five
6:20двадцать минут седьмогоtwenty of seven (= 6:40)twenty past six · six twenty
6:30полседьмогоhalf seven (= 7:30)six thirty

After the half-hour the systems agree, because Russian switches to без and English switches to to: без двадцати семь is twenty to seven, без четверти семь is a quarter to seven, без десяти семь is ten to seven.

The safe habit: say the digits. Six twenty cannot be misread by anyone. Every form, with the American preferences marked, is in Telling the time.

3. Date errors#

3.1 The American order#

✗ The contract starts on 04/03/2026. — written by a Russian speaker meaning April 3. An American reads March 4.

Russian and most of Europe write day/month/year; the United States writes month/day/year. The two are indistinguishable for every day from the 1st to the 12th.

WrittenAmerican readingRussian reading
3/4/26March 43 April
25/12/26unreadable — there is no month 2525 December

The rule that prevents all of it: spell the month. March 4, 2026 is unambiguous to every reader on earth. The ISO form 2026-03-04 is also unambiguous and standard in file names and technical writing, but it is not used in American prose.

3.2 Capital letters#

✗ I'll see you on monday in march. → ✓ …on Monday in March.

Russian writes понедельник and март in lowercase, and there is no reason for a learner to suspect otherwise.

CapitalizedNot capitalized
Monday, Tuesday, Wednesdaytoday, tomorrow, yesterday, tonight
January, February, Marchspring, summer, fall, winter
Thanksgiving, Christmas, the Fourth of Julymorning, afternoon, evening, night

The seasons catch nearly everyone, because they feel like the same kind of word as the months. They are not: ✗ I'm going in Summer → in the summer, unless the season is part of a name (Summer 2026 internship).

3.3 Writing and saying the date#

✗ We arrived on the 3 March. → ✓ …on March 3, said March third

Russian says третьего марта — ordinal, then month, no article and no preposition. The learner keeps the Russian order, supplies an article from somewhere, and drops the ordinal ending.

✗ Wrong✓ American written✓ Said aloud
the 3 March · 3 of MarchMarch 3March third · the third of March
March 3thMarch 3rd · March 3March third
on 3 March 2026on March 3, 2026March third, twenty twenty-six
on March, 2026in March 2026no comma without a day

The details that get marked in editing. Ordinal suffixes run 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, with all the teens in -th. In American prose the suffix is omitted in writing and pronounced in speech. A full date inside a sentence takes a comma after the year as well as before it: March 3, 2026, was a Tuesday. The 3rd of March is correct, more formal, and more British. And days and dates take on (§1.2): ✗ in Monday, ✗ at March 3.

3.4 Years, decades, and centuries#

✗ She was born in one thousand nine hundred eighty-five. ✓ …in nineteen eighty-five.

Russian reads a year as a single ordinal — в тысяча девятьсот восемьдесят пятом году — and the learner translates the whole machine. English splits the year into two two-digit numbers.

Year✓ SaidYear✓ Said
1985nineteen eighty-five2000two thousand
1900nineteen hundred2005two thousand five
1905nineteen oh five2026twenty twenty-six

The years 2000–2009 broke the pattern: nobody says ✗ twenty oh five. From 2010 onward the two-part reading came back, and twenty twenty-six is the ordinary American form.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
in 90th yearsin the '90s · in the nineties · in the 1990s
in the 1990'sin the 1990s — the apostrophe is possessive, not plural

The apostrophe in '90s replaces the missing 19, so it goes in front. Centuries transfer cleanly from Russian — the twentieth century is 1900–1999 — but the hyphen does not: a twentieth-century novel before a noun, written in the twentieth century after.

4. Already, yet, still, and anymore#

Russian has two words here — уже and ещё — and English has four, distributed along different lines. The result is the densest cluster of time errors in the language.

RussianCoversEnglishMeansSentence type
ужеalready, yet, anymorealreadyit happened, sooner than expectedaffirmative; surprised questions
ещёstill, yet, more, elseyetit hasn't happened, and is expectednegatives; neutral questions
уже неnot anymore, no longerstillit hasn't stoppedaffirmative and negative
ещё неnot yetanymore / no longerit has stoppednegatives (anymore)

The repair table.

✗ Wrong✓ RightWhat went wrong
I haven't finished already.I haven't finished yet.negatives take yet
Have you finished still?Have you finished yet?a neutral question takes yet
He already is not working here.He doesn't work here anymore.уже не is not anymore
I still don't work here.I don't work here anymore.still means it continues; this stopped
She isn't still here.She still isn't here.still goes in front of the negative
He is not here already.He isn't here yet. (not arrived) · …anymore. (left)уже не forces the choice

The four-way test. Ask what happened to the situation. It started early → already. It hasn't started and is expected → yet. It continues → still. It stopped → anymore / no longer.

Spelling. American English writes the time adverb as one word — anymore — and the quantity phrase as two: I don't want any more coffee. Positive anymore (Gas is expensive anymore) is regional; do not imitate it.

Already in questions carries surprise. Have you finished yet? is neutral; Have you finished already? means you are impressed or suspicious. Both are correct; only one is neutral. And just belongs to this family: I've just eaten and I just ate mean the same thing, and the past simple is the ordinary American form. British teaching marks it wrong; American usage does not.

5. Fixed phrases that differ by one preposition#

5.1 On time and in time#

✗ The train was in time. → ✓ The train was on time.

Вовремя is one word covering both. English splits it along a line Russian does not draw: on time is punctual — at the scheduled moment — and in time is early enough, with a margin, before it was too late. On time answers was it punctual?; in time answers was it soon enough? and usually carries for or to.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
He is never in time for meetings.He is never on time for meetings.
The ambulance arrived on time to save him.The ambulance arrived in time to save him.
We finished on time for the deadline.We finished in time for the deadline.

5.2 At the end and in the end#

✗ At the end, we decided not to go. → ✓ In the end, we decided not to go.

At the end (of X) marks the final point of a specific thing and always needs, or implies, of something. In the end means finally, after everything, and is a whole-sentence adverb that stands alone.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
In the end of the movie, she leaves.At the end of the movie, she leaves.
At the end, they got married.In the end, they got married.

The same shape governs the openings: at the beginning of something, against at first standing alone. ✗ In the beginning of July → at the beginning of July. In the beginning alone exists but is biblical and grand.

5.3 At last, finally, and lastly#

Four words compete for наконец and в конце концов, and they are not interchangeable.

WordMeansCarries
at lastit happened after a long, frustrating waitrelief
finallyafter a long time; or the last item in a sequenceneutral
lastlythe last item in a listorganizational only
eventuallyafter a delay, sooner or laterpatience, not relief
✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
At last, I'd like to thank the committee.Lastly, I'd like to thank the committee.nothing frustrating happened; it is list item four
At the end, he agreed.In the end, he agreed.at the end needs an of
Eventually I'll come tomorrow.Maybe I'll come tomorrow.eventually is not возможно — §9.5

6. Duration and the perfect#

6.1 The missing perfect#

✗ I live here two years. ✓ I have lived here for two years. / I've been living here for two years.

Why it happens. Russian uses the present tense for a stretch that began in the past and is still going: Я живу здесь два года. The verb is present because the living is present. English reasons the other way: the stretch started in the past, so the sentence needs a form that reaches back, and no present tense can do it.

This is the most common error in a fluent Russian speaker's English, and it survives everything.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
I work here since 2019.I've worked here since 2019.
We are married for ten years.We've been married for ten years.
How long do you know him?How long have you known him?
She studies English for three years.She's been studying English for three years.
The rule
if the stretch reaches the present, use a present perfect. If it is over, use the past simple — and that contrast is the payoff:

I've lived in Boston for ten years. — I live there now. · I lived in Boston for ten years. — I don't anymore.

Both are correct; they are about different people's lives. Present perfect works through the whole system.

6.2 The since sentence that is missing a perfect#

✗ I am waiting since an hour. → ✓ I've been waiting for an hour.

That sentence carries two errors, and learners usually fix only the visible one. Since takes a starting point, not a length (§1.6), so an hour becomes for an hour. And since is incompatible with a present continuous — it demands a perfect.

✗ Wrong✓ RightErrors
I am here since Monday.I've been here since Monday.tense
I am waiting since two hours.I've been waiting for two hours.tense + since/for

Continuous or simple? With live, work, study, wait, either is natural and the continuous stresses the activity. With stative verbs — know, be, have, own, believe — only the simple perfect is possible: ✗ I've been knowing him for years → I've known him for years.

6.3 The How long frame#

✗ How long time are you in Boston? → ✓ How long have you been in Boston?

Сколько времени is literally "how much time," so the learner builds ✗ How long time…? English has one fixed question word for duration — how long — and it never takes time after it.

✗ Wrong✓ RightAsking about
How long time do you live here?How long have you lived here?a stretch reaching now
How much time it takes?How long does it take?duration of a process
How long ago you came?How long ago did you come?distance back to a point
How many time have you been there?How many times have you been there?frequency — §9.1

The answers have shapes too. How long have you been here? takes For two years, Since 2019, or bare Two years — not ✗ From two years, and not Two years ago, which answers a different question. More frames are in Duration and frequency.

6.4 Spend time doing#

✗ I spent two hours to read the report. → ✓ I spent two hours reading the report.

Russian uses an infinitive — потратил два часа, чтобы прочитать — and English uses -ing after spend. The rule is lexical: spend + time + -ing, waste + time + -ing, be busy + -ing, have trouble + -ing, there's no point + -ing.

The trap in the other direction. Take goes with a to-infinitive, and take time to do something is correct English with its own meaning. So the pair is:

It takes me an hour to get to work. · I spend an hour getting to work.

Same fact, two grammars. ✗ It takes me an hour getting to work and ✗ I spend an hour to get to work are both wrong.

6.5 It takes me — word order#

✗ It takes to me twenty minutes. · ✗ It needs me twenty minutes. ✓ It takes me twenty minutes to get to work.

The frame
It takes + [person] + [amount of time] + to + [verb].
✗ Wrong✓ Right
It takes twenty minutes me to get there.It takes me twenty minutes to get there.
It took me to finish three days.It took me three days to finish.

The person slot is optional — It takes twenty minutes to get downtown — but it is never marked with to or for. And the verb is take, not need or last: ✗ The movie needs two hours → The movie runs two hours.

7. Age and life stages#

✗ I have thirty years. → ✓ I am thirty. / I'm thirty years old.

Russian says мне тридцать лет — a dative with no verb at all — and the learner reaches for whichever European pattern they met first, usually have. English treats age as a property of the subject, expressed with be.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
I have thirty years. · I am thirty years.I'm thirty. · I'm thirty years old.
He is 30 years old man.He's a 30-year-old man.
on the age of 30at the age of 30

The hyphen rule, which is the second half of this error. Before a noun, the phrase is hyphenated and the unit is singular:

✗ Wrong✓ Right
a two-years contracta two-year contract
a ten-minutes breaka ten-minute break
an eight hours shiftan eight-hour shift

After the noun, the hyphens go and the plural returns: a contract of two years, The boy is five years old. The compression into a modifier is what forces the singular.

The rest of the age vocabulary, where the errors are quieter: in his thirties (not ✗ in his thirty), in her late forties, she just turned forty (not ✗ became forty), a man in his mid-fifties. Life stages, generations, and eras are in Age, life stages, and eras.

8. Scheduling#

8.1 Push back and move up#

Two phrasal verbs that point in opposite directions, that both sound like they should mean "delay," and that appear in every American work calendar.

Phrasal verbDirectionExample
push back · move backlaterCan we push the call back to four?
put off · postponelaterWe put off the launch until May.
move upearlierThey moved the meeting up to nine.
move forwardambiguous — avoidhalf of American speakers hear "earlier," half hear "later"

Why it happens. Перенести carries no direction, so the learner picks an English phrasal verb by sound. Push back feels neutral; it is not. And move up sounds like "increase," which in a calendar would be later — but up here means toward the present, so it means earlier.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
Let's move up the meeting to next week. (meaning later)Let's push the meeting back to next week.
Can we move the call forward an hour?…move it up an hour (earlier) · …push it back an hour (later)
The safe habit in writing
name the new time. Can we move the call to 4 p.m.? has no direction to get wrong. The full inventory is in Phrasal verbs of time.

8.2 One Russian verb, three English ones#

Перенести встречу maps onto whichever English verb matches the direction and the formality.

The prepositions are fixed

8.3 Free on Tuesday, not at Tuesday#

✗ I will be free at Tuesday. → ✓ I'll be free on Tuesday. / I'm free Tuesday.

The preposition rule from §1.2 does not change because the sentence is about a calendar, but scheduling is where Russian speakers break it most, since Russian says в for all of it: во вторник, в три часа, в мае.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
I'm free at Tuesday.I'm free on Tuesday. · I'm free Tuesday.
Let's meet in 3 p.m.Let's meet at 3 p.m.
in the morning of Tuesdayon Tuesday morning
The deadline is until Friday.The deadline is Friday. · It's due by Friday.

That last row is the §1.4 error wearing a suit. A deadline is a point, so it takes by or nothing — though You have until Friday is correct, because have is a stretch. The American office vocabulary around meetings and deadlines is in Schedules, appointments, and deadlines.

9. Word-choice traps#

9.1 Time, countable and uncountable#

Time is two different nouns wearing one spelling, and Russian время and раз are two different words — so the error runs both ways.

SenseCountable?Example
time as a substance — времяnoI don't have time.
an occasion — разyesthree times, the first time
a clock reading, an experienceyesWhat time is it? · We had a great time.
✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
I don't have a time.I don't have time.uncountable in this sense
How many time have you been there?How many times…occasions are countable
How many times do you need? (meaning how long)How much time do you need?much for the substance
I worked eight times yesterday.I worked eight hours yesterday.times never means hours

That fourth row is the specifically Russian one. Восемь часов is eight hours; восемь раз is eight times. The learner who has just been taught that time can be countable applies it to the wrong Russian word.

9.2 Hour, o'clock, and time#

Russian час means both hour and one o'clock, and время covers what a clock shows. English keeps three words: hour is a 60-minute stretch, o'clock is a position on the clock face, and time is what the clock reads.

✗ Wrong✓ Right

At what hour does it start?What time does it start? · When does it start?

9.3 Watch and clock#

Часы covers both, and the split is purely about where the object sits: a watch is on your wrist or in your pocket — you carry it — and a clock stays put on a wall, a desk, a tower, or a stove.

✗ Wrong✓ Right
My clock is five minutes slow. (on your wrist)My watch is five minutes slow.
Look at the watch on the wall.Look at the clock on the wall.
I looked at my hours. · a hand clockI looked at my watch. · a wristwatch

The phone has muddied this slightly — the clock on my phone is what Americans say, because the app is a clock — but the wrist object is always a watch.

9.4 Last and latest#

Последний covers both, and getting it wrong changes the fact rather than the register. Last means final — nothing comes after it. Latest means most recent, with more expected. And the last + a period means the one just past: last week, the last ten minutes.

✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
Did you see his last movie?…his latest movie?last implies he has retired or died
What's the last news?…the latest news?more news is coming

Next has a smaller version of the same problem. Next Friday is ambiguous even to Americans — this week's Friday or the one after — and the next Friday means the one following whatever moment is under discussion. If the date matters, say the date.

These mean one thing in English and another in Russian, and the time-related ones are the most damaging because the sentences they produce sound fluent.

Two repay extra attention. Actual is the worst offender in professional writing, because актуальная информация is exactly what a résumé or a status update is about. ✗ Please send me the actual data asks an American for the real data, implying the previous batch was fake; what you wanted was the current data. And momentarily splits by country: in American English it means in a moment — We'll be landing momentarily — while in British English it means for a moment. Set phrases built on time words — in no time, about time, the eleventh hour, around the clock — are collected in Idioms and set phrases.

10. Tense-driven time errors#

Four errors here are really tense errors wearing time vocabulary. They are treated properly elsewhere; this section names them so the diagnostic table can point at them.

The future after when and if.

✗ I'll call you when I will arrive. → ✓ …when I arrive. · ✗ If it will rain, we'll cancel. → ✓ If it rains…

English marks the future once, in the main clause. Time clauses opened by when, as soon as, until, before, after, once, and by the time take a present form for future time, exactly as if-clauses do. Russian marks it in both halves, which is where the error comes from. Full treatment: The future and The four types.

The present for a stretch reaching now. ✗ I work here since 2019 → ✓ I've worked here since 2019. This is §6.1, and the machinery is in Present perfect.

Finished time with a present perfect. ✗ I have seen him yesterday → ✓ I saw him yesterday. Yesterday, last week, in 2019, two hours ago — each closes the time frame, and a closed frame takes the past simple.

The American just. I just ate and I've just eaten are both correct, and the past simple is the ordinary American form with just, already, and yet. A learner taught the British rule will over-correct into the perfect; nothing is wrong with that, and nothing is wrong with the simple either.

Quick reference#

Ten rules that prevent most time errors.

  1. No preposition before last, next, this, every, yesterday, today, tomorrow, tonight.
  2. At a point, on a day, in anything longer. At night, but in the morning.
  3. In an hour, never through. By a deadline, until a stretch. For a length, during a named period, while a clause. Since a starting point.
  4. Say the digits. Six thirty cannot be misread; half seven can.
  5. O'clock only with a whole hour, and never beside a.m. or p.m.
  6. Month first in American dates. Capitalize the day and the month. Spell the month whenever it matters.
  7. A stretch that reaches now takes a perfect: I've lived here for two years, never I live here two years.
  8. Already for affirmatives, yet for negatives and questions, still for what continues, anymore for what stopped.
  9. Age takes be, and a hyphenated time modifier takes the singular: a two-year contract.
  10. Up is earlier and back is later — or skip both and name the new time.
