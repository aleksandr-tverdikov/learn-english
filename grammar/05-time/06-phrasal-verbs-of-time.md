# Phrasal verbs of time

> The verbs English actually uses to postpone, start, finish, stretch, schedule, sleep through and waste a stretch of time — grouped by the job they do, with the separability rule for each.

[← Time](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

English has a Latinate verb for almost every operation you can perform on a schedule — *postpone*, *commence*, *conclude*, *accelerate*, *reschedule* — and in ordinary speech it uses almost none of them. The meeting is not postponed; it **gets pushed back**. Nobody commences the meeting; somebody **kicks it off**. It does not conclude; they **wrap it up**, or it **runs over** and everybody **knocks off** late. A learner who knows only the Latinate half of the vocabulary can read a contract and cannot follow a conversation about one.

Most of the verbs below already have full dictionary entries — pronunciation, Russian glosses, six examples each, and the separability rule spelled out — in **[the verb dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/README.md)**, which covers the irregular verbs and the phrasal verbs built on them. This section does the thing a dictionary cannot: it puts them side by side by **what they do**, so that you can pick the right one. *Put off*, *push back*, *hold off*, *shelve* and *table* all translate «отложить», and they are not interchangeable in a single sentence.

## How to read the tables

Three shapes turn up in every group below, and the difference is not stylistic — it decides where the object can stand.

| Shape | Object position | Pronoun object | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Separable** particle verb | either side | **must** go in the middle | *put **off** the meeting* / *put the meeting **off*** / *put it **off*** — ✗ *put off it* |
| **Inseparable** prepositional verb | after the particle only | also after the particle | *sat **on** the report* / *sat **on** it* — ✗ *sat it on* |
| **Intransitive** | no object at all | — | *The meeting **ran over**.* — ✗ *ran over the meeting* (that is a different verb) |

**The pronoun test settles it.** Say the phrase with *it* or *them*. If *put it off* sounds right and *put off it* sounds wrong, the verb is separable. If *get around to it* sounds right and ✗ *get it around to* sounds wrong, it is inseparable. Native speakers apply it without noticing, and it is the single most reliable thing you can memorize about phrasal verbs. One rule rides along with it: **a long object goes at the end**, because a heavy noun phrase does not sit comfortably in the middle — *push the meeting back*, but *push back the meeting scheduled for Thursday afternoon*.

The **Separable?** column below uses four values: **yes** (either side, pronoun in the middle), **no** (inseparable), **—** (intransitive, no object), and **fixed** (an idiom whose parts do not move at all, like *call it a day*).

## Delaying

The largest group, because English has more ways to not do something than to do it. They differ in **who is delaying, how long, and whether it is polite**.

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **put off** | postpone to a later, usually unspecified time | yes | *We **put off** the trip until spring.* &middot; *Don't **put it off** again.* |
| **hold off (on)** | wait before doing something; delay acting | no (*on* + noun / *-ing*) | *Let's **hold off on** a decision until Friday.* &middot; *They're **holding off** buying until rates drop.* |
| **push back** | move to a specific later time | yes | *Can we **push** the call **back** an hour?* &middot; *They **pushed back** the launch to March.* |
| **put back** | set a clock to an earlier reading; (British) postpone | yes | *We **put** the clocks **back** on Sunday.* |
| **hold up** | delay someone or something, usually by obstruction | yes, usually passive | *We **were held up** in traffic.* &middot; *Permits **held** the project **up** six months.* |
| **drag out** | deliberately make something last longer than it needs to | yes | *He **dragged** the meeting **out** for two hours.* &middot; *Don't **drag it out**.* |
| **stall** | delay on purpose to gain time | yes (a plain verb) | *Quit **stalling**.* &middot; *She **stalled** him until the lawyer arrived.* |
| **sit on** | receive something and deliberately do nothing with it | no | *They **sat on** the report for eight months.* &middot; *The committee is **sitting on** it.* |
| **table** | *(American)* take off the agenda; postpone indefinitely | yes (a plain verb) | *The board **tabled** the proposal.* &middot; *Let's **table** this and come back to it.* |
| **defer** | postpone formally; put off a payment or an obligation | yes (a plain verb) | *You can **defer** payment for ninety days.* &middot; *The decision **was deferred**.* |
| **shelve** | put aside indefinitely, with no plan to return to it | yes (a plain verb) | *The studio **shelved** the sequel.* &middot; *That idea got **shelved** years ago.* |

### The four degrees of postponement

They are not synonyms. They differ in how dead the thing is.

> We'll **hold off on** it for now. — not yet; nothing has moved  
> We'll **push** it **back** to the ninth. — it has a new date  
> We'll **put** it **off** until we hear from them. — it has no new date, but it is alive  
> We'll **shelve** it. — it is not coming back unless something changes

*Table* belongs at the *shelve* end in American usage, and *defer* is the formal register of *put off* — a letter from a bank defers your payments, a friend puts off lunch. Two of the eleven are accusations rather than descriptions: *drag out* says somebody is padding the thing on purpose (*he **dragged** the meeting **out** another hour*), and *sit on* says somebody received it and buried it (*they **sat on** our application from May to November*).

### *Table* means opposite things on opposite sides of the Atlantic

This is the single most dangerous word on the page, because both usages are ordinary in their own country and neither sounds foreign.

> **American:** *The committee **tabled** the motion.* = they set it aside; it will not be discussed  
> **British:** *The committee **tabled** the motion.* = they put it forward; it is now on the agenda

An American *tables* something to stop talking about it. A Briton *tables* something to start. In an international meeting, say *set it aside* or *put it on the agenda* and let everybody keep their afternoon.

### *Put back* is not the American way to postpone

*Put back* meaning "postpone" is British. American English keeps *put back* for two other jobs, and reaches for *push back* or *move back* for the schedule.

> ✗ *They **put** the meeting **back** to Thursday.* → *They **pushed** the meeting **back** to Thursday.*  
> *We **put** the clocks **back** an hour in November.* — the standard American use  
> *Please **put** the milk **back** in the fridge.* — the literal one

### *Hold off* is intransitive, *hold up* is not

*Hold off* names your own restraint and takes no direct object — the thing delayed goes after *on*, as a noun or an *-ing* form. *Hold up* names an outside obstruction and takes an object, most often in the passive.

> ✗ *Let's **hold off** the decision.* → *Let's **hold off on** the decision.*  
> ✗ *I **held off** to call her.* → *I **held off** calling her.* / *I **held off on** calling her.*  
> *Sorry I'm late — I **got held up** at the office.*

*Hold off* has a second, unrelated sense with an object — to keep an attacker or the weather at bay: *They **held off** the other team for the last five minutes.* And *hold up* means "rob at gunpoint" as well, which is why *a holdup* can be either a traffic jam or a bank robbery.

## Bringing forward

The mirror image, and a much shorter list — proof of something about how schedules actually move.

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **move up** | *(American)* reschedule to an **earlier** time | yes | *They **moved** the wedding **up** to June.* &middot; *Can we **move it up** to nine?* |
| **bring forward** | reschedule to an earlier time; formal, business | yes | *The hearing **was brought forward** two weeks.* |
| **bump up** | move earlier, or raise a number; informal | yes | *Can you **bump** my appointment **up**?* &middot; *They **bumped up** the price.* |
| **pull in** | *(project jargon)* bring a deadline earlier | yes | *We **pulled** the launch date **in** by a month.* |
| **step up** | increase the intensity or pace of something | yes | *The city **stepped up** inspections.* &middot; *We need to **step up** production.* |
| **speed up** | go faster, or make something go faster | yes / — | *The line **sped up**.* &middot; *They **sped up** the approval process.* |
| **hurry up** | be quicker; make somebody be quicker | yes / — | ***Hurry up**, we're late.* &middot; *Can you **hurry** him **up**?* |
| **rush through** | do or approve something too quickly | yes | *Congress **rushed** the bill **through**.* &middot; *Don't **rush through** the last page.* |

### *Move up* means earlier — and it is worth checking

In American English *move a meeting up* means make it **earlier**, and *move it back* means make it **later**, by analogy with a queue: the event moves toward the front. The metaphor is not obvious to anyone, and even Americans confirm it in email.

> "Can we **move** Thursday's call **up**?" — to Wednesday, or to an earlier hour  
> "Can we **move** Thursday's call **back**?" — to Friday, or to a later hour

Because the pair is genuinely confusing, business English has quietly settled on *push back* for later and *move up* or *bring forward* for earlier, with the new time always stated: *Let's push it back to three.* Say the time and nobody can misread you.

### *Speed up*, *step up*, *hurry up*, *rush through*

Four ways of going faster, with four different implications.

> The assembly line **sped up**. — the rate itself increased; neutral  
> The city **stepped up** patrols. — a deliberate increase in effort or intensity  
> **Hurry up!** — an instruction to a person, and mildly rude to a stranger  
> They **rushed** the bill **through**. — fast, and the speaker disapproves

*Speed up* works both ways with no change of form: *the train speeds up*, *they sped up the process*. *Hurry up* is overwhelmingly a command; *hurry* with an object (*don't hurry me*) is the polite alternative.

## Starting

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **start off / start out** | begin a period, a journey or a career in a certain way | — | *We **started off** with coffee.* &middot; *He **started out** as a mechanic.* |
| **kick off** | begin an event, meeting or season | yes / — | *The season **kicks off** Sunday.* &middot; *She **kicked off** the meeting with an apology.* |
| **set off** | begin a journey | — | *They **set off** at dawn.* |
| **get going** | start; get moving | — (or *get* + object + *going*) | *We should **get going**.* &middot; *Let's **get** this thing **going**.* |
| **get under way** | begin; of a process or event, formal | — | *The renovation **got under way** in April.* |
| **set out** | begin a journey, or begin with an aim | — | *We **set out** to prove them wrong.* |
| **take off** | begin to succeed quickly; of a plane, leave the ground | — | *The business **took off** in 2019.* &middot; *We **take off** at 6:40.* |
| **jump in** | start doing something without preparation; interrupt | — | *Don't wait for the agenda — just **jump in**.* |
| **dive in** | start immediately and enthusiastically | — | *She **dove in** without reading the instructions.* |

### *Set off* has two lives

Intransitive, it starts a journey. Transitive and separable, it triggers something — and the two never overlap, because the first takes no object at all.

> We **set off** for Tulsa a little after six. — departure  
> The smoke **set off** the alarm. &middot; *It **set** the alarm **off**.* — trigger  
> ✗ *We **set off** the trip at six.* → *We **set off** at six.* / *We **set out** at six.*

*Set out* and *set off* are near-identical for a journey. *Set out* adds a purpose — *set out to do something* — which *set off* does not have: ✗ *he set off to prove them wrong*.

### *Take off*: three senses, all intransitive

> The flight **takes off** at ten. — a plane leaves the ground  
> The podcast really **took off** last year. — it began to succeed fast  
> He just **took off** without saying goodbye. — he left abruptly (informal)

A fully separable *take off* also covers removing things and time away from work (***take** a week **off***), and that one always has an object: *I'm taking Friday off* is time, *the plane is taking off* is departure.

### *Get under way* is three words, and it has no object

Americans write *under way* as two words in careful prose (*the search got under way*), though *underway* as one word is now common and is standard as an adjective before a noun (*an underway replenishment*). The verb takes no object and no passive.

> ✗ *They **got under way** the meeting.* → *They **got** the meeting **under way**.* / *The meeting **got under way**.*  
> ✗ *The project **was gotten under way**.* → *The project **got under way**.*

## Finishing

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **wrap up** | finish; bring to a close | yes / — | *Let's **wrap up** — it's almost six.* &middot; *She **wrapped** the call **up** fast.* |
| **wind up** | bring to an end; **or** end up in an unplanned state | yes / — | *They **wound up** the meeting early.* &middot; *We **wound up** staying two extra nights.* |
| **wind down** | gradually reduce activity; relax at the end of the day | — / yes | *The party **wound down** around one.* &middot; *They're **winding down** operations.* |
| **finish up** | complete the last part of something | yes / — | *I'll **finish up** and meet you outside.* &middot; ***Finish up** your homework.* |
| **round off** | end something pleasantly | yes | *We **rounded off** the evening with dessert.* |
| **call it a day** | stop working for the day | fixed | *It's seven — let's **call it a day**.* |
| **call it a night** | stop an evening's activity and go home | fixed | *After the second set we **called it a night**.* |
| **knock off** | stop work, especially at the usual hour | — | *We **knock off** at five on Fridays.* |
| **clock out** | record the end of a shift | — | *He **clocked out** at 4:58.* |
| **close out** | *(American)* finish and settle — a quarter, an account, a file | yes | *We **closed out** the quarter ahead of target.* |
| **run out (of time)** | have no more of something left | no (*of* + noun) | *We're **running out of** time.* &middot; *Time **ran out**.* |
| **time out** | of a system or session, expire automatically | — | *The page **timed out**.* &middot; *My session **times out** after ten minutes.* |
| **be up** | of an allotted period, be finished | — | ***Time's up.*** &middot; *Your hour **is up**.* |

### *Wind up* and *wind down* are not opposites

They look like a pair and they are not. Both are pronounced with /waɪnd/ and both take the past form *wound* /waʊnd/, which is the first trap; the second is that *wind up* has a second meaning with no *wind down* counterpart at all.

| Phrase | Meaning | Object? |
|---|---|---|
| ***wind up*** (transitive) | bring to a deliberate close | *wind up the meeting* |
| ***wind up*** (intransitive) | end up somewhere or in some state, unplanned | *we wound up in Reno* |
| ***wind down*** (intransitive) | taper off gradually | *the evening wound down* |
| ***wind down*** (transitive) | shut something down in stages | *wind down the division* |

> We **wound up** the meeting at four. — we ended it  
> We **wound up** working until four. — that is where the day landed, and nobody planned it  
> The music **wound down** and people started putting their coats on.

*Wind up* in the second sense is one of the most useful verbs in conversational American English and has no neat single-word equivalent: *ended up* is the close synonym, *оказаться* is the Russian.

### *Knock off*, *clock out*, *call it a day*

All three end the workday, at different levels of formality and literalness.

> We **knock off** at five. — the habitual hour; informal, blue-collar in origin, now general  
> I **clocked out** at 4:58. — literally recorded on a timecard or an app  
> Let's **call it a day**. — a decision made in the moment, usually because progress has stopped

*Knock off* also means to reduce a price (*knock ten dollars off*) and, in crime slang, to kill or to rob; the "finish fast" sense takes *out*, not *off* (*I'll knock this out before lunch*). *Call it a day* can be used at any hour and about anything, including a career — *After thirty years, she called it a day* — while *call it a night* is only ever used in the evening, about an evening's activity rather than about work.

### *Run out*, *time out*, *be up*

Three ways to say a period has ended, and none of them is interchangeable with the others.

> We **ran out of** time. — a subject with people in it; *of* is obligatory before a noun  
> Time **ran out**. — no *of*, because *time* is now the subject  
> The session **timed out**. — a machine ended it, automatically, after inactivity  
> **Time's up.** — an announcement to whoever was being timed

> ✗ *We **run out** time.* → *We **ran out of** time.*  
> ✗ *The time **is finished**.* → ***Time's up**.* / *Time **is up**.*

*Time out* is also a noun with a hyphen — *a time-out* in basketball, *a time-out* for a misbehaving four-year-old — and a transitive verb in computing: *the server timed the request out*.

## Continuing and lasting

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **carry on** | continue | — (or *with* + noun) | ***Carry on** — don't let me interrupt.* &middot; *She **carried on with** the presentation.* |
| **keep on** | continue, often stubbornly | — (+ *-ing*) | *He **kept on** talking through the whole movie.* |
| **go on** | continue; happen; last | — | *The argument **went on** for an hour.* &middot; *What's **going on**?* |
| **drag on** | continue far too long, tediously | — | *The lawsuit **dragged on** for six years.* |
| **stretch on** | extend, of time or distance, with no end in sight | — | *The afternoon **stretched on**.* |
| **run over** | exceed the allotted time | — | *The meeting **ran over** by twenty minutes.* |
| **run late** | be behind schedule | fixed | *I'm **running late** — start without me.* |
| **hold out** | last; be enough to reach the end | — | *Will the battery **hold out** until we land?* |
| **last out** | *(chiefly British)* survive to the end of a period | yes / — | *He won't **last out** the week.* |
| **live on** | continue to exist after its source is gone | — | *Her music **lives on**.* |
| **wear on** | of time, to pass slowly and wearingly | — | *As the evening **wore on**, people drifted home.* |

### *Go on* + *-ing* and *go on* + *to*

One of the highest-value contrasts in the language, and it is entirely carried by the form after the verb.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *go on* + *-ing* | **continue** doing the same thing | *She **went on talking** for another hour.* |
| *go on* + *to* + infinitive | **move on** to the next thing | *She **went on to talk** about the budget.* |

> He **went on reading** the report. — he was reading, and he kept reading  
> He **went on to read** the report. — he finished something else, then read it

*Carry on* and *keep on* both take *-ing* and both mean "continue," with *keep on* adding persistence, usually irritating: *He kept on asking* implies he should have stopped. *Carry on* is neutral and is the polite word for resuming after an interruption.

### *Drag on* versus *drag out*

The intransitive one blames the event; the transitive one blames a person.

> The negotiations **dragged on** for months. — nobody in particular is at fault  
> They **dragged** the negotiations **out** for months. — somebody did this on purpose

*Stretch on* is the neutral member of that family — long, but without the note of irritation: *The road stretched on for another forty miles.*

### *Run over*, *run late*, *run behind*

*Run* is the workhorse verb of scheduling, and the particle decides which thing is off.

> The meeting **ran over**. — the **event** used more time than it had  
> I'm **running late**. — the **person** will arrive after the appointed time  
> The doctor is **running behind**. — the whole **schedule** has slipped, for everyone in the waiting room

*Run over* has two other senses — to hit with a vehicle (*he ran over a nail*) and to review something quickly (*let's run over the agenda*) — and only the time sense is intransitive, which is what tells them apart.

### *Hold out* and *wear on*

*Hold out* asks whether a supply will reach the end: the battery, the food, the weather, the money. It is intransitive in that sense; the separate *hold out for* means to refuse to settle for less (*they're holding out for more money*), and *hold out* with an object means to extend (*she held out her hand*).

*Wear on* is almost fixed: the subject is a stretch of time, the tense is nearly always past, and the sentence is nearly always a scene-setter.

> As the afternoon **wore on**, it became clear nobody was coming.  
> The winter **wore on** and the road stayed closed.

✗ *I wore on my work* is not English. Only time wears on.

## Catching up and falling behind

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **catch up on** | deal with a backlog of something | no | *I need to **catch up on** sleep.* &middot; *She's **catching up on** email.* |
| **catch up with** | reach somebody ahead of you; meet to exchange news | no | *Go ahead — I'll **catch up with** you.* &middot; *Let's **catch up** next week.* |
| **fall behind** | drop back relative to a schedule or a group | no (+ *on* / *with*) | *We **fell behind** schedule in July.* &middot; *He **fell behind on** the rent.* |
| **get behind** | *(American, informal)* the same, said of oneself | no (+ *on*) | *I **got behind on** my reading.* |
| **keep up (with)** | maintain the same pace as | no | *I can't **keep up with** the reading.* |
| **make up time** | recover time already lost by going faster | yes | *The pilot **made up** most of the time over Kansas.* |
| **make up for** | compensate for something lost | no | *We **made up for** lost time.* |
| **gain on** | gradually close the gap with somebody ahead | no | *The other boat is **gaining on** us.* |
| **lose ground** | fall behind relative to a competitor | fixed | *The incumbent is **losing ground** in the suburbs.* |

### *Catch up on* versus *catch up with*

Both are inseparable, and the preposition decides whether you are dealing with a **thing** or a **person**.

> I have to **catch up on** work. — a backlog of tasks  
> I have to **catch up with** Dana. — a person, either to reach her or to hear her news

Bare *catch up* with nothing after it means the social version: *We should catch up sometime* is an invitation to coffee, not an offer to help with your inbox.

A third use turns the tables: *catch up with* is also what consequences do to people. *All those late nights are **catching up with** him* — the subject is the problem, not the person.

### *Get behind* points two ways

In American English *get behind* + *on* is falling behind; *get behind* + a plain object is supporting something.

> I **got behind on** the invoices. — I am late with them  
> The whole department **got behind** the proposal. — they backed it

### *Make up time* and *make up for lost time*

*Make up time* is literal and separable: you lost twenty minutes and you recover them. *Make up for* is figurative and inseparable: you compensate for something that cannot be recovered.

> The flight left late but **made up** the time in the air.  
> They spent the whole weekend together, **making up for** lost time.  
> ✗ *We made up for the time in the air.* → *We **made up** the time in the air.*

## Scheduling

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **pencil in** | schedule tentatively, subject to change | yes | ***Pencil me in** for Thursday.* &middot; *I've **penciled in** the 14th.* |
| **block off / block out** | reserve a continuous stretch of time | yes | *I **blocked off** two hours Friday morning.* |
| **set aside** | reserve time, money or attention for a purpose | yes | ***Set aside** an hour for the drive.* |
| **pin down** | get somebody to commit to a definite time | yes | *I can't **pin him down** to a date.* |
| **fit in** | find room in a schedule for | yes | *The dentist can **fit you in** at four.* |
| **squeeze in** | fit in with difficulty | yes | *We **squeezed in** a coffee between flights.* |
| **slot in** | place into an open slot in a schedule | yes | *We'll **slot** your item **in** after lunch.* |
| **double-book** | schedule two commitments for one slot | no (a plain verb) | *I **double-booked** myself again.* |
| **clock in** | record the start of a shift | — | *She **clocks in** at seven.* |
| **check in** | register your arrival; report briefly to somebody | — (+ *with*) | *We **check in** at three.* &middot; *I'll **check in with** you Monday.* |
| **sign in / sign out** | record your arrival or departure in writing | yes / — | *Please **sign in** at the desk.* &middot; *He **signed** the visitor **out**.* |

### *Pencil in* is a promise you can break

The metaphor is the point: pencil can be erased. *Penciling something in* commits you to nothing and signals as much, which is the polite way to accept an invitation you are not sure about.

> "Are you free on the 12th?" "**Pencil me in** and I'll confirm Monday."  
> We've **penciled in** March for the rollout, but nothing's fixed.

The opposite is *lock in* (*Let's **lock in** the 12th*), or *pin down*, which is what you do to a person rather than a date — and it is mildly adversarial, implying the other person has been dodging.

### *Fit in*, *squeeze in*, *block off*

Three degrees of room, and all three are separable, which matters because the object is usually a pronoun.

> Can you **fit me in** this week? — ✗ *fit in me*  
> We **squeezed** a walk **in** before dinner. — with effort  
> I've **blocked off** Thursday afternoon. — the time is now unavailable to everyone else

*Fit in* has a completely separate intransitive sense about belonging (*she never really fit in*), and *block out* has one about refusing to think of something (*he blocked out the whole year*). Both are distinguished by having no object.

### *Clock in*, *check in*, *sign in*

They look like variants and they are used in three different places.

| Verb | Where | What it records |
|---|---|---|
| **clock in / clock out** | a workplace, for pay | the exact start and end of a shift |
| **check in / check out** | a hotel, a flight, a conference, a clinic | your arrival as a guest or passenger |
| **sign in / sign out** | a visitor log, a building, a piece of equipment | your name, on a list |

*Check in* also has a purely social sense — *I just wanted to check in* means "see how you are," and *check in with somebody* is the standard American way to say you will report back. *Clock in at* has yet another sense with no time-keeping in it at all: *The movie clocks in at three hours and ten minutes*, meaning "measures."

## Waking and sleeping

The densest family in the whole section, because English divides the morning into steps that most languages cover with one verb each.

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **wake up** | stop sleeping; cause somebody to stop sleeping | yes / — | *I **woke up** at five.* &middot; ***Wake me up** at six.* |
| **get up** | leave the bed; rise from a seat | yes / — | *I **woke up** at five but didn't **get up** until six.* |
| **sleep in** | *(American)* sleep later than usual, on purpose | — | *We **slept in** on Saturday.* |
| **lie in** | *(British)* the same | — | *He likes a **lie-in** on Sundays.* |
| **oversleep** | sleep past the time you meant to get up | — (one word) | *I **overslept** and missed the train.* |
| **sleep through** | stay asleep despite something | no | *I **slept through** my alarm.* &middot; *She **slept through** the storm.* |
| **stay up** | not go to bed | — | *We **stayed up** until two watching the returns.* |
| **turn in** | go to bed *(informal)* | — | *I'm going to **turn in** early.* |
| **doze off** | fall into a light sleep without meaning to | — | *He **dozed off** in the second half.* |
| **nod off** | fall asleep sitting up, briefly | — | *She **nodded off** during the sermon.* |
| **drift off** | fall asleep gradually and pleasantly | — | *I **drifted off** listening to the rain.* |
| **set an alarm** | arrange to be woken | fixed collocation | *I **set an alarm** for 5:45.* |
| **hit the snooze** | press the button for nine more minutes | fixed | *He **hit snooze** four times.* |

### *Wake up* and *get up* are two different events

Russian «вставать» and «просыпаться» make this distinction too, and learners still collapse it in English, usually by using *wake up* for both.

> I **wake up** at five and lie there. I **get up** at six. — two acts, an hour apart  
> ✗ *I wake up from bed at six.* → *I **get up** at six.*  
> ***Wake** the kids **up**, and make sure they actually **get up**.*

*Wake up* is separable, and with a pronoun the middle position is obligatory: *wake me up*, never ✗ *wake up me*. *Get up* behaves the same with a causative object: *get the kids up*.

### *Sleep in*, *lie in*, *oversleep* — three different mornings

> I **slept in** on Sunday. — deliberately, and it was pleasant  
> I **overslept** on Monday. — accidentally, and it was a problem  
> He's having a **lie-in**. — British; an American says *sleeping in*

*Oversleep* is a single word and takes no object: ✗ *I overslept my alarm* → *I **slept through** my alarm*, or *I **overslept***. And *sleep in* has a second, now dated sense in American English — a live-in domestic employee "slept in" — which nobody will hear in a sentence about Saturday.

### *Sleep through* has two readings

The preposition is the same and the difference is what follows it. Both are inseparable: ✗ *slept the alarm through*.

> I **slept through** the alarm. — it went off; I did not wake  
> The baby finally **slept through** the night. — a continuous stretch, all the way to morning

### *Doze off*, *nod off*, *drift off*

All three mean falling asleep unintentionally, and they differ in posture and in how sorry you should feel about it.

| Verb | Picture | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|
| **doze off** | light sleep, easily interrupted | an armchair, a lecture, a long drive as a passenger |
| **nod off** | the head actually drops | a meeting, a class, church — mildly embarrassing |
| **drift off** | gradual and pleasant | in bed, on purpose or nearly so |

*Fall asleep* is the neutral verb that covers all three and is never wrong. Also nearby: *pass out* (from exhaustion or alcohol), *crash* (*I crashed at nine*), and *conk out*, which a person or a machine does when it quits entirely.

### *Turn in* and *stay up*

*Turn in* is bedtime, and it is the same phrasal verb as *turn in your homework* only in spelling. When it means bedtime it has no object; when it means submit, it is separable and always has one.

> I'm going to **turn in**. — I'm going to bed  
> ***Turn in** your timesheets by Friday.* / ***Turn** them **in** by Friday.* — submit them

*Stay up* means to remain out of bed, and it needs a marker of how long or how late: *stay up late*, *stay up till two*, *stay up for the fireworks*. Bare ✗ *I stayed up* with nothing after it sounds unfinished. *Stay awake* is a different fight — it is about not falling asleep, whether or not you are in bed.

## Arriving and timing

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **show up** | arrive, especially where expected | — | *Only four people **showed up**.* |
| **turn up** | arrive unexpectedly; be found after being lost | — | *He **turned up** at midnight with a pizza.* &middot; *My keys **turned up** in the car.* |
| **roll in** | arrive late and unhurried | — | *He **rolled in** around eleven.* |
| **make it** | succeed in getting there, or in getting there in time | fixed | *I can't **make it** Thursday.* &middot; *We **made it** with a minute to spare.* |
| **get in** | arrive — of a person, a flight, a train | — | *What time does your flight **get in**?* |
| **run behind** | be behind schedule | — | *The clinic is **running behind** today.* |
| **be running late** | be going to arrive after the appointed time | fixed | *I'm **running late** — twenty minutes out.* |
| **cut it close** | leave dangerously little margin | fixed | *We made the connection, but we **cut it close**.* |
| **beat the clock** | finish before the deadline | fixed | *They **beat the clock** by ninety seconds.* |

### *Show up*, *turn up*, *roll in*

All three are intransitive and all three carry an attitude.

> Half the class didn't **show up**. — neutral, and the standard word for attendance  
> She **turned up** an hour later with no explanation. — unexpectedly, often unwelcome  
> He **rolled in** at eleven like nothing had happened. — late, casual, speaker annoyed

*Turn up* also means "be found": *The wallet turned up behind the couch.* A missing object turns up; a missing person shows up.

### *Make it* is the verb Russian speakers most often need

*Make it* covers three ideas at once — arriving, arriving in time, and being able to attend — and no single English verb replaces it.

> Can you **make it** on Friday? — can you attend  
> We **made it** to the gate with four minutes to spare. — we arrived in time  
> He didn't **make it** through the first round. — he did not survive to the next stage

It is fixed: the object is always *it*, and the destination follows *to*. ✗ *I can't make the meeting it* → *I can't **make it** to the meeting*, or simply *I can't **make** the meeting*.

### *Cut it close* and *beat the clock*

Two idioms for the margin around a deadline, from opposite emotional directions. The British form of the first is *cut it fine*.

> We **cut it close** — the doors were closing as we got there. — we nearly failed  
> They **beat the clock** and filed at 4:59. — we nearly failed, and we are pleased about it

## Passing time

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **kill time** | fill an empty interval with anything at all | fixed | *I **killed time** in the bookstore until my flight.* |
| **pass the time** | occupy an interval pleasantly | fixed | *We played cards to **pass the time**.* |
| **while away** | spend a stretch of time idly and agreeably | yes | *They **whiled away** the afternoon on the porch.* |
| **fritter away** | waste time or money in small useless pieces | yes | *I **frittered away** the whole morning online.* |
| **fly by** | pass very quickly | — | *The summer **flew by**.* |
| **creep by** | pass painfully slowly | — | *The last hour **crept by**.* |
| **whiz by** | pass extremely fast | — | *The years **whiz by** once you have kids.* |

### *Kill time* versus *pass the time*

Nearly synonymous, and the difference is worth having.

> I **killed** two hours at the airport. — the time was in my way; I got rid of it  
> We **passed the time** telling stories. — the time was going to pass anyway; we filled it well

*Kill time* is always about waiting for something else. *Pass the time* can describe a whole pleasant afternoon with nothing waiting at the end of it. Note the articles: *kill time* takes no article, *pass **the** time* requires one.

### *Waste away* does not mean waste time

The trap on this page that produces the most startling sentences. *Waste away* is an intransitive verb about a **body**: to become progressively thinner and weaker through illness or starvation.

> ✗ *I **wasted away** the whole afternoon.* → *I **wasted** the whole afternoon.*  
> *After the diagnosis he **wasted away** to nothing.* — the only meaning it has

To waste time, use plain *waste* with an object — *waste time*, *waste an hour*, *waste the day* — or *fritter away*, which adds the sense of losing it in small pieces rather than in one block.

### *While away* and its spelling

*While away* is built on the conjunction *while* and means to spend time pleasantly with nothing much at stake. The spelling ✗ *wile away* appears constantly, including in print — it comes from *wiles*, cunning tricks — and edited American prose still treats it as an error.

> We **whiled away** the afternoon watching the boats. — ✗ *We wiled away the afternoon.*

### How time itself moves

> The week **flew by**. &middot; *The last twenty minutes **crept by**.* &middot; *The years just **whiz by**.*

The subject is a stretch of time, not a person, and none of the three takes an object. Plain *drag* belongs here too: *The afternoon **dragged***.

## Getting around to things

The smallest group and the most idiomatic. These are about a date reaching you, or you finally reaching a task.

| Verb | Meaning | Separable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **get around to** | finally find the time to do something long deferred | no (+ noun / *-ing*) | *I finally **got around to** it.* &middot; *She never **got around to** calling.* |
| **put aside** | set something down for later, deliberately | yes | *Let's **put** that **aside** for now.* |
| **come around** | of a recurring date, arrive in its turn | — | *When the holidays **come around**, the store hires twenty people.* |
| **roll around** | the same, informally, of a day that comes back | — | *By the time Friday **rolls around**, nobody's listening.* |

### *Get around to* never takes an infinitive

The particle *to* here is a preposition, not the *to* of an infinitive, which means what follows it is a noun or an *-ing* form — never a bare verb. This is the most common single error with the phrase.

> ✗ *I never **got around to call** her.* → *I never **got around to calling** her.*  
> ✗ *He hasn't **got around to fix** it.* → *He hasn't **gotten around to fixing** it.*  
> *I'll **get around to** the taxes this weekend.* — a plain noun is fine

The same *to*-as-preposition trap catches *look forward to*, *be used to* and *object to*, and it is worked through in [Verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md). British English says *get round to*; American English says *get around to*, and *gotten* is the normal American participle here.

### *Come around* and *roll around*

Both describe a date arriving on its own schedule, with the date as the subject. *Roll around* is more informal and is restricted to recurring points — a weekday, a holiday, an anniversary.

> Every time the audit **comes around**, we find the same three problems.  
> When Monday **rolls around**, that inbox is going to be ugly.  
> ✗ *I came around the meeting.* → *The meeting **came around**.*

*Come around* also means to change your mind, to regain consciousness, and to visit. The time sense is the one with a date as subject.

## For Russian speakers

**1. «Отложить» is four different English verbs, and picking wrong changes the meaning.**

Russian covers with one prefixed verb what English splits by how long and how final the delay is.

| Russian | English | What it actually says |
|---|---|---|
| отложить встречу (на потом) | **put off** the meeting | postponed, no new date |
| отложить встречу на четверг | **push** the meeting **back** to Thursday | postponed, with a new date |
| отложить деньги / время | **set aside** money / time | reserved for a purpose — not a delay at all |
| отложить проект (надолго) | **shelve** the project | indefinitely, probably for good |
| отложить в сторону | **put** it **aside** | set it down to return to it |
| отложить решение | **hold off on** a decision / **defer** it | wait before acting / formal postponement |

> ✗ *I **put off** two hours for this.* → *I **set aside** two hours for this.*  
> ✗ *They **put off** the project forever.* → *They **shelved** the project.*

**2. «Успеть» has no English verb at all.**

This is the gap that produces the most un-English sentences, because the Russian verb is extremely common and every English equivalent is a different construction.

| Russian | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Я не успел. | *I didn't **have time**.* / *I didn't **get to** it.* | the general case |
| Я не успел на поезд. | *I **missed** the train.* | a departure — *miss*, not ✗ *didn't manage* |
| Успеешь в пятницу? | *Can you **make it** Friday?* | attendance |
| Мы успели вовремя. | *We **made it** in time.* / *We **were in time**.* | arrival |
| Я всё-таки успел ей позвонить. | *I finally **got around to** calling her.* | a long-deferred task |
| Успеть до дедлайна | ***beat the clock*** / *finish **in time*** | the margin |
| Я успеваю за ним. | *I can **keep up with** him.* | pace |

> ✗ *I didn't **succeed** to finish.* → *I didn't **have time** to finish.* / *I didn't **get** it **finished**.*  
> ✗ *Do you **manage** on Friday?* → *Can you **make it** on Friday?*

Note also **in time** versus **on time**: *in time* means with enough margin (*we got there in time to eat*), *on time* means at the appointed moment (*the train left on time*). Russian «вовремя» covers both.

**3. «Опаздывать» / «опоздать» splits by aspect, and English splits by verb.**

| Russian | English | Situation |
|---|---|---|
| Я опаздываю. | *I'm **running late**.* | right now, still on my way |
| Я опоздал. | *I **was late**.* / *I'm **late**.* | it already happened |
| Он вечно опаздывает. | *He's **always late**.* | a habit |
| Мы отстаём от графика. | *We're **running behind** schedule.* / *We've **fallen behind**.* | a schedule, not a person |
| Я опоздал на самолёт. | *I **missed** my flight.* | ✗ *I was late for my flight* is possible but weaker |

*Run late* describes a person in motion and is nearly always in the continuous: *I'm running late*, not ✗ *I run late*. *Be late* is the state on arrival.

**4. The morning vocabulary maps one to one — and learners still merge it.**

| Russian | English | Not |
|---|---|---|
| просыпаться / проснуться | **wake up** | ✗ *wake up from the bed* |
| вставать / встать | **get up** | — |
| поспать подольше | **sleep in** | ✗ *sleep more* |
| проспать (будильник) | **oversleep**, **sleep through** the alarm | ✗ *oversleep the alarm* |
| ложиться спать | **go to bed**, **turn in** | ✗ *go to sleep* means fall asleep |
| засыпать | **fall asleep**, **doze off**, **drift off** | — |
| не ложиться спать | **stay up** | ✗ *don't sleep* |

The pair that carries the most weight is *go to bed* versus *go to sleep*: the first is the act of getting into bed, the second is losing consciousness. *I went to bed at eleven and went to sleep at one* is a perfectly ordinary and rather sad sentence.

**5. Separability has no Russian analogue.**

Russian attaches its particles to the front of the verb as prefixes — от-ложить, за-снуть, до-гнать — where they cannot move. English particles are separate words that **can** move, and with a pronoun object they **must**: *put it off*, *wake me up*, *fit us in*, *pencil her in*. The instinct to keep the pair together is strong and produces ✗ *put off it*, ✗ *wake up me*, ✗ *fit in us* in otherwise fluent speech. The fix is mechanical: pronoun in the middle, always.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *Can you **put off it** until Monday?* | *Can you **put it off** until Monday?* | A pronoun object must go inside a separable phrasal verb. |
| ✗ *Let's **hold off** the decision.* | *Let's **hold off on** the decision.* | *Hold off* takes *on* + noun, or a bare *-ing*. |
| ✗ *They **put** the meeting **back** to Friday.* | *They **pushed** the meeting **back** to Friday.* | *Put back* for postponement is British; American keeps it for clocks. |
| ✗ *The board **tabled** it, so we'll discuss it Thursday.* | *The board **tabled** it, so it's off the agenda.* | In American English *table* means set aside, not bring up. |
| ✗ *We **moved back** the wedding to June — it's sooner now.* | *We **moved up** the wedding to June.* | *Move up* = earlier, *move back* = later. |
| ✗ *We **set off** the trip at six.* | *We **set off** at six.* / *We **set out** at six.* | *Set off* for a journey is intransitive. |
| ✗ *They **got under way** the meeting.* | *They **got** the meeting **under way**.* | The object goes before *under way*, or the verb goes intransitive. |
| ✗ *We **run out** time.* | *We **ran out of** time.* | *Run out of* is inseparable and needs *of* before a noun. |
| ✗ *The time **is finished**.* | ***Time's up.*** | English announces an expired period with *be up*. |
| ✗ *She **went on to talk** for another hour.* | *She **went on talking** for another hour.* | *Go on* + *-ing* continues; *go on* + *to* moves to the next thing. |
| ✗ *I **wore on** my work all afternoon.* | *I **worked on** it all afternoon.* | Only time *wears on*, and only intransitively. |
| ✗ *I need to **catch up with** my email.* | *I need to **catch up on** my email.* | *On* for a backlog, *with* for a person. |
| ✗ *We **made up for** the time in the air.* | *We **made up** the time in the air.* | *Make up time* is literal recovery; *make up for* is compensation. |
| ✗ *Can the dentist **fit in me** tomorrow?* | *Can the dentist **fit me in** tomorrow?* | Separable verb, pronoun object, middle position. |
| ✗ *I **wake up** from bed at seven.* | *I **get up** at seven.* | *Wake up* is opening your eyes; *get up* is leaving the bed. |
| ✗ *I **overslept** my alarm.* | *I **slept through** my alarm.* / *I **overslept**.* | *Oversleep* takes no object. |
| ✗ *I **wasted away** the afternoon.* | *I **wasted** the afternoon.* | *Waste away* means to grow thin from illness. |
| ✗ *We **wiled away** the evening.* | *We **whiled away** the evening.* | The verb is built on *while*, not *wiles*. |
| ✗ *I never **got around to call** her.* | *I never **got around to calling** her.* | The *to* is a preposition, so a gerund follows. |
| ✗ *I **run late** this morning.* | *I'm **running late** this morning.* | *Run late* is a continuous-tense description of a person in motion. |
| ✗ *Did you **manage** on Friday?* | *Can you **make it** on Friday?* | «Успеть» about attendance is *make it*. |
| ✗ *The train left **in time**.* | *The train left **on time**.* | *On time* = punctually; *in time* = with enough margin. |

## Quick reference

**The five most confusable pairs.**

> ***put off*** *= postpone with no new date* &middot; ***push back*** *= postpone to a stated time*  
> ***wake up*** *= stop sleeping* &middot; ***get up*** *= leave the bed*  
> ***run over*** *= the meeting used too much time* &middot; ***run late*** *= the person will arrive late*  
> ***catch up on*** *= a backlog of work* &middot; ***catch up with*** *= a person*  
> ***kill time*** *= waiting for something* &middot; ***pass the time*** *= filling it well*

**Which delay verb.**

```
   no new date, alive            put off
   new date given                push back
   don't act yet                 hold off on
   formal, a payment or a term   defer
   off the agenda (American)     table
   indefinitely, probably dead   shelve
   somebody is blocking it       hold up
   somebody is padding it        drag out
   somebody received it
     and did nothing             sit on
```

**Inseparable — the object never goes in the middle.**

*sit on* &middot; *hold off on* &middot; *run out of* &middot; *catch up on / with* &middot; *keep up with* &middot; *fall behind on* &middot; *get behind on* &middot; *gain on* &middot; *make up for* &middot; *sleep through* &middot; *get around to* &middot; *check in with*

**Intransitive — no object at all.**

*set off* (journey) &middot; *set out* &middot; *get under way* &middot; *take off* &middot; *jump in* &middot; *dive in* &middot; *knock off* &middot; *clock in / out* &middot; *time out* &middot; *be up* &middot; *drag on* &middot; *stretch on* &middot; *run over* &middot; *wear on* &middot; *live on* &middot; *sleep in* &middot; *oversleep* &middot; *stay up* &middot; *turn in* (bedtime) &middot; *doze off* &middot; *nod off* &middot; *drift off* &middot; *show up* &middot; *turn up* &middot; *roll in* &middot; *get in* &middot; *run behind* &middot; *fly by* &middot; *creep by* &middot; *come around* &middot; *roll around*

**Fixed idioms — nothing moves.**

*call it a day* &middot; *call it a night* &middot; *run late* &middot; *make it* &middot; *cut it close* &middot; *beat the clock* &middot; *kill time* &middot; *pass the time* &middot; *lose ground* &middot; *hit the snooze*

**Three verbs whose two senses are told apart by the object:** *wind up* (no object = end up somewhere unplanned; with one = bring to a close), *turn in* (go to bed / submit), *get behind* (+ *on* = fall behind; + noun = support).
