Dates, days, and years#

Every way American English names a point on the calendar — the capitalized day and month names, the month/day/year order the rest of the world reads backward, the digits you write and the ordinals you say, and the years read in halves.

← Time · All grammar topics

A date is the one corner of English where the written form and the spoken form come apart completely. You write March 3 and say March third. You write 1985 and say nineteen eighty-five. You write 3/7/2026 and — if you are in the United States — you mean the seventh of March, while almost everyone else on the planet reads those same nine characters as the third of July.

So this section has two jobs. What to write is convention, and the American conventions differ from the Russian ones in three ways: the day and month names take capital letters, the month comes before the day, and the year stands alone with no noun after it. What to say follows rules the writing does not show — the digit 3 in a date is read as the ordinal third, never as three, and the four digits of a year are read as two two-digit numbers. Nothing on the page announces either rule; you have to bring it with you.

Here is the idea underneath everything below:

A calendar expression is a name, and English capitalizes the names and lowercases the categories. Monday and March and Christmas are names, so they are capitalized every time, in every position. Spring, century, week, month, year and weekend are categories, so they are not. Russian treats понедельник and март as categories too, which is why the capital letter is the first thing to fix and the last thing to stop forgetting.

The prepositions that go in front of all this — on, in, at, by, through, until — are section 3 and the preposition catalog; the clock is section 1. This section is the calendar.

Three rules ride along with the table.

  • The period belongs to the prose form. Mon., Tues., Thurs. take a period; the three-letter forms in a table, a calendar grid or a data file do not: Mon Tue Wed Thu Fri Sat Sun.
  • Tues. and Thurs. are the American preference; Tue. and Thu. are the computing forms. And abbreviations do not belong in a sentence — they are for tables, schedules and notes: ✗ I'll see you on Mon. → on Monday.

American class schedules use a compressed code worth recognizing: MWF is Monday/Wednesday/Friday, TR or TTh is Tuesday/Thursday. R stands in for Thursday because T is already spoken for.

Dropping the preposition is American. I'll call you Monday, We're closed Sundays, The mayor resigned Tuesday — all completely ordinary here, and all impossible in British English, which requires on. It is the house style of American journalism, where nearly every dateline sentence does it.

The day name carries no time of its own. The tense does.

I saw him on Monday. — the Monday just past I'll see him on Monday. — the Monday coming I see him on Mondays. — every week

That is why the bare plural and the bare singular can look identical in a headline and still be read correctly: the verb settles it.

The first rule is a grammatical one: this, next, last and every take no preposition. They are determiners, and they replace on rather than following it.

✗ on next Monday → next Monday ✗ in last March → last March ✗ on every Friday → every Friday ✗ on yesterday → yesterday

And then the honest part about next. Suppose today is Tuesday the 3rd. Next Friday means either Friday the 6th — the next Friday to come — or Friday the 13th, the Friday belonging to next week. Both readings are live in American English, neither is wrong, and native speakers get this wrong with each other constantly. The same ambiguity runs through last Friday on a Monday or Tuesday, though less badly.

There is no rule that resolves it, and any reference that gives you one is inventing it. What speakers actually do is repair it on the spot, and a learner should do the same:

Next Friday — the thirteenth, not this week. · Not this Friday, the one after. A week from Friday. · Let's say Friday the 6th.

When it matters, say the date. That one habit removes the whole problem, and it is what careful Americans do without thinking about it.

Monday through Friday#

American English uses through to make the last day count. This is the one place where American writing deliberately spends an extra word to buy precision, and it is worth adopting.

We're open Monday through Friday. — five days, Friday included The office is closed December 24 through January 2. — January 2 is closed too The exhibit runs March 3 through May 18.

Monday to Friday is used in American English too, and nobody will misunderstand a work schedule. But to leaves the endpoint genuinely open — British writing has to add inclusive to get what through gives for free — so in anything that matters, use through. There is a fuller treatment in through and the British forms table.

In a table or a sign, the en dash does the same work: Mon–Fri, Mar 3–May 18. What you cannot do is mix the two systems:

✗ from Monday–Friday → from Monday to Friday / Monday through Friday / Mon–Fri

Americans learn the lengths from a rhyme — Thirty days hath September, April, June, and November; all the rest have thirty-one — and February's extra day, added every fourth year except in centuries not divisible by 400, makes a leap year.

Mid- is a prefix, not a word. It cannot stand alone in front of a month: ✗ in mid March → in mid-March. Before a capitalized month the hyphen is required; before an ordinary noun it usually disappears — midweek, midmonth, midnight, midcentury (though mid-century is also standard).

Seasons: lowercase#

The four seasons are common nouns and take no capital letter.

spring · summer · fall · winter

Fall is the American word; autumn is available and sounds literary or formal. In British English the relationship is reversed.

I'm going back to school in the fall. · It rains here in the spring. We met in the summer of 1997. Last winter was brutal. — no preposition, as always with last a spring morning · fall weather · summer clothes — attributive, still lowercase

In the spring and in spring are both correct. The article is the more common choice in American English and is the safer default for a Russian speaker, who will otherwise drop it everywhere.

A season is capitalized only when it is part of an actual name: the Spring 2026 semester, the Summer Olympics, the Winter Palace. And in poetry, where a personified season is a character. Everywhere else: ✗ in the Spring → in the spring.

Ordinals in dates#

Write the digit, say the word#

This is the rule the page does not tell you. The number in a date is an ordinal, even when it is written as a bare cardinal digit.

March 3 → say March third July 4 → say July fourth the 22nd → say the twenty-second ✗ March three · ✗ July four

There is no exception. If a digit is a date, it is read as an ordinal.

The suffix#

-th on everything, except -st after 1, -nd after 2, and -rd after 3 — and except 11, 12 and 13, which take -th despite their last digits.

-st after 1 — 1st, 21st, 31st · -nd after 2 — 2nd, 22nd · -rd after 3 — 3rd, 23rd -th everywhere else, including 11th, 12th, 13th, which ignore their last digits

Two patterns cover nearly all of it. A word ending in -y changes to -ieth (twenty → twentieth, thirty → thirtieth), the same -y → -ie rule that runs city → cities and study → studies. And in a compound, only the final element takes the ordinal ending: twenty-first, not ✗ twentieth-first. The full ordinal system, past the end of the month, is in numerals and ordinals.

Saying the hard ones#

Four ordinals end in consonant clusters that English barely tolerates, and every American simplifies them in ordinary speech.

fifth /fɪfθ/ → /fɪθ/ · sixth /sɪksθ/ → /sɪkst/ · eighth /eɪtθ/ → /eɪθ/ · twelfth /twɛlfθ/ → /twɛlθ/

The reduced forms are standard spoken American English, not errors. What matters is that the spelling keeps every letter: fifth, sixth, eighth, twelfth.

The one rule that surprises people: in edited American prose the suffix comes off when the month is present. March 3, not March 3rd. The ordinal is still what you say; it is just not what you write. When the month is absent, the suffix returns, because See you on the 3 is not a thing anyone writes.

Dates, the American way#

The order#

Month, day, year. That is the American sequence, and it is the one reversal a Russian speaker has to make deliberately every time until it stops costing anything.

March 7, 2026 · March 7 · Mar. 7, 2026 3/7/2026 · 3/7/26 · Tuesday, March 7, 2026

The 3/7/2026 problem#

An all-numeral date is not international. The same string means two different days depending on who is reading it, and there is nothing in the string to tell you which.

WrittenAn American readsNearly everyone else reads
3/7/2026March 73 July
7/3/2026July 37 March
1/12/2026January 121 December
12/1/2026December 112 January

The pattern is worth stating exactly: an all-numeral date is ambiguous whenever both numbers are 12 or lower, which is 144 of the 365 days in a year. When one number is above 12 the date solves itself, because only one reading is possible. That is a lot of days to leave to luck, and it is why every style guide that touches an international audience says to spell the month.

The forms that cannot be misread#

FormExampleWhere it belongs
Month spelled outMarch 7, 2026the general-purpose fix
Month abbreviatedMar. 7, 2026tables, footnotes, tight columns
Day first, month spelled7 March 2026military, scientific, international
ISO 86012026-03-07filenames, databases, anything that sorts

ISO 8601 is the form to know even though nobody says it aloud. Four-digit year, two-digit month, two-digit day, hyphens, largest unit first: 2026-03-07. Because the units descend, a list of ISO dates sorts correctly as plain text, which is why filenames are written 2026-03-07-notes.md. Read aloud it becomes an ordinary date: March seventh, twenty twenty-six. The practical rule: if the reader might not be American, spell the month; if a machine will sort it, use ISO; the slash form is for Americans writing to Americans.

The comma rule#

Five cases, and they are all one idea: the comma exists to keep the year from colliding with the day.

PatternComma?Example

…and mid-sentenceyes, after it as wellThe lease began on March 7, 2026, and runs two years.

The error that survives longest is dropping the second comma. The year is a parenthetical, and a parenthetical is closed as well as opened:

✗ On March 7, 2026 we signed the lease. ✓ On March 7, 2026, we signed the lease.

And the mirror error, a comma where the day is missing:

✗ March, 2026 was unusually warm. → March 2026 was unusually warm.

Ranges#

The exhibit runs March 3–May 18. Closed December 24 through January 2. March 3–7 · 2026–2028 · the 2025–26 school year ✗ from March 3–7 → from March 3 to 7

The dash is an en dash (–), not a hyphen, and it means to. Mixing it with from produces a sentence with two openings and one close, which is why from March 3–7 is wrong in every style guide. Either write out both words or use the dash alone.

Years#

The halves rule#

Split the four digits into two pairs and read each pair as a number. That is the default for every year in English, and it accounts for most of the language's history.

   1066   →   ten | sixty-six          ten sixty-six
   1492   →   fourteen | ninety-two    fourteen ninety-two
   1776   →   seventeen | seventy-six  seventeen seventy-six
   1985   →   nineteen | eighty-five   nineteen eighty-five
   2026   →   twenty | twenty-six      twenty twenty-six

The 2000s wrinkle is recent enough to be worth explaining. From 2000 through 2009, Americans said two thousand nine rather than twenty oh nine, because the oh forms sounded stilted. Starting in 2010 the ordinary halves pattern reasserted itself — twenty ten, twenty twelve, twenty twenty — and it has held ever since. Today twenty twenty-six and two thousand twenty-six are both correct; the first is far more common in speech, the second is the one a lawyer reads out loud.

And is the British half of this. British English says two thousand and five; Americans usually drop it — two thousand five — exactly as they drop it in two hundred five. Either is understood; the bare one is the American default.

Years in sentences#

She was born in 1985. In 2026 the rules change. the 1985 season · a 1985 Buick — attributive, no preposition by 2030 · since 1985 · from 1985 to 1992 · March 2026 — no comma the year 1985 · in the year 2000 — for emphasis or clarity

✗ in 1985 year → in 1985

That last one is the Russian «в 1985 году» carried across intact. English has no noun after the year and no ordinal in it: the year is a plain cardinal number standing alone.

Decades#

Five written forms, all standard:

FormExampleNote
spelled outthe eightieslowercase is the default
apostrophe + two digitsthe '80sthe apostrophe replaces the dropped 19
four digitsthe 1980sno apostrophe anywhere

The apostrophe rule, which is the whole reason this is in a grammar book. An apostrophe marks an omission or a possessive. It never makes a plural.

✓ the 1980s — the plural of 1980 ✗ the 1980's — that is a possessive, and there is nothing being possessed ✓ the '80s — the apostrophe stands in for the missing 19, then s makes the plural ✗ the 80s — the years are 1980–1989, so something is missing and must be marked ✗ the 80's — both errors at once ✓ the '80s' biggest hits — now the second apostrophe is a genuine possessive

One typographic trap: the mark before 80 is an apostrophe (') and not an opening quotation mark. Every word processor guesses wrong and produces ‘80s, and it has to be fixed by hand or by typing the character twice and deleting the first.

Subdivisions: the early eighties, the mid-eighties, the late eighties, the turn of the decade. And two decades have naming problems. The 1900s is ambiguous — it means either the ten years 1900–1909 or the whole century — so if you mean the decade, say the first decade of the twentieth century. And 2000–2009 has no settled name: the 2000s, the two-thousands, the aughts and the oughts are all in use and none of them won.

Centuries, and why the number is off by one#

The twentieth century is the 1900s. The century number is always one higher than the first two digits of the year, and that is a permanent, structural offset rather than a quirk.

Why
there is no year zero. The count starts at AD 1, so the first century ran from AD 1 through AD 100; the second from 101 through 200; and the twentieth from 1901 through 2000. The twenty-first began on January 1, 2001 — which is the argument everybody had at the end of 1999, and the pedants were right and lost anyway.
YearsCenturyStrictly
1–100the first century1–100
1400sthe fifteenth century1401–1500
1700sthe eighteenth century1701–1800

In ordinary use people ignore the strict column and treat the twentieth century as 1900–1999. For a learner the working rule is the arithmetic: take the first two digits of the year and add one. 1776 → 17 + 1 = the eighteenth century.

Writing them. The twentieth century spelled out and lowercase is the Chicago preference; the 20th century is AP style and is normal everywhere. Both are fine; consistency is not optional. And the hyphen appears only when the century modifies a noun:

twentieth-century architecture · a 19th-century novel — hyphenated, attributive the architecture of the twentieth century — no hyphen, it is a noun here mid-century modern · turn-of-the-century houses

The turn of the century is now ambiguous — 1900 or 2000 — so name the year when it matters. Above the century sits the millennium: the second millennium was 1001–2000, the third is 2001–3000, and the turn of the millennium means 2000.

AD comes first because it is a Latin prepositional phrase. AD 1066 reads as "in the year of the Lord 1066," and putting the phrase after its object is like writing 1066 in the year of the Lord. In practice 1066 AD is everywhere and most editors now accept it, but AD 1066 is the form nobody will correct.

With a century the Latin logic collapses, because anno Domini modifies a year and not a hundred-year stretch. So the label moves to the back:

the second century AD — ✓ ✗ the AD second century

BCE and CE are the religiously neutral pair, standard in academic, archaeological and museum writing. They map exactly onto the older pair — 1 BCE is 1 BC, 1066 CE is AD 1066 — so nothing is renumbered. What you cannot do is mix the systems in one document.

Three more points:

  • Periods. B.C., A.D., B.C.E. and C.E. are the older punctuated forms; AP still uses them, Chicago prefers the bare capitals. Choose one.
  • BC counts backward. 100 BC is earlier than 44 BC, and the fifth century BC runs from 500 to 401 BC. Everything in BC is upside down relative to intuition.
  • CE is usually left off for years anyone would assume are recent — 1066 CE in an ancient-history context, but just 1985 everywhere else.
  • An approximate year takes c., ca. or circa, read aloud as circa: c. 1450, around 1450, and c. 1450–1460 for a range of uncertainty.

The pair worth separating is by and until. They are not interchangeable, and the difference is what kind of verb follows.

As of points in two directions, which confuses people. As of March 1 can mean "starting on March 1" (As of March 1, parking is $5) or "measured at March 1" (As of March 1, we had 400 subscribers). Context settles it, and it always does.

In contracts and notices the date is load-bearing, and the language gets correspondingly explicit:

This agreement is effective March 1, 2026, and terminates on December 31, 2026. Payment is due on or before the third day of each month. Applications will be accepted from March 1 through March 31, inclusive.

Every one of these prepositions has a full entry in prepositions of time — see as of, effective, no later than and through.

Holidays and recurring dates#

The preposition depends on whether you mean the day itself, the season around it, or the stretch you spent inside it.

PatternExampleMeans
on + a named dayon Christmas Day, on Thanksgiving, on New Year's Daythat one day
at + a holiday seasonat Christmas, at Easter, at New Year'sthe general period
over + a multi-day stretchover Thanksgiving, over Christmas, over the holidaysacross the span
for + the occasionfor Christmas, for my birthdaythe reason, not the time
duringduring Ramadan, during Lentthroughout

Americans say on Thanksgiving far more often than at Thanksgiving; at Christmas is the one that survives as a season. And the holidays, lowercase and plural, means the late-December stretch and nothing else: What are you doing over the holidays?

The Fourth of July is the one date Americans habitually say day-first — a fossil, and the only living trace of the British order in American speech. The Fourth of July, July Fourth, the 4th of July, or Independence Day; also on the Fourth, over the Fourth, and the Fourth of July weekend. The capital F belongs to the holiday. Written lowercase, the fourth of July is just a date on a calendar.

Movable holidays are named by their rule, and the rule is how Americans state them: Thanksgiving is the fourth Thursday in November. Memorial Day is the last Monday in May. Labor Day is the first Monday in September. In November and of November are both fine there.

Weekends. On the weekend is American; ✗ at the weekend is British and should not be written here. Over the weekend covers the whole stretch and is the most common of all. This / last / next weekend take no preposition, and on weekends or bare weekends is the habitual form.

What are you doing on the weekend? · We drove up over the weekend. I work weekends. · He's away next weekend.

Birthdays and anniversaries take on for the day and for for the occasion: on my birthday, for my birthday, my thirtieth birthday, our tenth anniversary, on the anniversary of the fire. And the verb is turn: She turns thirty on Friday.

Holiday names are proper nouns and take capitals throughout: Christmas, Christmas Eve, Thanksgiving, Halloween, New Year's Eve, New Year's Day, the Fourth of July, Memorial Day, Labor Day, Presidents' Day, Veterans Day, Easter, Passover, Hanukkah, Ramadan, Eid. Watch the apostrophes, which are inconsistent by tradition rather than by rule: New Year's Day, Mother's Day and Father's Day are singular possessives; Presidents' Day is plural (and often written Presidents Day); Veterans Day has no apostrophe at all.

Reading a date aloud#

In a full sentence, nothing is added and nothing is skipped — the written form is simply pronounced by the rules above.

The contract was signed on March 3, 2026. — "on March third, twenty twenty-six" She was born on July 4, 1985. — "on July fourth, nineteen eighty-five" The meeting is Tuesday, March 3. — "Tuesday, March third" The photo is dated 10/12/99. — "October twelfth, ninety-nine"

Formal and legal English keeps the long forms, because a date read aloud in a courtroom or written on a deed has to be unmistakable and hard to alter.

this 3rd day of March, 2026 — "this third day of March, two thousand twenty-six" the third day of March in the year two thousand twenty-six Dated as of March 1, 2026.

Note that legal reading takes two thousand twenty-six, not twenty twenty-six. The longer form has no possible second parse, and that is the whole point of it.

Two practical habits. Over the phone, spell the month — "March, M as in Mary" — because March and May collapse into each other on a bad line. And for a year that must be exact, read the digits: "two zero two six."

One convention from American broadcast and wire journalism, which AP style makes explicit: within a week either side of today, use the day of the week; beyond that, use the date. The mayor resigned Tuesday for something three days old; The mayor resigned on March 3 for something three months old.

For Russian speakers#

Seven interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

1. Capital letters. Russian lowercases both «понедельник» and «март». English capitalizes both, everywhere, including in the middle of a sentence and in an attributive phrase.

✗ on monday in march → on Monday in March

Capitalized in English: days, months, holidays, their abbreviations. Not capitalized: seasons (spring, fall), and the words century, decade, year, month, week, weekend.

2. The order is reversed, and the separator changes. Russian writes «07.03.2026» for 7 March. American English writes 3/7/2026 for March 7. Same six digits, different day, and the periods become slashes.

✗ 7.3.2026 in an American document will be read as July 3 by a machine and as nonsense by a person. When in doubt, spell the month.

One line to carry: at a point, on a day, in anything longer. And no preposition at all after this, next, last, every, tomorrow or yesterday: ✗ on next Monday, ✗ in last March, ✗ on yesterday.

4. The genitive date. Russian puts the ordinal in the genitive and needs no preposition — «третьего марта». English needs a word in front of it, and offers two orders:

«Третьего марта» → (on) March third — written March 3 → (on) the third of March — written the 3rd of March, formal

And «3 марта» reverses: March 3. The question and answer reverse too. Russian asks «Какое сегодня число?» and answers with a bare genitive; English asks What's the date? and answers It's the third or It's March third.

5. A year in English is a cardinal number with nothing attached. Russian says «в тысяча девятьсот восемьдесят пятом году» — one enormous ordinal ending in a noun. English has neither the ordinal nor the noun, and splits the number in half:

✗ in 1985 year · ✗ in the 1985th · ✗ in 1985-th → in 1985, said nineteen eighty-five ✗ March 3, 2026 year → March 3, 2026

6. Articles, which Russian does not have and dates are full of. The names take none; the descriptions take the.

No article
in March · on Monday · in 1985 · at Christmas · in spring

The: in the spring · in the eighties · the third of March · on the 3rd · the twentieth century · on the weekend · the Fourth of July · the first Monday in September

7. «с … по …» is through, not to. Russian «с понедельника по пятницу» includes Friday. The English form that keeps the inclusion is Monday through Friday; Monday to Friday leaves it open. Same for dates: «с 3 по 7 марта» → March 3 through 7.

Six rules that prevent almost every error.

  1. Capitalize days, months and holidays. Lowercase seasons.
  2. Month, day, year — and spell the month out whenever a non-American might read it.
  3. Write the digit, say the ordinal. March 3 is March third.
  4. Split a year in half and read each half as a number — except the two thousand years.
  5. No apostrophe in a plural: the 1980s, the '80s.
  6. The century number is the year's first two digits plus one.

Practice#

  1. Capitalize: we're closed on monday and tuesday in july, and all of march.
  2. Say aloud: March 3, 2026 · July 4, 1776 · the 22nd.
  3. Say aloud: 1066, 1900, 1905, 2000, 2007, 2010, 2026.
  4. Rewrite in American form: 7 March 2026.
  5. What does 3/7/2026 mean to an American? To a Londoner?
  6. Fix: The lease begins on March 1, 2026 and runs one year.
  7. Fix: I grew up on music from the 1990's.
  8. Fix: We're open Monday to Friday. (Friday has to be included.)
  9. Fix: See you on next Friday.
  10. Spell out the ordinal: 2, 5, 8, 9, 12, 19, 20, 21, 30.
  11. Which century is 1776 in? Which is 2026?
  12. Place the era label: 44 ( BC ), 1066 ( AD ), the second century ( AD ).
  13. Fill in the preposition, or leave it blank: ___ Monday · ___ March · ___ 1985 · ___ the spring · ___ Christmas Day · ___ the weekend · ___ next Tuesday.
  14. Fix: In 1985 year I was born in march.
  15. What is ambiguous about next Friday, and how would you repair it?
  16. Fix: March, 2026 was unusually warm.
  17. Rewrite as ISO 8601: December 1, 2026.
  18. Fix: The exhibit runs from March 3–May 18.
