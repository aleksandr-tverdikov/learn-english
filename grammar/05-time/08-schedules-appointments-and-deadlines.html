Schedules, appointments and deadlines#

The working adult's half of the time topic — making a time, confirming it, moving it, killing it, and the deadline vocabulary that runs from EOD to grace period.

← Time · All grammar topics

Everything else in this topic tells you how to name a moment. This section is about negotiating one. It is the part of the language a working adult uses most and the part a course usually skips, because it is made of collocations rather than rules: you cannot derive pencil me in from the grammar of pencil, and nothing about push back tells you which direction it pushes.

Three facts shape the whole section.

American scheduling language is blunt. Does Tuesday work for you? is not casual or rude; it is the neutral, professional way to ask, and it is what a vice president writes to a vice president. Russian politeness formulas — «Не могли бы вы сообщить, когда вам было бы удобно?» — translate into English that sounds either foreign or faintly sarcastic. The American move is to propose a specific time and let the other person counter. Vagueness is the discourtesy here, not directness.

The vocabulary comes in opposed pairs that are easy to invert. Push back and move up point in opposite directions; by and until attach to opposite kinds of verb; hard and soft deadlines differ in whether missing one costs anything; on the clock and off the clock decide whether you are being paid. Each pair is one word apart and produces a real-world consequence when reversed — a meeting at the wrong hour, work delivered a week late.

Most of it is written, not spoken. Meetings are arranged in email and calendar invitations, which have their own compressed register: no greeting flourishes, times in a fixed format, abbreviations that appear nowhere else in English. Three model texts at the end show what that looks like on the page.

The words for the thing itself#

Schedule, and how to say it#

The first problem with schedule is the pronunciation, and it is the one word in this entire topic where American and British English simply disagree.

PronunciationSpoken as
American/ˈskɛdʒuːl/SKEJ-ool
British/ˈʃɛdjuːl/SHED-yool

Say SKEJ-ool in the United States. It is not a regional variant or an informal one: it is the only pronunciation most Americans have ever heard, and SHED-yool will be noticed every time. The derived words follow: rescheduling is /riˈskɛdʒuːlɪŋ/, scheduler is /ˈskɛdʒulər/.

Schedule is also a verb, and a very common one: We scheduled it for Thursday. She's scheduled to present at two. I'll get it scheduled today. Note the preposition — you schedule something for a time, never ✗ on a time.

Can we schedule something for next week? The surgery is scheduled for the ninth. · He's scheduled to land at 6:40. Nothing on my schedule moves before Thursday. We're two weeks behind schedule, and the train is running ahead of schedule, which never happens.

On schedule, behind schedule and ahead of schedule take no article. On time is the everyday equivalent; the difference between on time and in time is in Idioms and set phrases.

Five nouns that Russian tends to cover with «расписание», «график» and «план», and that English keeps strictly apart.

Can you send me the agenda before the call? — not ✗ the schedule, if you mean the topics Our itinerary has us in Santa Fe by Thursday night. What's the timeline on the rollout? — phases and dates, not a clock time It's on my calendar. · My calendar is a disaster this week.

Two traps. An agenda is not a schedule: an agenda without times is perfectly normal (1. Budget. 2. Hiring. 3. Anything else.). And agenda has a second, political sense — a hidden purpose — so What's your agenda? asked of a person is an accusation, while What's the agenda? asked of a meeting is a normal question.

Back-to-back and double-booked#

I'm back-to-back until four. — meetings with no gap between them I'm double-booked at two. — two things claim the same slot, and one has to go I'm booked solid through Thursday. · I'm slammed. · I'm swamped.

These are not the same state. Back-to-back is about density and is nobody's fault; double-booked is a conflict that has to be resolved, and admitting it is the normal way to ask somebody to move: I'm double-booked at two — any chance we could do three?

Making an appointment#

Asking whether somebody is free#

From most casual to most formal. The middle of this list is where most working email lives.

RegisterQuestion
CasualAre you around Tuesday? · Free Thursday? · You got a minute?
Neutral spokenAre you free on Tuesday? · Do you have time this week? · What does your week look like?
Neutral writtenDoes Tuesday work for you? · Are you available Tuesday afternoon? · When works for you?
FormalWould you be available on Tuesday the 14th? · Please let me know a time that works for you.
Very formalI am writing to request an appointment to discuss… · Kindly advise when it would be convenient.

When works for you? is a complete, ordinary question, subject and all — not a fragment, and not casual. Note also the preposition and its absence. Free on Tuesday, free at three, free in the morning — but American English deletes on freely before a bare weekday: Are you free Tuesday? is completely standard. What is never possible is ✗ free at Tuesday. The whole system is in Prepositions of time.

Proposing a specific time#

Asking about availability in the abstract usually produces a slow answer. Proposing a time produces a fast one, and that is the American default.

Does 3 p.m. work for you? How does Thursday at two sound? Would Tuesday at ten be okay? · Can we say Thursday? How about Friday morning? · What about the 14th? I was thinking Tuesday afternoon, if that's not terrible for you.

Work for you is the workhorse phrase of the whole section, and it takes a time as its subject: Does Tuesday work? Does two work? Would next week work better? Its negative is equally standard: Tuesday doesn't work for me — Wednesday does. How does X sound? is warmer and slightly more casual, and it can take a whole plan as its subject: How does lunch on Thursday sound?

I'd like to make an appointment with Dr. Okafor. Can we set something up for next week? I'll book a room and shoot you an invite. Let me pencil you in for Thursday at two and we'll confirm Monday. I blocked off two to four so nobody could take it. Can you put it on my calendar?

Pencil in earns its own note. The metaphor is pencil against ink: a penciled appointment can be erased. Pencil me in means "yes, tentatively, but I am not promising," and Americans hear it as exactly that soft. Shoot me an invite — also shoot me an email, shoot me a text — is informal but entirely ordinary in American offices; it is the register of a Slack message, not risky slang.

Appointment or meeting?#

Russian «встреча» covers both, and choosing wrong is one of the most audible non-native touches in office English.

WordWho it is with
appointmenta professional whose time you book: doctor, dentist, lawyer, hairdresser, mechanic, the DMV
meetingcolleagues, clients, partners — two or more people with equal claim on the time
callany meeting held by phone or video, however many people
interview, consultationa job candidate or a journalist's subject; a paid first meeting with a specialist

✗ I have an appointment with my manager at three. → I have a meeting with my manager at three. ✗ I have a meeting with the dentist. → I have a dentist appointment. I have a doctor's appointment at four, so I'll be out.

Doctor's appointment, dentist appointment, hair appointment — the possessive sticks to doctor's and dentist's and drops elsewhere, and both are heard. Nobody will notice either way.

Saying yes, saying no, and saying "how about Thursday"#

Yes#

That works for me. — the default confirmation That works. · Works for me. · Two works. · Sounds good. · Perfect. Thursday it is. — a confirmation with a small flourish I'm free then. · I'm open at two. · Put me down for Thursday. See you then. — closes the exchange; nothing further is needed

That works for me is the one to learn first. It is neutral, it is professional, it cannot be too casual for any office, and it does not sound enthusiastic when you do not feel enthusiastic.

No, and the vocabulary of being busy#

A flat I can't is rare. The American convention is to name a reason, however thin, and immediately offer an alternative — and the reasons are almost all idiomatic.

PhraseLiterallyUsed for
I'm booked.my calendar is fullany conflict
I have a conflict.two things overlapthe neutral office word
I'm tied up.I am occupieda stretch of time you cannot leave
I'm slammed / swamped.overwhelmeda whole busy period
I'm back-to-back.no gapsa specific day
Something came up.an unforeseen thingcanceling, usually last-minute
I'm out that week. · I'm OOO Thursday.away, on leavevacation, travel; OOO is written only

I'm booked Tuesday, but Wednesday is wide open. I have a conflict at two — could we do three? I'm tied up until about four. Sorry, something came up and I have to push our one-on-one. I won't be able to make it — can we find another time?

Something came up is the standard, almost formulaic excuse for a late cancellation. It is vague on purpose, nobody asks what came up, and asking would be odd. What it should always be followed by is a replacement time; canceling without one is what turns an inconvenience into a slight.

Rain check#

An Americanism, common enough that a learner will meet it in the first month and opaque enough that nobody guesses it.

The origin is baseball. When a game was rained out, the stadium gave ticket holders a rain check — a stub good for admission to a later game. The phrase spread to retail (a rain check on a sale item that is out of stock, redeemable at the sale price when it comes back in) and from there into ordinary speech.

What it means now
no this time, yes another time — a decline that explicitly preserves the invitation.

Can I take a rain check? — the standard form I'll have to take a rain check on dinner — I'm buried this week. "Drinks after work?" "Rain check. I've got my kid tonight."

Two notes. It is social, not corporate — you take a rain check on drinks, a dinner, a weekend trip, not on a quarterly review. And it is spelled as two words in American usage, though raincheck appears and nobody objects.

Counter-proposing#

Declining and proposing are one move, not two, and the alternative comes in the same sentence.

Can we do Thursday instead? · Tuesday's tough — how about Wednesday? Would Friday be any better? · Same time Thursday? I can't do mornings. Any chance of an afternoon? That's cutting it close for me — could we say four instead of three?

Instead goes at the end of the clause: Can we do Thursday instead?, not ✗ Can we instead do Thursday? And Any chance…? is an extremely useful softener that takes either a noun phrase or an of + -ing: Any chance of a later slot? Any chance we could move it?

Changing the time#

The neutral verbs#

VerbDirectionRegisterNote

Can we reschedule for Friday? · They've rescheduled twice already. Let's move it to two. · We postponed the launch until spring. The flight was delayed three hours. I keep putting off that conversation. · They bumped our review to next month.

Reschedule takes for or to with a new time and needs no object repetition: Can we reschedule for Friday? is complete. ✗ Can we reschedule the meeting on Friday? mixes two patterns; write reschedule the meeting for Friday.

Postpone versus delay. Postpone is a decision somebody made about a scheduled event; delay is something being made late, usually by circumstances. A flight is delayed; a wedding is postponed. ✗ We delayed the meeting to Friday is odd — you moved or pushed it. And postpone takes until in careful writing (postponed until March), though postponed to March is common in speech. Full entries: work and business verbs, and Phrasal verbs of time for the rest.

Push back and move up: the pair that reverses#

This is the single most consequential pair in the section, because getting it backwards puts you in a room on the wrong day.

Push back = later. Can we push the meeting back to four? — it was at three Move up = earlier. Can we move the meeting up to two? — it was at three

The metaphor is a calendar page, where earlier is higher up and later is further along. Up is toward the top of the page — earlier. Back is against the direction of travel — later.

PhraseNew time isExample
push backlaterPushed back to Friday.
move backlaterMoved the call back an hour.
put off, bumplaterPut it off until next quarter. · Bumped to the 14th.
move upearlierMoved the deadline up a week.
bump up, advanceearlierThey bumped up the launch date.
move forwardambiguous — avoidcould be either

Two things to be careful about. First, move forward is genuinely ambiguous and native speakers disagree about it: some hear move the meeting forward as earlier (forward toward now), others as later (forward along the timeline). Americans argue about this in real offices, and the result is a missed meeting. Do not use it — say push it back, move it up, or, safest of all, give the new time: Let's move it to Thursday at two.

Second, the amount versus the destination. Both verbs take either.

Push it back an hour. — by one hour, to four · Push it back to four. — to that time Move it up two days. — two days earlier · Move it up to the 12th. — to that date

Confirming the actual clock time afterward is not fussy; it is standard practice, and the reason calendar invites exist.

✗ Can we push up the meeting to four? → Can we push back the meeting to four? — push up is not a scheduling verb at all ✗ They moved back the launch to an earlier date. → They moved up the launch.

They canceled the whole conference. · The game was called off because of lightning. Our plans fell through, so I'm free after all. We had three no-shows this morning. He stood her up. — a social, personal failure, not a corporate one Don't blow off the orientation; they take attendance.

American spelling keeps one l: canceled, canceling — but the noun is cancellation with two, everywhere in the world. Memorize the pair. And no-show is a noun, an adjective (a no-show fee) and now a verb (two people no-showed); clinics and restaurants charge for it, which is why it turns up on paperwork.

Deadlines#

The words#

WordMeaning

The deadline is Friday at five. · We missed the deadline by two hours. The report is due Friday. — due takes a bare date with no preposition When is it due? · It's due the 15th. · It's due by noon. The cutoff for registration is March 1.

The verbs that go with deadline are worth listing because they do not translate: you meet a deadline, make one, miss one, hit one, blow one (informal), set one, extend or push back one, and a deadline slips. You do not ✗ fulfill or ✗ realize one.

By, until, through, within#

The by / until contrast is worked out in general in Prepositions of time. In the deadline register it narrows to one rule, and the rule is about the verb.

A deadline is a boundary, so it takes by. A span of availability is a stretch, so it takes until.

PrepositionWhat it attaches toExample
bya completed action — a single event that must be done no later thanGet it to me by Friday.
until / tilla continuing state or action that stops at that pointYou have until Friday.
througha span including the last day (American)The offer runs through Friday.
withina stretch measured from nowWe'll respond within 24 hours.
no later than / as offormal by; a state beginning at a pointSubmit no later than 5:00 p.m. · As of Monday…

The confusing part is that both prepositions appear in perfectly ordinary deadline sentences — because they attach to different verbs:

The report is due by Friday. ✓ — due is a boundary You have until Friday to finish it. ✓ — have is a state that continues ✗ Finish it until Friday. → Finish it by Friday. ✗ You have by Friday. → You have until Friday. I'll be in meetings until three, so I'll get to it by four.

That last sentence is the whole rule in one line: be in meetings continues and takes until; get to it completes and takes by.

American through is inclusive. Monday through Friday includes Friday; the sale runs through the 15th includes the 15th. British English writes Monday to Friday, and for an American reader to the 15th is unclear about whether the 15th counts — which is why forms and contracts write through, or spell it out as to and including.

Hard and soft deadlines#

An American office distinction with no grammar behind it and real consequences attached to it.

TermWhat it means
hard deadlinemissing it has a consequence — money, a lost slot, a legal problem
soft deadlinea target; missing it is noticed but survivable
drop-dead datethe hardest possible deadline, usually contractual — while a hard stop is not a deadline at all, but the moment you must leave

Is the 15th a hard deadline or a soft one? The filing date is a hard deadline — the court doesn't take late submissions. We have a drop-dead date of June 1 in the contract.

Asking Is that a hard deadline? is not a challenge and is not rude. It is a standard, expected clarifying question, and an American manager will usually answer it honestly.

Can you get me the draft by EOD? I'll have it to you by COB Thursday. The invoice needs to go out by EOM. I'm OOO next week — ETA back in the office is the 14th.

EOD is genuinely ambiguous, and this causes real problems. In some offices it means the end of the workday — five or six in the evening. In others, especially where work crosses time zones, it means the end of the calendar day, midnight. The two readings are seven hours apart. If the difference matters, do not use it: write by 5 p.m. Thursday or by midnight Thursday. And if somebody else uses it, asking EOD as in 5, or as in midnight? is a normal question that marks you as careful rather than as a beginner.

ASAP has a register problem. It is genuinely urgent in some mouths and a verbal tic in others, and it gives the recipient nothing to plan around. A named time is always better — by 2 today instead of ASAP — and the safe reply to one names a time back: I can have it to you by 3, does that work? COB and EOB, meanwhile, assume a time zone, usually the recipient's; across zones, say it: by 5 p.m. Eastern. The zones are in Time zones and American conventions.

Running late versus late. I'm running late is said on the way, before you arrive, and it is the phrase to text. I'm late is said once you are there, or about a habit (he's always late). Both are correct English; only one is the right thing to send from the car. And overdue has a second, positive life as an idiom: This conversation is long overdue means it should have happened sooner and is welcome now — a compliment, not a complaint.

Duration at work#

The hyphen rule, which is not optional#

When a duration sits in front of a noun and modifies it, it becomes a compound adjective: hyphenated, and singular.

Before the noun (hyphen, singular)After the verb (no hyphen, plural)
a half-hour meetingThe meeting lasted half an hour.
a 30-minute slotThe slot is 30 minutes long.
a two-hour delayWe were delayed two hours.
a three-day extensionThey gave us three days.

✗ a 30-minutes meeting → a 30-minute meeting ✗ a two hours delay → a two-hour delay ✗ a three days weekend → a three-day weekend

The same word can be either: a half hour is a noun phrase (I waited a half hour), a half-hour a modifier (a half-hour show). Both half an hour and a half hour are standard American and mean the same thing, and an hour and a half is the usual way to say ninety minutes.

Blocking, running long, hard stops#

I'll block off an hour Thursday morning. · Can you block two to four on my calendar? The meeting ran long. — it went past its end time We ran over by twenty minutes. — the same, with an amount Let's try to wrap up on time today. · Let's wrap this up — I have another call. I have a hard stop at four. · Sorry, hard stop — I'll follow up by email.

Hard stop is the single most useful phrase in this section for a non-native speaker in American meetings. It means: I must leave at this time, it is not negotiable, and I am telling you now so nobody is offended later. Announcing it at the start (Heads up, I have a hard stop at four) is courteous, not abrupt — and once announced, leaving at four needs no apology.

Run long and run over both mean exceeding an allotted time, and they differ in what follows: run long stands alone, run over takes an amount or nothing. ✗ The meeting ran long by ten minutes is not idiomatic; use ran over. Wrap up means finish, transitive (wrap up the presentation) or not (let's wrap up), and wrap alone works in speech.

Call the after-hours line if it's an emergency. Try to go off hours — the line is an hour long at noon. I'm off the clock, but I'll take a look tonight anyway. They pay time and a half past forty hours, and we have flextime with core hours ten to three. He works the graveyard shift at the hospital. I'm on call this weekend, and I'm taking PTO the week of the 20th. It's a nine-to-five — decent money, no surprises.

Three notes an American would assume you already know. PTO is one pool: most American employers no longer separate vacation days from sick days, so I'm taking PTO is the neutral way to say you will be away, and giving no reason is not rude. Overtime is a legal category: non-exempt hourly employees are owed time and a half past forty hours, salaried exempt employees are not, so I worked a lot of overtime from a salaried person means "long hours," not "extra pay." And off the clock has a second sense — "unofficially, not for the record": Off the clock, what do you actually think of the proposal?

Three model texts#

These are what the register looks like on the page. Each is short on purpose; American work email is shorter than a Russian equivalent and puts the request in the first line.

1. A meeting invitation.

Subject: 30 min this week — Q3 numbers

Hi Marta,

Do you have 30 minutes this week to go over the Q3 numbers
before they go to the board? Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon
both work on my end — Tuesday at 2 would be ideal.

Let me know what works and I'll send an invite.

Thanks,
Ray

Notice what is missing: no apology for writing, no inquiry after the recipient's health, no closing formula longer than one word. The shape — duration, purpose, two options, one preference, a request to reply — is close to fixed.

2. A deadline reminder.

Subject: Reminder — expense reports due Friday 5 p.m.

Quick reminder that Q3 expense reports are due by 5 p.m.
Friday, October 3. This one is a hard deadline — anything
that comes in after Friday goes into the next quarter.

If you need an extension, email me before Thursday and
I'll see what I can do.

Thanks,
Priya

The pattern: what, by when in full, hard or soft, what happens if you miss it, how to ask for more time. Note by 5 p.m. Friday, October 3 — day name and date together, because one of them is always misread.

3. A reschedule email.

Subject: Re: Thursday 2 p.m. — need to move

Hi Dan,

Something came up Thursday afternoon and I need to push our
call back. Would Friday at 2 work, same length? If Friday is
bad, I'm fairly open Monday morning.

Sorry for the short notice — I'll send an updated invite as
soon as you confirm.

Ray

The pattern: a one-line reason, the verb that says which direction it moves, a specific alternative, a fallback, a short apology, who will send the new invite. The apology is one clause. Extending it past that reads as either anxious or insincere.

For Russian speakers#

1. «Записаться» fans out. Russian uses one reflexive verb for every act of putting your name in somebody's book; English picks a different verb depending on what is being booked.

RussianEnglishUsed for
записаться к врачу, на приёмmake an appointment, book an appointmenta professional's time
записаться на курсsign up for, enroll in, register fora class, a course
записаться в очередьget on the list, put my name downa waiting list
записаться в спортзалjoin (a gym), sign upa membership
записать что-то в календарьput it on the calendaryour own schedule

✗ I want to write myself to the doctor. → I want to make an appointment with the doctor. ✗ I recorded for the course. → I signed up for the course. ✗ Please write me for Tuesday. → Please put me down for Tuesday.

2. «Перенести» splits into two verbs that point in opposite directions. This is the error with the largest practical cost in the whole topic. Russian «перенести» is neutral about direction and lets «на» plus the new date supply it. English has no neutral common verb: push back means later and move up means earlier, and a learner who has met only one of them will use it for both.

RussianEnglishDirection
перенести на более поздний срок, отложитьpush back, postpone, put off, deferlater
перенести на более ранний срокmove up, bump upearlier
перенести (на другое время, нейтрально)reschedule, moveeither — needs a target
отменитьcancel, call offgone

✗ Can we transfer the meeting to Friday? → Can we move the meeting to Friday? ✗ They carried over the interview to Monday. → They pushed the interview back to Monday. ✗ We moved up the deadline to next month. → We pushed back the deadline to next month.

The reliable strategy when you are unsure: use reschedule and then name the new time. Can we reschedule for Friday at two? cannot be misunderstood in either direction, and it is perfectly good English at any level of formality.

3. «Срок» is three different English nouns.

RussianEnglishExample
срок (сдачи)deadline, due dateThe deadline is Friday.
срок (действия)term, period, validitya five-year term
сроки (проекта)timeline, time frameWhat's the timeline?
в срокon time, by the deadlineWe finished on time.
в кратчайшие сроки · продлить срокas soon as possible · get an extension

✗ What is the term of the report? → When is the report due? · ✗ We finished in term. → We finished on time.

4. The prepositions: «в» before a day, «до» before a deadline. «Во вторник» maps onto on Tuesday, but the at of «в три часа» leaks onto the day and produces the commonest error in this section. And «до пятницы» does two jobs with one word, where English splits them by what the verb is doing.

✗ I will be free at Tuesday. → I will be free on Tuesday. / I'll be free Tuesday. ✗ The meeting is in Monday. → The meeting is on Monday. ✗ Let's meet at the next week. → Let's meet next week. — no preposition at all ✗ Please send it until Friday. → Please send it by Friday. ✗ I'm busy by Friday. → I'm busy until Friday.

At takes a clock time, on takes a day or a date, in takes a month, season or year, and next, last, this and every take nothing (next week, last Friday, every Tuesday). The whole system, including the zero-preposition rule, is in Prepositions of time.

5. American scheduling language is more direct than Russian politeness leads you to expect.

Russian arranges a time through conditional and impersonal formulas — «Не могли бы вы сказать, когда вам было бы удобно?», «Если вас не затруднит…» — and translating them produces English too heavy for the situation. A native speaker reading Would you perhaps be so kind as to inform me when it might be convenient for you to meet? concludes one of three things: that you are being sarcastic, that something is wrong, or that English is not your first language.

Instead ofWrite
Would you be so kind as to inform me of your availability?When works for you?
I would be extremely grateful if you could find the time.Do you have 30 minutes this week?
If it is not difficult for you, could we possibly meet on Tuesday?Does Tuesday work?
I apologize profusely for the inconvenience caused.Sorry for the short notice.

Two transfers to watch. Do not stack softeners — Could you possibly maybe just see if perhaps Tuesday might work? is less polite in English, not more, because it makes the reader work. One is plenty: Any chance Tuesday works? And do not apologize more than once; the convention is a single short apology, then the fix.

6. Three calques worth naming.

✗ I will be on the meeting. → I'll be in a meeting. / I'll be at the meeting. ✗ I have no time this week. → I don't have time this week. — the first is grammatical but dramatic ✗ I didn't succeed to finish. → I didn't have time to finish. / I didn't get to it. — «успеть» has no single English verb; the everyday equivalents are make it, have time to, get to, and make the deadline

WrongRightWhy
✗ I'll be free at Tuesday.I'll be free on Tuesday.At takes a clock time; a day takes on, or nothing.
✗ Send it until Friday.Send it by Friday.A completed action takes by; until needs a continuing one.
✗ You have by noon.You have until noon.Have is a continuing state, so it takes until.
✗ We moved up the deadline to next month.We pushed back the deadline to next month.Move up means earlier; push back means later.

Quick reference#

The direction words, once more.

   EARLIER  ←────────────────────────────────→  LATER
   move up                                      push back / move back
   bump up                                      put off / postpone
   advance                                      bump / defer

                  reschedule / move  — neutral; name the new time
                  move forward       — ambiguous. Do not use it.

The deadline preposition, in one question.

   Does the verb describe something that FINISHES,
   or something that CONTINUES?

   FINISHES ──── by      Send it by Friday.  It's due by noon.
   CONTINUES ─── until   You have until Friday.  I'm tied up until noon.
   A SPAN ─────── through  The offer runs through Friday. (Friday included)

The five phrases to learn first.

Does Tuesday work for you? — the standard way to propose a time That works for me. — the standard way to accept one I have a conflict — can we do Thursday instead? — decline and counter in one line Can we push it back to four? — move it later, unambiguously I have a hard stop at four. — I am leaving then, and this is not rude

Abbreviations, shortest to longest span
ASAP · EOD · COB / EOB · EOW · EOM · EOQ. When the hours matter, write the hour: by 5 p.m. Thursday beats by EOD every time.
Register in one line
propose a specific time, give one reason if you decline, offer one alternative, apologize once.

Practice#

Fix, complete, or choose. Some items have more than one good answer.

  1. ✗ I'm free at Tuesday afternoon. — fix the preposition.
  2. The report is due ____ Friday, and you have ____ Friday at five to finish it. [by / until]
  3. The meeting was at three. They pushed it back an hour — and in another version moved it up an hour. Give both new times.
  4. ✗ Can we reschedule the call on Thursday? — fix the preposition.
  5. ✗ We had a two hours delay, and we need two weeks lead time. — fix both measure phrases.
  6. Your colleague writes: Can you get it to me by EOD? It matters whether that means 5 p.m. or midnight. What do you write back?
  7. Give the one-line American reply that declines Tuesday and proposes Thursday.
  8. ✗ Sorry, I have no time — I am late for the meeting, I will come in ten minutes. — rewrite it as a text message from the car.
  9. What is a rain check, and would you take one on a performance review?
  10. ✗ The presentation ran long by fifteen minutes. — fix the phrasal verb.
  11. Translate the intent: «Не могли бы вы сообщить, когда вам было бы удобно встретиться?»
  12. ✗ They cancelled the flight, and the cancelation cost us a day. — fix the spelling. Both words.
  13. A meeting invitation arrives for a time you cannot commit to but do not want to refuse. What do you reply?
  14. ✗ I recorded myself to the Spanish course. — fix the verb.
  15. What is the difference between a hard deadline and a hard stop?
  16. Name the direction: put off, bump up, defer, advance, postpone.
  17. ✗ I have an appointment with the marketing team at four. — fix the noun.
  18. You are chairing a meeting and must leave at four no matter what. What do you say at the start, and is it rude?

One more pass#

Write the three model emails again, changing only the times: a 45-minute invitation for Monday morning, a soft deadline reminder for the 20th, and a reschedule that moves a Wednesday call up to Tuesday. The third is the test — the verb has to be move up, and the confirmation has to name the new day and hour.
