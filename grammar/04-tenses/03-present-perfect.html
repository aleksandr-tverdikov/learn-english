The present perfect#

Have or has plus a past participle — a present tense that uses a past event to say something about now, and the one English form a Russian speaker has no instinct for at all.

← Tenses · All grammar topics

The present perfect is have or has followed by a past participle: I have finished, she has gone, they haven't called. The form is easy. The choice behind it is not, and it is the reason this is the section Russian speakers reread.

Here is the whole difficulty in one pair. Both sentences are correct English, and they are not interchangeable.

I lost my keys. I've lost my keys.

The first reports a past event. The second reports the present situation — I cannot get into my apartment. Nothing separates them in Russian: «Я потерял ключи» does both jobs, and the speaker never has to decide which one is meant. English makes you decide in every sentence. A learner who never noticed the decision defaults to the past simple everywhere and sounds, in a way that is hard to point at, slightly wrong.

This section covers the present perfect simple. The present perfect continuous — I've been waiting — is section 4, and the two are compared there rather than here.

The form#

The basic pattern#

Subject + have / has + past participle. The auxiliary agrees with the subject; the participle never changes for anything.

SubjectAuxiliaryParticipleFull form
I / you / we / theyhavefinishedI have finished.
he / she / ithasfinishedShe has finished.

There is nothing else to learn about the agreement. Has goes with exactly the subjects that take the third-person -s in the present simple, which is the same list as always: he, she, it, and any singular noun or noun phrase.

My brother has moved to Seattle. Everyone has left. The kids have eaten. Neither of them has called. — neither is singular

Affirmative#

I've finished the report. She's read all three books. We've lived here since 2019. They've already left. It's rained every day this week.

Negative#

The negative is not after the auxiliary: have not → haven't, has not → hasn't. There is no do-support anywhere in the present perfect — ✗ I don't have finished is not a possible English sentence.

I haven't finished the report. She hasn't read it yet. We haven't heard anything. He's not called me back. — the other contraction; see below

Both negative contractions exist here, exactly as they do with be: he hasn't called and he's not called. The -n't form is the American default by a wide margin, and 's not / 've not sounds faintly British in print. Either is standard.

Questions#

Invert the subject and the auxiliary. Again, no do.

Have you finished? Has she called yet? Have they left? How long have you lived here? How many times has he asked?

A wh-word that is itself the subject takes no inversion, because there is nothing to invert with:

Who has seen this? — not ✗ Who has this seen? What has happened?

Negative questions#

Haven't you finished yet? Hasn't she called you?

The contracted form is the ordinary one. The uncontracted version splits the not off and moves it after the subject, which makes it formal, indignant, or both: Have you not finished? The same split works in the other tenses and is covered in Contractions.

Short answers#

The short answer repeats the auxiliary. It does not repeat the participle, and it is never contracted in the affirmative.

QuestionYesNo
Have you eaten?Yes, I have.No, I haven't.
Has she called?Yes, she has.No, she hasn't.
Have they left?Yes, they have.No, they haven't.

✗ Yes, I've. — a contraction cannot end a clause ✗ Yes, I have finished. — grammatical, but it is the long answer, not the short one

That constraint is general: 've, 's, 'd, 'll, 're and 'm all need something after them to lean on. The rule is stated in full in Contractions.

In speech the auxiliary is almost always contracted, and in ordinary writing it usually is too.

The trap is 's. Written he's is either he is or he has, and nothing in the spelling tells you which.

The test is the word right after it. A past participle means has; anything else — an adjective, a noun, an -ing form, a preposition — means is.

The trap sharpens where the participle and the -ing form sound alike in a sentence, and it sharpens further with verbs whose participle doubles as an adjective: He's finished can mean he is finished (an adjective — he's done for) or he has finished (the tense). Context settles it and nothing else does. The full entry for each of these forms, with its pronunciation and its expansions, is in the contraction dictionary; the 's and 'd ambiguities are laid out together in Contractions.

Two further notes on how this is actually spoken:

  • After a noun, 've and 's are said but not written: The kids've eaten, My car's broken down. Write The kids have eaten.
  • ✗ I've went, ✗ should of, ✗ would of all come from the same place — a reduced auxiliary heard wrong. Have in I've is /əv/, which is exactly how of sounds. It is still have.

The past participle: regular#

For a regular verb the participle is the base plus -ed, and it is identical to the past simple. The spelling rules are the same ones:

RuleBaseParticiple
defaultwork, call, playworked, called, played
ends in -e — add -d onlylive, close, hopelived, closed, hoped
consonant + -y → -iedcarry, try, studycarried, tried, studied
one syllable, one vowel, one final consonant — double itstop, plan, dropstopped, planned, dropped
two syllables, stress on the second — double itprefer, admit, occurpreferred, admitted, occurred
two syllables, stress on the first — do not doubleoffer, visit, openoffered, visited, opened
final -l — American English does not doubletravel, cancel, labeltraveled, canceled, labeled

That last row is a real spelling difference and not a matter of taste: traveled, canceled, labeled, modeled, signaled are the American forms, and travelled, cancelled are British.

The ending is pronounced three ways, automatically, according to the sound before it — /t/ after a voiceless sound, /d/ after a voiced one, and /ɪd/ only after /t/ or /d/, where it adds a syllable. I've worked is /wɝkt/, I've called is /kɔld/, I've wanted is /ˈwɑntɪd/. The table is in Pronunciation.

The past participle: irregular#

About two hundred English verbs form the participle some other way, and they include nearly every verb you use in an hour of conversation. The single most productive error in this whole section is using the past simple as the participle.

✗ I have went to the bank. ✓ I have gone to the bank. ✗ I've saw that movie. ✓ I've seen that movie. ✗ She's took the car. ✓ She's taken the car. ✗ We've ate already. ✓ We've eaten already.

A verb with three distinct forms has a past simple and a participle, and after have only the participle is possible. The twenty that come up most:

The irregular verbs are grouped in the verb dictionary by how the three forms differ, which is a far better way to learn them than an alphabetical list. The groups that matter most for participles:

  • All three forms identical — cut, put, let, hit, cost — and past = participle — find / found / found, buy / bought / bought, say / said / said. Nothing to get wrong in either group, except read, where the spelling never changes and the pronunciation does: I've read it is /rɛd/.
  • Participle = base + -n, with a vowel change, and past in -o-, participle = past + -n — take / took / taken, speak / spoke / spoken, know / knew / known. These are the ones the errors come from, because they are the ones with three separate forms.
  • The i — a — u pattern — sing / sang / sung, drink / drank / drunk, begin / began / begun. Three forms again, and the participle is the -u- one.

Gotten and got#

American English keeps two participles for get, and the split is real and worth learning, because British English has only one.

FormMeansExample
have gottenobtained, received, became — an actual eventI've gotten three offers. · It's gotten cold.
have gotpossess — a present-tense synonym for haveI've got a car. = I have a car
have got tomust — a present-tense obligationI've got to go. = I have to go

Only the first row is a present perfect at all. I've got a car looks like one and is not: it says nothing about the past and simply means I have a car. Americans use gotten freely and it is fully standard here; I've got in the possession sense is equally standard and equally American, despite an old prescriptive grumble about it.

The core idea: it is a present tense#

The name misleads everyone. The present perfect is a present tense. Its auxiliary is have or has — a present-tense verb — and the claim it makes is a claim about now. The past participle supplies the event; the present auxiliary supplies the time.

        past                                      NOW                future
        ──────●─────────────────────────────────────┤
              │                                     │
         the event happened              this is what the sentence
         (when: unstated,                     is actually about
          and unstatable)

That is the whole mechanism. The present perfect reaches back, picks up an event, and brings it forward to say something about the present moment. The event is the evidence; the present is the point.

Two consequences, and everything else in this section follows from them.

1. It never says when. You can say how long, how often, how recently, whether ever — but not when. The moment a sentence names a finished past time, the present perfect is impossible.

✗ I've seen him yesterday. ✓ I saw him yesterday. ✗ We've met two years ago. ✓ We met two years ago.

2. It cannot narrate. A story is a sequence of past events in order, and every one of them is anchored to a moment. The present perfect has no moment to anchor to, so it cannot take a second step. I've woken up, I've made coffee, I've gone to work is not English. Stories are past simple, and that is section 5.

The present relevance test#

Before choosing the present perfect, finish the sentence out loud with "…so…" or "…and that's why…". If something sensible comes out, the present perfect is right.

I've lost my keys — so I can't get in. ✓ She's broken her arm — so she can't drive. ✓ I've been to Japan — so I know what to expect there. ✓

And when nothing comes out, or when the only thing that comes out is "…so that is a thing that occurred," the past simple is the form:

I saw him yesterday. — so…? Nothing. It was just a past event. We moved here in 2019. — so…? Nothing; the date is the point.

There are three distinct ways a past event can be relevant now, and they are the three families of use below:

The relevance is…Because…Example
a resultthe effect is still in placeI've lost my keys.
an open periodthe time it happened in has not endedI've had three coffees today.
a recordthe experience is part of who you are nowI've been to Japan.

The uses#

1. Life experience#

The present perfect says an event is somewhere in a person's record, with no interest at all in when.

I've been to Japan. She's never eaten oysters. Have you ever ridden a horse? I've read that book — don't lend me your copy.

The word ever belongs to this use, in questions, and never is its negative. Both sit between the auxiliary and the participle.

Have you ever been to Alaska? I've never been to Alaska. That's the strangest thing I've ever heard.

The pattern to copy is the conversational one. The present perfect opens a topic; the past simple develops it. This is the single most useful thing in the section, because it is what fluent conversation sounds like:

— Have you been to the new place downtown? — Yeah, I went on Saturday. It was packed, and the food took forever.

As soon as the second speaker supplies an occasion, the tense switches, and it stays switched for the rest of the story.

2. Unfinished time periods#

If the period of time containing the event has not ended, the event is still inside the present, and the present perfect is correct.

I've had three cups of coffee today. We've hired two people this month. She's called me twice this week. It's rained every day this year. How many times have you been late this month?

The expressions that create an open period: today, this morning / afternoon / evening, this week, this month, this year, this semester, in the last few days, in my life, all day, all week, ever. So far, up to now and up to this point name the open period out loud and often sit at the front: So far we've raised about four thousand dollars · Up to now nobody has complained · Everything has gone fine so far.

The same phrase flips tense when the period closes. This morning is an open period at ten o'clock and a closed one at six in the evening:

At 10 a.m.: I've had two meetings this morning. At 6 p.m.: I had two meetings this morning.

We've sold forty tickets today. — the day is still going We sold forty tickets yesterday. — closed

That is the cleanest demonstration there is that the present perfect is about now, not about the event. The event is identical in both sentences. What changed is what time it is.

3. Recent events with present consequences#

A finished action whose result is what matters. The event may have been a second ago or a month ago; the sentence is about the state it left behind.

I've lost my keys. — I don't have them now She's broken her arm. — it's in a cast now They've canceled the flight. — we're stuck now I've forgotten his name. — I can't introduce him now The cat's knocked over the plant. — there's dirt on the floor now The price has gone up again.

Each of those has a present-tense paraphrase, which is the giveaway. I've lost my keys ≈ my keys are missing. She's broken her arm ≈ her arm is broken. If the paraphrase works, the present perfect is the right form.

This is also how English opens a piece of news and then retreats into the past simple for the detail:

A gas leak has forced the evacuation of two buildings downtown. Crews arrived shortly after nine and shut off the main. No injuries were reported.

First sentence: what the situation is. Everything after it: what happened. Same alternation as the conversation above.

4. The adverbs#

A small group of adverbs lives with this tense. Their positions are fixed and worth memorizing as a block, because they are the part learners get wrong even when the tense is right.

just. Very recently — minutes, not days.

I've just gotten here. She's just walked out the door.

already. It happened, and sooner than the listener expected. In questions it carries surprise.

We've already eaten. Have you already paid? — you paid? that fast?

yet. Questions and negatives only. It means the thing is expected and has not happened.

Have you finished yet? The package hasn't arrived yet.

✗ I've done it yet is not English. In an affirmative, the word is already.

still vs yet. Both concern something that has not happened; they differ in position and in temperature. Yet goes at the end and is neutral. Still goes in front of the negative auxiliary and adds impatience.

He hasn't called yet. — plain statement He still hasn't called. — and I'm annoyed

Note the position carefully: before the contracted negative, never after. ✗ He hasn't still called.

recently and lately. Close, and not identical. Recently works with the present perfect and with the past simple; lately is essentially restricted to the perfect, and leans toward questions and negatives.

Have you seen her recently? / I recently moved to Denver. — both fine I haven't been sleeping well lately. / Have you talked to him lately? ✗ I moved to Denver lately.

5. For and since: a state that reaches the present#

This is the use Russian speakers get wrong most often, and it is worth slowing down for. A situation that started in the past and is still true now takes the present perfect in English — not the present.

I've lived here for ten years. — and I live here now She's worked at the hospital since 2018. — and she works there now We've known each other since college. He's been sick for three days. How long have you had that car?

The ✗ forms a learner produces instead — ✗ I live here ten years, ✗ I am here since Monday — are dealt with in For Russian speakers below, because they come from a specific and predictable source.

The test is mechanical. Ask how long — the answer is a for phrase. Ask since when — the answer is a since phrase. The same stretch of time can be described either way:

SituationWith forWith since

✗ since three years is the single commonest error in this section. Three years is a length, so it takes for.

✗ I've worked here since five years. ✓ I've worked here for five years. ✗ We've been married since a long time. ✓ We've been married for a long time.

Since as a conjunction. When since introduces a clause rather than a noun phrase, the clause goes into the past simple, because it names a completed starting event, while the main clause stays in the present perfect:

I've felt better since I quit coffee. She hasn't spoken to him since they argued. We've had three power cuts since we moved in.

A warning about for. Unlike since, a for phrase does not by itself select the present perfect. It sits happily in the past simple, and the choice of tense then carries the whole meaning:

I've lived in Chicago for ten years. — I live there now I lived in Chicago for ten years. — I don't anymore

That pair is worth saying out loud a few times. It is the clearest case in English of the tense, and nothing else, telling the listener whether something is still true.

Simple or continuous? With a state verb — know, have, be, own, believe, belong, understand — the simple present perfect is the only option: I've known her for years, never ✗ I've been knowing her. With an activity verb, the present perfect continuous is usually more natural: I've been waiting for an hour. The full comparison is section 4.

6. Repeated actions up to now#

Something that has happened more than once in a period that is still open. The count is a count so far, and it can still go up.

I've seen that movie three times. She's called me twice today. We've been to Denver four times. He's applied for six jobs this month. They've raised the rent twice since we moved in.

Compare a count in a closed period, which takes the past simple:

I saw that movie three times when I was a kid. She called me twice yesterday.

The life-of-the-subject test. When the subject is a person and the count is of their life's work, the tense reports whether they are still alive to add to it:

Philip Roth wrote more than twenty novels. — he died in 2018; the total is final Louise Erdrich has written more than twenty novels. — she is still writing

Using the present perfect for a dead author is a small, specific, and very noticeable error, and the reverse — the past simple for a living one — quietly implies they have stopped.

7. Superlatives, ordinals, and It's the first time…#

After a superlative or an ordinal, the relative clause takes the present perfect. It is close to obligatory, and the pattern is worth learning as a fixed shape.

That's the best meal I've ever had. It's the worst storm we've seen in years. This is the first email I've gotten all week. That's the third time she's canceled on me.

It's the first time… and its relatives run on the same machinery, and this is one of the very few places where the present perfect is not optional:

It's the first time I've flown alone. It's the second time this has happened this month. That's the third time you've asked me that. ✗ It's the first time I fly alone. ✗ It's the first time I flew alone.

Move the frame into the past and everything shifts with it, into the past perfect:

It was the first time I'd flown alone.

The past perfect is section 7.

8. Have been vs. have gone#

Two participles, two different claims, and one of them uses the participle of be to do the work of go.

FormMeansWhere is the subject now?
has gone to Xleft for Xat X, or on the way — not here
has been to Xvisited X at some pointback — here, or anywhere else

Mom has gone to the store. — she's out; she isn't home Mom has been to the store. — she went and came back; the milk is in the fridge

Two follow-on notes:

  • First person almost never takes gone. If I had gone to Paris, I would not be here telling you about it. I've been to Paris is the sentence. The exception is a habitual one, where gone means "used to go regularly": I've gone to that same dentist for fifteen years.
  • Been to vs been in. Been to is a visit, finished. Been in or been at is a stay, still going: I've been to Chicago (I visited) against I've been in Chicago since Tuesday (I'm there now).

The big contrast: present perfect vs. past simple#

Everything above comes down to one recurring decision, so here it is as a procedure.

The decision procedure#

  1. Does it name a finished past time — yesterday, in 2019, at six, ago?
         YES ──────────────────────────────────►  PAST SIMPLE. Stop.

  2. Is it a narrative — two or more events in order?
         YES ──────────────────────────────────►  PAST SIMPLE. Stop.

  3. Is the period it happened in still open — today, this week, so far?
         YES ──────────────────────────────────►  PRESENT PERFECT.

  4. Is the point the present result, not the past event?
     (test: "…so…" finishes the sentence)
         YES ──────────────────────────────────►  PRESENT PERFECT.

  5. Is it still going on — for / since / how long?
         YES ──────────────────────────────────►  PRESENT PERFECT.

  6. Otherwise ──────────────────────────────────►  PAST SIMPLE.

Step 1 comes first for a reason: it overrules everything. However relevant the result is, a stated past time forces the past simple. I've lost my keys is fine; ✗ I've lost my keys yesterday is not, no matter how lost they still are. The repair is I lost my keys yesterday.

The honest American picture#

Textbooks written in Britain present the perfect as compulsory with just, already, and yet. That is not American usage. In American English the past simple is ordinary in all three cases, and nobody hears it as an error:

American, everydayAlso American, and standardBritish requires
Did you eat yet?Have you eaten yet?Have you eaten yet?
I just saw him.I've just seen him.I've just seen him.
She already left.She's already left.She's already left.
Did you ever go to Rome?Have you ever been to Rome?Have you ever been to Rome?
I never met her.I've never met her.I've never met her.

Both columns are correct in the United States. A learner who says Have you eaten yet? sounds slightly careful; a learner who says Did you eat yet? sounds like everyone else. Neither will be corrected.

Which is safer in writing? The present perfect, without qualification. In edited American prose — a report, an email to a client, an application, anything that will be read rather than heard — the present perfect is the expected form with just, already, yet, ever, never, so far, since, and any unfinished period. A copy editor will change Did you eat yet? and will not change Have you eaten yet? The advice is therefore asymmetric and easy to follow: use whichever you like in speech, and write the perfect.

Two limits on the licence, because it is narrower than it looks:

  • It applies to these adverbs and to recent events, and to nothing else. It does not extend to for and since: ✗ I live here since Monday and ✗ How long do you live here? are errors in every variety of English, spoken or written, American or not.
  • The reverse error is worse. ✗ I have finished it yesterday is broken English and marks a learner instantly. I already finished it does not. If you are going to err, err toward the past simple with a stated time, never toward the perfect with one.

The passive#

Have / has + been + past participle. The auxiliary agrees with the subject; been never changes.

The report has been sent. All the tickets have been sold. Nothing has been decided yet. The road hasn't been repaved since 2014. Has the package been delivered? That question has never been answered.

Three forms to hold apart, because all of them contain has been:

FormWhat it isExample

has been + adjective / nounpresent perfect of beShe has been sick.
has been + -ingpresent perfect continuousHe has been working.

For Russian speakers#

The problem in one line#

Russian has no present perfect, and the Russian perfective past covers both English forms at once. «Я потерял ключи» is I lost my keys and I've lost my keys. «Он уехал» is He left and He's gone. «Я прочитал эту книгу» is I read that book and I've read that book. The distinction English forces on you in every sentence is one your first language never asked you to notice, which is why it does not feel like a hard choice — it feels like no choice at all, and the past simple comes out by default.

«Я был в Японии»I went to Japan in 2015.I've been to Japan.Is there a date attached?
«Я сломал руку»I broke my arm skiing.I've broken my arm.Is it in a cast now?
«Мы встретились в 2010 году»We met in 2010.—A date blocks the perfect entirely

Read the right-hand column again. The question is never when did it happen. It is always does it matter now — and that is the test to carry around.

The "does it matter now?" test#

Take the Russian sentence you want to say and ask one question about it:

Am I telling them about a past event, or about the present situation?

  • About the event — where, when, how, what happened next → past simple.
  • About the situation — what the world looks like as a result → present perfect.

If you can attach «вчера», «в прошлом году», «в 2019 году», «два года назад» to the Russian sentence without it sounding odd, you are talking about the event, and English wants the past simple. If attaching one of those would spoil the point you were making, you want the present perfect.

Why Russian aspect is not a shortcut#

It is tempting to map Russian perfective onto the English perfect. The names are close and the idea seems similar. It does not work, and relying on it produces errors in both directions:

RussianAspectEnglish
«Я вчера прочитал книгу»perfectiveI read the book yesterday. — past simple
«Я читал эту книгу»imperfectiveI've read that book. — present perfect
«Я потерял ключи»perfectiveI've lost my keys. — present perfect
«Он три года работал там»imperfectiveHe worked there for three years. — past simple

The two systems are not measuring the same thing. Russian aspect asks was the action completed. English asks does it bear on now. Those questions have different answers, and the correspondence between the two columns is close to random.

The how long trap — the big one#

This is the error that survives longest, because it is produced by a rule that works perfectly in Russian.

Russian uses the present tense for a state that started in the past and is still going. English uses the present perfect. The duration in Russian is carried by the adverbial; in English it is carried by the tense.

Russian✗ Word for word✓ English
«Я живу здесь два года»✗ I live here two yearsI've lived here for two years.
«Я знаю её с детства»✗ I know her since childhoodI've known her since childhood.
«Я работаю здесь с 2019 года»✗ I work here since 2019I've worked here since 2019.
«Я здесь с понедельника»✗ I am here since MondayI've been here since Monday.
«Мы женаты уже десять лет»✗ We are married ten yearsWe've been married for ten years.
«Сколько ты здесь живёшь?»✗ How long do you live here?How long have you lived here?
«Я не вижу её с июня»✗ I don't see her since JuneI haven't seen her since June.

The rule, in one sentence: if the situation started in the past and is still true, English uses the present perfect, however present it feels in Russian. The tell is the word how long or a for / since phrase; when you see one, the present tense is not available.

The reason the error is so persistent is that ✗ I live here two years is perfectly comprehensible. Nobody misunderstands it, so nobody corrects it, and it can survive a decade of otherwise excellent English.

«уже» and «ещё не»#

Russian «уже» does the work of two English words, and they are not interchangeable.

RussianEnglishWhere
«Я уже это сделал»I've already done it.affirmative → already
«Ты уже это сделал?»Have you done it yet?neutral question → yet
«Ты уже это сделал?!»Have you already done it?surprise → already
«Я ещё не сделал»I haven't done it yet.negative → not… yet
«Он всё ещё не позвонил»He still hasn't called.«всё ещё не» → still + negative

And «уже» in «Я живу здесь уже два года» is not already at all — it is the for of duration: I've lived here for two years now.

Two smaller habits#

The missing auxiliary. Russian expresses all of this without a helper verb, so the auxiliary is the thing that gets dropped: ✗ I never been there, ✗ You seen this? Both of these exist in casual American speech, which makes them easy to pick up and hard to shed. In anything written, the auxiliary is required: I've never been there, Have you seen this?

Adverb position. Russian puts «уже», «никогда», «только что» wherever the emphasis wants them. English fixes them between the auxiliary and the participle: I've already done it, She's never been, They've just left. ✗ I already have done it and ✗ I have done already it are both wrong for the same reason.

Quick reference#

The one-line version. Have / has + past participle. It is a present tense. It never says when.

Four rules that catch most of the errors.

  1. A stated past time forces the past simple. Yesterday, last week, in 2019, ago, when I was a kid — any of them and the perfect is out.
  2. For + a length, since + a point. For three years, since 2019. Never ✗ since three years.
  3. How long + still true = present perfect. Not the present, however present it feels in Russian.
  4. After have, use the participle. Gone, not went. Seen, not saw. Taken, not took.

The four sentences to remember.

I've lost my keys. — the result is what matters (and I still can't get in)

I've lived here for ten years. — and I live here now

Have you eaten yet? — expected, hasn't happened; Did you eat yet? in speech

I saw him yesterday. — a time is named, so the perfect is impossible

Practice#

Put the verb in brackets into the present perfect or the past simple, and choose for or since where both are offered.

  1. I ____ (never / be) to Alaska.
  2. ____ you ____ (finish) the report yet?
  3. She ____ (live) in Portland ____ 2018.
  4. We ____ (have) three meetings this morning. (It is 10 a.m.)
  5. We ____ (have) three meetings this morning. (It is 6 p.m.)
  6. I ____ (see) him yesterday at the gym.
  7. How long ____ you ____ (know) her?
  8. He ____ (not call) me back yet.
  9. They ____ (move) to Denver in 2019 and ____ (live) there ever since.
  10. That's the best meal I ____ (ever / eat).
  11. It's the first time I ____ (fly) alone.
  12. Mom isn't here — she ____ (go) to the store.
  13. ____ you ever ____ (be) to the new museum? — Yes, I ____ (go) last month.
  14. I ____ (work) here ____ five years.
  15. She ____ (be) at her desk ____ eight o'clock.
  16. The package ____ (not deliver — passive) yet.
  17. He ____ (write) four novels. (He died in 2003.)
  18. She ____ (write) four novels. (She's forty and still writing.)
  19. I ____ (lose) my wallet, so I can't pay for lunch.

4We've had three meetings this morning.The morning is still going.
