The present simple#

The form English uses for whatever is simply the case — facts, habits, schedules, rules, states, headlines, stories — plus the one ending, third-person -s, that the whole tense rests on.

← Tenses · All grammar topics

The name misleads twice. The present simple is often not about the present — The train leaves at six is about tomorrow morning, and So I walk in and the place is empty is about last Tuesday. And it is not "simple" in the sense of easy. It is simple in the technical sense: the verb with nothing added to it, no auxiliary, no participle, no aspect. It is the bare form of the English verb, and that is exactly why it ends up carrying so many jobs.

Here is the idea the rest of the file uses:

The present simple does not report what is happening. It reports what is the case. I work downtown is not a claim about this minute — I may be asleep. It is a claim about the kind of fact my employment is. The present continuous is the form that reports what is happening; the present simple is the form that reports what holds.

I work downtown. — that is my situation I'm working downtown this month. — that is what is going on right now

Every use below is that same claim aimed at something different: at a fact, at a routine, at a law of nature, at a timetable, at a rule of a game, at the plot of a story.

Form#

The paradigm#

One verb, all six persons, three shapes each. Verb: work.

PersonAffirmativeNegativeQuestion
II workI don't workDo I work?
youyou workyou don't workDo you work?
he / she / ithe workshe doesn't workDoes he work?
wewe workwe don't workDo we work?
theythey workthey don't workDo they work?

Exactly one cell in the affirmative column differs from the others, and it is the third person singular. Everything else in the table is the base form. That single -s is the last surviving piece of English verb agreement, and it is treated in full in the third-person -s below.

Notice what happens to it in the other two columns: it moves onto the auxiliary. He works → he doesn't work → Does he work? The -s never appears twice, which is why ✗ He doesn't works and ✗ Does he works? are errors rather than variants.

Negative#

Do / does + not + base form. The contraction is the normal form in speech and in most writing.

I don't eat meat. She doesn't answer her phone after nine. They don't open on Sundays. The car doesn't start when it's this cold.

The full forms do not and does not are emphatic, formal, or both — a contract, a warning sign, a fight. Do not touch the glass. She does not know, and she is not going to find out from me.

✗ She doesn't works here. → She doesn't work here. ✗ He not works here. → He doesn't work here. ✗ He no works here. → He doesn't work here.

Two verbs skip do entirely, because they are their own auxiliaries: be (I'm not ready, ✗ I don't be ready) and the modals (he can't swim, ✗ he doesn't can swim).

Emphatic do#

The same auxiliary that carries the negative also carries emphasis, and this is the only way English has of stressing a plain present-tense verb. The auxiliary is stressed; the main verb stays bare.

I do know him — we went to school together. She does care. She just doesn't say so. It does get cold here in February.

This is what a contradiction sounds like in English, and it is nearly always a reply to a doubt, spoken or implied.

Questions#

Yes/no questions
do / does + subject + base form.

Do you live near here? Does she know about the meeting? Do they deliver on weekends?

Wh-questions: the question word comes first, and the rest is unchanged.

Where do you live? What does she do? — the most common question in the language about someone's job, and it contains do twice: the auxiliary and the main verb How much does it cost? Why don't they answer?

Subject questions take no do at all. When the question word is the subject, there is nothing to invert, and the verb keeps its -s.

Who lives here? — not ✗ Who does live here? What happens next? Which door opens from the outside? How many people work in your office?

Compare the two in one pair: Who knows Dana? asks for the subject; Who does Dana know? asks for the object. The presence of do is the only signal, and reversing it reverses the question.

✗ What means this word? → What does this word mean? ✗ Where lives your sister? → Where does your sister live?

Negative questions#

Contracted, the negative attaches to the auxiliary and everything else is unchanged:

Don't you like it? Doesn't she work on Fridays? Why don't we just call them?

Uncontracted, the not moves behind the subject, and the result is markedly formal:

Do you not understand the question? Does he not realize how this looks?

Negative questions are rarely neutral requests for information. They carry an expectation, and which one depends on the context:

ShapeWhat it doesExample
Don't you…?expects yes; half-arguing for itDon't you think we should wait?
Doesn't he…?surprise, or a correctionDoesn't he live in Austin anymore?
Don't you ever…?reproachDon't you ever answer your phone?
Why don't we…?a suggestion, not a questionWhy don't we take the train?

Short answers#

The answer repeats the auxiliary, never the main verb.

QuestionYesNo
Do you smoke?Yes, I do.No, I don't.
Does she drive?Yes, she does.No, she doesn't.
Do they know?Yes, they do.No, they don't.
Are you ready?Yes, I am.No, I'm not.
Is he here?Yes, he is.No, he isn't. / No, he's not.

Two rules ride along with the table.

  • Never repeat the lexical verb. ✗ Yes, I smoke. is not a short answer; it is a new sentence, and as an answer it sounds abrupt and foreign.
  • Never contract an affirmative short answer. ✗ Yes, I'm. ✗ Yes, he's. ✗ Yes, they're. An auxiliary at the end of a clause has to be full. The negatives contract freely: No, he isn't and No, he's not are both fine.

And one thing English does that many languages do not: a short answer answers the fact, not the question. After a negative question, yes always means the affirmative:

"Don't you like it?" — "Yes, I do." = I like it. "Don't you like it?" — "No, I don't." = I don't like it.

Contracted forms as they are actually said#

The affirmative present simple has nothing to contract — the only contraction available anywhere in the paradigm belongs to be (I'm, he's, they're) and to the negatives.

don't /doʊnt/ · doesn't /ˈdʌzənt/ · isn't /ˈɪzənt/ · aren't /ɑrnt/

What a learner actually hears is a stage further reduced. In ordinary speech the auxiliary fuses with the pronoun after it, and the h of he, him, and her disappears:

None of these is sloppy English; they are what fluent American speech sounds like at normal speed. The full inventory, and the difference between a written contraction and a spoken reduction, is in Contractions.

One American note on have: as a main verb it does not contract and does not skip do. Write I have a car and Do you have a minute?, not ✗ I've a car and not Have you a minute? — the second is British. I've got a car and Have you got a minute? are fine but informal.

The third-person -s#

Where it appears, and where it does not#

The -s shows up in exactly one cell of the whole system: third person singular, present simple, affirmative, main verb. It is missing everywhere else, and each absence has a reason.

ContextFormWhy
Affirmativeshe worksthe one place it lives
Negativeshe doesn't workthe -s moved onto does
Questiondoes she work?same — it is on the auxiliary
After a modalshe can work, he must gomodals never inflect, and never take to
SubjunctiveI insist that she work latethe mandative subjunctive is tenseless
Plural subjectthe dogs barkthe subject is not singular
Infinitiveshe wants to worknot a tensed form at all

A mnemonic that is worth more than it looks: the -s goes on the noun or on the verb, never on both. The dog barks. The dogs bark. If you can hear which word is carrying it, you are agreeing correctly.

And the indefinite pronouns are all singular, however plural they feel: Everybody knows. Nobody cares. Each of the files needs a label. One of my brothers lives in Tulsa.

Pronunciation: /s/, /z/, /ɪz/#

The ending is spelled two ways and pronounced three. The choice is completely automatic, and it is decided by the last sound of the base form — never by the last letter.

SoundAfterExtra syllable?Examples
/ɪz/a sibilant: /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/yespasses, misses, closes, uses, refuses, pushes, washes, watches, teaches, catches, changes, manages, judges, fixes
/s/any other voiceless sound: /p, t, k, f, θ/nostops, keeps, hopes, sits, wants, gets, writes, works, takes, walks, thinks, laughs, coughs

The rule in one line
a sibilant cannot sit next to another sibilant, so English inserts a vowel and you get an extra syllable; everywhere else the ending simply matches the voicing of the sound before it.

Put a finger on your throat and say the base form. If the last sound buzzes — /b, d, g, v, ð, m, n, ŋ, l, r/ or any vowel — the ending is /z/. If it does not, the ending is /s/. If the last sound is already a hiss or a hush, the ending is /ɪz/ and the word gains a beat: watch is one syllable, watches is two.

This is the same three-way split as the noun plural (cats /s/, dogs /z/, buses /ɪz/) and the possessive 's, and it is the exact mirror of the -ed rule (/t/, /d/, /ɪd/). Learn it once and it pays out in three places. The endings are laid out phonetically in Pronunciation.

The irregular ones#

Four verbs break the pattern, and between them they cover a large share of everything anyone says.

Have is worth its own line because it is also an auxiliary. As a main verb it takes do-support like any other verb: She has a car → She doesn't have a car → Does she have a car? Never ✗ Has she a car? in American English. As an auxiliary in the present perfect it needs no do: She has left → She hasn't left → Has she left?

The irregular past and participle forms of these and 360 other verbs are in the verb catalog; the present simple is the one tense where the irregularity mostly does not show, because the -s form is regular for nearly every verb in the language.

be: am / is / are#

Be is the exception to everything above. It has three present forms instead of two, it needs no do in the negative or the question, and it is the verb that turns up in more sentences than any other.

PersonAffirmativeNegativeQuestionNegative question
II am / I'mI'm notAm I?Aren't I?
youyou are / you'reyou're not / you aren'tAre you?Aren't you?
he / she / ithe is / he'she's not / he isn'tIs he?Isn't he?

theythey are / they'rethey're not / they aren'tAre they?Aren't they?

Both negative contractions are standard, and they differ only in what they stress: he isn't ready puts the weight on the negation, he's not ready on not ready as a whole.

The first person has a hole in it. There is no ✗ amn't, and ain't is nonstandard, so the negative question borrows the plural form: Aren't I supposed to sign this? It is illogical and it is the standard.

Be takes no do: ✗ Do you be ready? ✗ He doesn't be here on Mondays. And there is / there are follow the number of what comes after them: There is a problem. There are three problems.

I'm from Chicago. She's a nurse. They're at the airport. He's not ready, and the car isn't either. Is it far? — Are they open on Sunday?

What the present simple is used for#

Eleven uses follow. They look unrelated and they are not: in every one of them the speaker is stating what is the case rather than reporting what is under way.

1. Permanent states and facts#

The default use. Where somebody lives, what they do, who they are, what belongs to whom.

I live in Denver. She works for a credit union downtown. He has two brothers and a sister. My parents own a hardware store in Grand Junction. The building dates from 1910 and holds about forty offices. This road runs all the way to the coast.

"Permanent" means permanent-for-now, not forever. I live in Denver stays true until I move, and nobody hears it as a lifetime commitment. What it is not is temporary-and-marked: I'm living with my sister until the lease starts uses the continuous precisely because the arrangement has an expiration date built into it.

2. Habits and routines#

Something done repeatedly. This is where the frequency adverbs live — see Time expressions.

I take the 7:15 bus. He goes to the gym on Tuesdays and Thursdays. We eat dinner at six. She checks her email before she gets out of bed. I don't drink coffee after noon. Does he always park there?

A habit is not an action in progress. He smokes describes the man; He's smoking describes the moment. The difference matters in job interviews, doctors' offices, and arguments.

3. General truths#

Laws of nature, mathematics, how a species behaves, how a machine works — anything the speaker presents as holding independently of the occasion.

Water boils at 212 degrees Fahrenheit. Metal expands when it gets hot. The sun rises in the east. Cats sleep about sixteen hours a day. Oil floats on water. Two and two makes four. The Missouri flows into the Mississippi at St. Louis.

This is also the tense of definitions and rules: A noun names a thing. The clock stops when the ball goes out of bounds. Coverage begins on the first of the month.

4. The scheduled future#

A timetable puts the future in the present simple. The event has to be fixed by something external — a schedule, a program, a calendar — not merely decided by the speaker.

The train leaves at six. Our flight gets in at 11:40. The movie starts at 7:20 and runs two hours. School starts on the 24th. The game is at noon tomorrow. The store opens at nine and closes at seven. The tour stops in Dallas on Friday, then goes to Austin.

The verbs that do this are a short list: leave, arrive, start, begin, end, finish, open, close, run, depart, land, be, and a few more. They share a meaning — a fixed point in a program.

The boundary is worth being exact about, because it is the one place the present simple genuinely competes with the present continuous for the future:

The future is…FormExample
a published timetablepresent simpleThe train leaves at six.
a personal arrangementpresent continuousI'm leaving at six.
a predictionwillThe train will be late again.
a plan you can already see formingbe going toWe're going to take the early train.

So ✗ I go to the dentist tomorrow is odd — a dentist's appointment is a personal arrangement, and English wants I'm going to the dentist tomorrow. But The dentist opens at eight is a fact about the office, and the simple is correct.

5. Instructions, directions, and procedures#

Explaining how something is done, when the you is a generic one rather than a particular person.

You take the second left, then go about a mile and it's on your right. First you brown the onions, then you add the garlic and let it cook for a minute. You press this, and the green light comes on. You fill out the top half, sign it, and mail it to the address on the back.

The imperative is the alternative and is common in written recipes — Brown the onions. Add the garlic. The present-simple version is what people say out loud, and it is gentler, because it describes a procedure rather than ordering someone around.

6. Commentary and demonstration#

Sports commentary, cooking demonstrations, magic tricks, lab walk-throughs — anything where the words keep pace with the action. The present simple is used for each completed move, because it is over by the time the sentence ends; the continuous describes the background that is still going on.

He passes to Rodgers — Rodgers shoots — and it's in! She serves, he returns it long, and that's the set. Now I add two eggs and whisk until it thickens. I take an ordinary card, place it here, and turn it over. Martinez rounds second and heads for third.

Mixed with the continuous, in one breath of real commentary: Jones is running down the sideline — he cuts inside, jumps, and catches it.

7. Headlines#

Newspaper headlines report past events in the present simple. It is a convention of the form, not a mistake and not the narrative present: the space is tight, and the present sounds immediate.

Mayor Resigns Over Budget — she resigned yesterday Senate Passes Infrastructure Bill Dow Falls 300 Points

Headlines also drop articles and forms of be (Three Injured in Crash = three people were injured), and they use the to-infinitive for the future: President to Visit Texas. Inside the article itself the ordinary past tense returns: The mayor resigned Tuesday after…

8. The narrative present: stories and jokes#

Storytellers switch into the present to pull the listener into the scene. Everything in the story is over; the tense says otherwise, and that is the point.

So I walk into the kitchen and there's water everywhere. I call the landlord, he doesn't pick up, and meanwhile the ceiling starts dripping. A guy walks into a bar and orders a drink… In 1804, Lewis and Clark set out from St. Louis. — the historic present, standard in history writing

American conversation has its own quotative version, with goes or is like standing in for says:

So she goes, "I never got the message," and I'm like, "I sent it twice."

Once a narrative starts in the present, it stays there. Sliding back and forth between the present and the past inside one anecdote is the most common way the device goes wrong.

9. Performative verbs#

With a small set of verbs, saying the sentence is doing the thing. There is no separate act to report, so there is nothing for the continuous to report as in progress — the present simple is the only possible form.

I promise I'll be there. I apologize for the delay. I resign, effective Friday. I object. I accept your offer. We thank you for your patience. I bet you five dollars he's late. I hereby declare this bridge open. I pronounce you married.

Test it: I promise is the promise. I'm promising is not a promise — it is a description of somebody making one, which is why it sounds evasive. The full list of these verbs is with the performative verbs in the verb reference.

10. Always and the complaint#

A plain habit takes the present simple. A habit that annoys the speaker takes the present continuous with always, forever, or constantly — and the exaggeration is the whole message.

He always loses his keys. — a neutral fact about him He's always losing his keys. — and it drives me crazy

She always leaves the lights on. — that is what she does She's always leaving the lights on. — a complaint

My laptop always crashes when I open that file. — reliable, almost a rule My laptop's always crashing. — an exasperated exaggeration; it is not literally always

The continuous version is not a different frequency. It is the same behavior with an attitude attached, and it is nearly always negative — praise takes it only ironically. The mechanics of the form belong to the present continuous; what matters here is that a present simple with always is neutral, and swapping it for a continuous adds a complaint you may not have meant.

11. Time and condition clauses: where English refuses the future#

English marks the future once per sentence, and the mark goes in the main clause. A subordinate clause of time or condition takes the present simple even though its meaning is plainly future.

I'll call you when I get home. — ✗ when I will get home We'll start as soon as she arrives. — ✗ as soon as she will arrive Wait here until I come back. — ✗ until I will come back If it rains, we'll move it inside. — ✗ If it will rain Before you leave, turn off the lights. After the movie ends, we can get something to eat. By the time you read this, I'll be in Denver. Once the paperwork clears, the money shows up in two days.

The conjunctions that take this treatment: when, whenever, while, before, after, until, till, as soon as, once, by the time, the moment — and on the condition side if, unless, in case, provided that, as long as, even if.

A present perfect is also allowed, and insists that the event be finished first: I'll call you when I've finished. What is never allowed is will.

One thing this rule does not cover: when and if introducing an indirect question, where will is perfectly correct because the clause is an object, not a time or condition. I don't know when he'll arrive. Ask her if she'll be there. The test is whether the clause answers "when?" for the main verb or acts as its object.

The conditional half of all this is worked out in full in Conditionals, and specifically in The four types, where the zero and first conditionals are nothing but a present simple in the if-clause.

Time expressions and where they go#

One-word frequency adverbs#

The core list, roughly ordered:

AdverbRoughlyExample

usually, normally, generally~90%I usually work from home on Fridays.

  1. Before the main verb. I always take the bus. She never complains.
  2. After be. He is always late. They are never on time.
  3. After the first auxiliary. I don't usually eat breakfast. Do you ever go back? She has never been to Texas.

So be pushes the adverb to the right and every other verb pulls it to the left. That is one rule with one exception, and the exception is the same verb that is exceptional everywhere else.

✗ I take always the bus. → I always take the bus. ✗ He always is late. → He is always late. ✗ She doesn't never call. → She never calls. / She doesn't ever call.

Fronting. Sometimes, usually, normally, often, occasionally and generally can also start the sentence: Sometimes I walk to work. Always, never, rarely and seldom cannot — not in a plain statement. Fronting a negative adverb forces inversion and is strongly literary: Never have I seen anything like it. Rarely does he call first.

End position is available for sometimes and often (I walk to work sometimes), and for almost nothing else in the list.

Adverbial phrases#

Multi-word time expressions behave completely differently: they go at the end of the clause, or at the front for emphasis. They never go in the middle.

I check my email every morning. He works from home on Fridays. We go to my sister's twice a year. The bus comes every fifteen minutes. She calls her mother every Sunday night. Every morning, I walk the dog before work. On Mondays the office opens an hour late.

✗ I every day check my email. → I check my email every day. ✗ She twice a week goes to the gym. → She goes to the gym twice a week.

The inventory, and the order when several of them pile up at the end of a clause, is in Adverbs.

On Mondays means every Monday; on Monday means one particular Monday, usually the next one. Americans also drop the preposition entirely — I work Saturdays — which is not possible in British English and is completely ordinary here.

Never is already negative. It takes no do: He never calls, not ✗ He doesn't never call and not ✗ He never doesn't call. The same goes for rarely, seldom, and hardly ever.

State verbs#

The verbs that live here#

Some verbs describe a state rather than an activity: knowing, owning, liking, seeming, containing. A state has no stages — you cannot be in the middle of knowing something — so these verbs normally refuse the continuous, which means the present simple is where they spend nearly all their time.

GroupMembers
Thinking, believingknow, believe, understand, think (opinion), doubt, remember, forget, recognize, suppose, mean, realize, agree, disagree, imagine (suppose)
Wanting, feelinglike, love, hate, prefer, want, need, wish, mind, care, dislike, appreciate, envy, regret
Sensessee, hear, smell, taste, feel, look, sound, seem, appear
Having, relatinghave (possess), own, possess, belong, contain, include, consist of, lack, involve, depend on
Being, measuringbe, cost, weigh, measure, equal, resemble, fit, matter, deserve, owe, exist

I know what you mean. She owns three rental properties. This belongs to my aunt. It costs forty dollars, and it doesn't include shipping. He seems upset. The box contains two spare fuses. I don't want anything, thanks. Do you mind if I open a window? That sounds expensive.

The list is not a list of exceptions to memorize. It follows from meaning: ask whether the verb names something you do or something that simply holds. If it holds, the present simple is the form.

Verbs with two lives#

Many of these verbs have both a state sense and an action sense, and the continuous is not only allowed with the action sense, it is required. The verb does not change; the meaning does.

The genuine exceptions#

Some state verbs are drifting into the continuous in informal American English, and pretending otherwise leaves a learner unable to explain what they hear every day.

I'm loving this weather. I'm not liking the new layout. I'm hating every minute of this.

The continuous here adds temporary and right now, plus a note of personal enthusiasm. It is casual, it is marked, and it is everywhere in speech and advertising. In edited writing, use the simple — I love this weather.

Two more that are fully standard even in careful prose, because the continuous softens rather than intensifies:

I'm hoping you can help me with something. — gentler than I hope I'm wondering whether we should wait. — gentler than I wonder

And two that remain firmly out: ✗ I'm knowing, ✗ I'm wanting. There is no register in which those are ordinary American English. The stative/dynamic split is also covered from the verb side in Verbs.

The passive#

The present simple passive is is / are + past participle. The tense is unchanged; only the direction is.

ActivePassive
SingularThe city owns the lot.The lot is owned by the city.
PluralThey make these in Maine.These are made in Maine.

The mail is delivered around ten. Spanish is spoken here. The office is cleaned every night. Tickets are sold online only. He is paid every other Friday. These forms are reviewed by two people before they are filed.

Negatives and questions work exactly as they do with be, because be is the auxiliary — no do:

The bill isn't paid automatically. Are the forms signed at the counter? How is it spelled? Why aren't these labeled?

The present simple passive is the standard voice of rules, processes, signs, and instructions — anywhere the doer is unknown, obvious, or beside the point. Shoes are removed at the door. The results are posted on Friday. Applications are not accepted after the deadline. The full passive paradigm across all tenses is in Verbs.

For Russian speakers#

Four interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

1. Russian's imperfective covers both English present tenses, so the choice is invisible.

«Я читаю» is I read and I'm reading at once. Russian marks aspect lexically — perfective against imperfective — and the imperfective present does habitual and in-progress with the same form, leaving the difference to context. English splits that one form into two tenses and makes the split obligatory: every present-tense sentence forces a choice a Russian speaker has no feeling for.

RussianHabitual → present simpleIn progress or temporary → present continuous
«Я работаю в банке»I work at a bank.—
«Что ты делаешь?»What do you do? — your jobWhat are you doing? — right now
«Он живёт в Чикаго»He lives in Chicago. — permanentlyHe's living in Chicago. — for now
«Я читаю»I read a lot. — habitI'm reading a book about Texas. — now
«Она не курит»She doesn't smoke.She isn't smoking right now.

The two questions in row two are a real trap: What do you do? asks about a career and What are you doing? asks what is in your hands at this moment. Russian «Чем ты занимаешься?» is used for both.

The working rule: if you could add right now or at the moment, use the continuous; if you could add every day or in general, use the simple.

2. Russian has no present-tense to be, so English sentences come out with a hole in them — or with an extra verb.

«Он врач», «Она дома», «Я голоден», «Это дорого» have no verb at all. English requires one, and an article besides:

✗ He doctor. → He is a doctor. ✗ She at home. → She is at home. ✗ I hungry. → I am hungry. ✗ This very expensive. → This is very expensive.

The same gap produces the opposite error, and it is the more persistent one. Russian expresses some English verbs with an adjective — «согласен», «уверен», «рад» — so the learner correctly reaches for a copula and attaches it to an English word that is already a verb:

✗ I am agree. → I agree. — «Я согласен» ✗ I am not agree. → I don't agree. / I disagree. ✗ I am think so. → I think so. ✗ I am need help. → I need help.

I am sure, I am glad, and I am afraid are correct, because those really are adjectives in English too. Agree, think, need, want, know and remember are verbs, and a verb takes no am.

3. The third-person -s has no Russian analogue.

Russian marks every person on the verb — «читаю, читаешь, читает» — so agreement itself is not a foreign idea. The problem is the opposite: in a language where all six endings are audible and meaningful, one lonely ending that carries no information at all is easy to drop, and dropping it costs nothing in comprehension. That is why ✗ He go survives into advanced English long after everything harder has been fixed.

Three specific spots where the -s goes wrong:

✗ He go to work at eight. → He goes to work at eight. ✗ She don't know. → She doesn't know. — you will hear this in songs and in speech; it is not written English ✗ Does he goes there? → Does he go there? — the -s is already on does

It also helps to know that the ending looks plural to a Russian eye, because -s on a noun is a plural everywhere else in the language. It is not. The dog barks has a singular dog; the dogs bark has plural dogs and a bare verb. The -s is on one word or the other, never on both.

4. Russian asks yes/no questions with intonation; English asks them with do.

«Ты знаешь его?» is a statement said with a rising tone. English cannot do that in writing and does not do it in neutral speech:

✗ You know him? → Do you know him? ✗ Where you live? → Where do you live? ✗ What means this word? → What does this word mean? — Russian «Что значит это слово?» puts the verb first, and English will not

Answering is the mirror image. Russian answers the question («Ты не голоден?» — «Да» = "right, I'm not"); English answers the fact. Don't you like it? — Yes always means you like it.

What transfers for free. Two things, worth saying because they are rare. Russian also uses the present for scheduled future events — «Поезд отходит в шесть» is The train leaves at six — so use 4 above needs no unlearning. And Russian also uses the present for vivid storytelling — «Иду я вчера…» — so the narrative present is already familiar.

Common mistakes#

WrongRightWhy
✗ He go to work at eight.He goes to work at eight.Third person singular takes -s.
✗ She don't know.She doesn't know.The -s moves onto the auxiliary: does + n't.
✗ He doesn't works here.He doesn't work here.The -s is on does; the main verb goes bare.
✗ Do you can help me?Can you help me?Modals are their own auxiliaries and never take do.
✗ I am agree with you.I agree with you.Agree is a verb, not an adjective. No be.
✗ I am knowing the answer.I know the answer.State verbs refuse the continuous.
✗ He doctor.He is a doctor.English requires the copula and an article.
✗ What means this word?What does this word mean?Questions need do and statement word order after it.
✗ Who does live here?Who lives here?A subject question takes no do.
✗ Yes, I'm.Yes, I am.Affirmative short answers never contract.
✗ I take always the bus.I always take the bus.Frequency adverbs go before the main verb.
✗ He always is late.He is always late.After be, the adverb follows the verb.
✗ I every day go there.I go there every day.Phrases go at the end, not in mid-position.
✗ She doesn't never call.She never calls.Never is already negative; it takes no do.

✗ Have you a minute?Do you have a minute?Main-verb have takes do-support in American English.

Quick reference#

The paradigm, compressed.

   affirmative   I / you / we / they  work          he / she / it  works
   negative      I / you / we / they  don't work    he / she / it  doesn't work
   question      do   I / you / we / they  work?    does  he / she / it  work?
   short answer  Yes, I do.  /  No, I don't.        Yes, he does.  /  No, he doesn't.
   be            I am        you / we / they are    he / she / it  is

Three rules that prevent most errors.

  1. The -s appears once, in one place. Third person singular, affirmative, main verb. In the negative and the question it moves onto do, and the main verb goes back to bare.
  2. Everything that is not be or a modal needs do to make a negative or a question. Be needs nothing. Modals need nothing.
  3. The present simple states what is the case; the present continuous reports what is in progress. When the two compete, ask whether right now or in general fits better.

Choosing the tense in one question.

   Is the sentence about something under way at this moment,
   or about something temporary?

   YES ──── present continuous   I'm working on it right now.
   NO ───── present simple       I work downtown.
                                 ...and also: habits, facts, timetables,
                                 states, commentary, headlines, stories,
                                 performatives, and time / if clauses.

Practice#

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form — and where a choice is offered, decide between the present simple and the present continuous.

  1. My sister (work) for an insurance company in Hartford.
  2. Water (freeze) at 32 degrees Fahrenheit.
  3. He (not have) a car, so he (take) the bus.
  4. What time (the train / leave) tomorrow morning?
  5. (you / know) where she keeps the spare key?
  6. She (be) usually at her desk by eight.
  7. I (think) you're right — but I (think) about it some more tonight.
  8. Who (want) coffee?
  9. They (not open) on Sundays, and they (close) at six the rest of the week.
  10. Look — he (pass) to Torres, Torres (shoot), and it (be) in!
  11. I'll email you as soon as I (hear) back from them.
  12. The mail (deliver — passive) around ten every day.
  13. ✗ He always is complaining about the food. — fix the word order.
  14. I (be) sorry — I (apologize) for the confusion.
  15. Mark the pronunciation of the ending — /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/: watches, stops, plays, teaches, laughs, goes, fixes, needs.
