# The present perfect continuous

> *I've been waiting* — the form that measures how long, what it does that the present perfect simple cannot, why a counted quantity kills it, and the long list of pairs where the two forms say different things.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The present perfect continuous is two aspects welded into one form. The **perfect** half — *have been* — attaches the situation to the present moment. The **continuous** half — the `-ing` — gives the situation width: it went on, it took time, and the sentence is about that going-on.

> **I've been waiting for an hour.**

Neither half alone would do the job. *I waited for an hour* puts the waiting in finished past time and says nothing about now. *I'm waiting* says nothing about the hour. *I've waited for an hour* is possible but changes the emphasis to a completed stretch, as if the waiting were now a thing I have accumulated. Only *I've been waiting* says both things at once: the waiting started in the past, it has been going on the whole time, and it reaches this moment.

Here is the sentence to carry through the whole section, because everything else follows from it.

**The present perfect continuous is about the activity. The present perfect simple is about the result.** Ask what the sentence is really reporting — the running of the thing, or the outcome of the thing — and the form follows.

```
   present perfect simple         I've painted the kitchen.
   ───────────────────●           the event is closed; what survives is the result
                     now

   present perfect continuous     I've been painting the kitchen.
   ─────~~~~~~~~~~~~~~●           the activity has width, and it runs up to now
                     now
```

## The form

### The pattern

***have* / *has* + *been* + the `-ing` form.**

Three words, and only the first one ever changes. *Been* is frozen, the participle is frozen, and the whole burden of person, number, negation and question order falls on *have*.

| Person | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | *I have been working* | *I have not been working* | *Have I been working?* |
| you | *you have been working* | *you have not been working* | *Have you been working?* |
| he / she / it | *he has been working* | *he has not been working* | *Has he been working?* |
| we | *we have been working* | *we have not been working* | *Have we been working?* |
| they | *they have been working* | *they have not been working* | *Have they been working?* |

Only *he / she / it* takes *has*. Everything else takes *have*, including singular *they* (*Has the new hire been complaining?* but *Have they been complaining?*).

### Affirmative

> **I've been trying** to reach you all morning.  
> **She's been studying** for the bar exam since June.  
> **It's been raining** since Tuesday.  
> **We've been living** out of boxes for three weeks.  
> **They've been arguing** about that fence for years.

### Negative

The *not* goes on *have*, exactly where it goes in any other perfect.

> **I haven't been sleeping** well.  
> **He hasn't been answering** his phone.  
> **They haven't been paying** rent since March.  
> **We haven't been getting** the reports.

There is no other placement. ✗ *I have been not sleeping* is not the negative of the sentence — it is a sentence about deliberately not sleeping, and almost nobody produces it on purpose.

### Questions

*Have* moves in front of the subject. *Been* and the participle stay put, in that order.

> **Have** you **been waiting** long?  
> **Has** she **been feeling** all right?  
> **How long have** you **been working** here?  
> **What have** you **been doing** all afternoon?  
> **Who has** he **been talking** to?  
> **Where have** you **been hiding**?

Note that *What have you been doing?* and *What have you done?* are not the same question. The first asks how you have been spending your time; the second asks what you have accomplished, and in the wrong tone it is an accusation.

### Negative questions

Contracted *haven't* / *hasn't* inverts as one word. The uncontracted version splits, and sounds formal to the point of stiffness.

> **Haven't** you **been listening**?  
> **Hasn't** she **been taking** her medication?  
> **Haven't** they **been paying** you?  
> *Have* you **not been sleeping**? — formal, and slightly cold

Like every negative question in English, these carry an expectation. *Haven't you been listening?* is not a request for information; the speaker already believes you have not been, and is saying so.

### Short answers

The answer repeats *have* / *has* and nothing else.

> "Have you been waiting long?" — "**Yes, I have.**" / "**No, I haven't.**"  
> "Has it been raining?" — "**Yes, it has.**" / "**No, it hasn't.**"

*Yes, I have been* is also available, and it is emphatic — it insists on the point, usually against doubt: "You haven't been looking." "**I have been.**" What does not happen is repeating the whole phrase: *Yes, I have been waiting* is a full sentence, not a short answer, and as an answer it sounds like a complaint, which is often exactly what the speaker wants.

### Contractions as they are actually said

Nobody says *I have been waiting*. The contracted form is the ordinary one, and the full form is emphasis.

| Written full | Written contracted | Roughly how it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| I have been | **I've been** | /aɪv bɪn/, often /aɪbɪn/ |
| you have been | **you've been** | /juv bɪn/ |
| he has been | **he's been** | /hiz bɪn/ |
| she has been | **she's been** | /ʃiz bɪn/ |
| it has been | **it's been** | /ɪts bɪn/ |
| we have been | **we've been** | /wiv bɪn/ |
| they have been | **they've been** | /ðeɪv bɪn/ |
| there has been | **there's been** | /ðɛrz bɪn/ |
| have not been | **haven't been** | /ˈhævn̩t bɪn/ |
| has not been | **hasn't been** | /ˈhæzn̩t bɪn/ |

Three things about that table are worth saying out loud.

**First, *been* is unstressed and short.** In American English it is /bɪn/, rhyming with *bin*, not the long British /biːn/. It carries no stress at all in a normal sentence, which is why it disappears so completely from what a learner hears: *I've been waiting* arrives as three beats, *I*-*b'n*-*WAIT-ing*.

**Second, the *'s* in *he's been* is *has*, not *is*.** Written *'s* is ambiguous, and the word after it settles the question: *he's been waiting* (a participle *been* follows — *has*) against *he's waiting* (an `-ing` follows directly — *is*). The same ambiguity is worked through in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

**Third, spoken American English drops the auxiliary entirely** in casual registers — *I been waiting an hour*, *She been calling all day*. This is real, it is widespread, and it is not writable. In anything edited it is *I've been*.

### The trap: *I've been* is not always a continuous

*Been* is the past participle of *be*, and it appears in perfectly ordinary present perfect **simple** sentences that have no continuous aspect in them at all.

> **I've been** here since eight. — present perfect simple of *be*  
> **I've been** sick all week. — present perfect simple of *be*  
> **I've been** to Chicago twice. — present perfect simple of *be*, meaning "visited"

None of those is a present perfect continuous, because none of them has an `-ing` after *been*. There is no ✗ *I've been being here*. The test is mechanical: **a present perfect continuous always ends in `-ing`.** If nothing follows *been* but an adjective, a place, or a prepositional phrase, you are looking at the perfect of *be*.

The *been* / *gone* pair belongs here too, and it catches everyone:

> **She's been** to Paris. — she went and she is back  
> **She's gone** to Paris. — she went and she is still there  
> **She's been going** to Paris every spring for years. — a repeated trip; this one is the continuous

## What it is used for

### 1. An action continuing up to now

The core use. Something started in the past, it has not stopped, and it is still true as you speak.

> **I've been waiting** for an hour.  
> **She's been working** here since 2019.  
> **It's been snowing** all morning.  
> **We've been saving** for a down payment for two years.  
> **He's been sitting** in that chair since lunch.  
> **They've been building** that overpass since before we moved in.  
> **I've been reading** the same page for ten minutes.  
> **The dog's been barking** all night.

Every one of those sentences is still running as it is spoken. The waiting has not ended, the snow has not stopped, the overpass is not finished. That is the unmarked reading of the form, and it is why *for* and *since* sit in nearly all of these sentences.

### 2. An action that has just stopped, and left evidence

The second use is the mirror of the first: the activity **has** stopped, very recently, and what makes it worth mentioning is the state it left behind. This is the use that has no equivalent anywhere else in the tense system.

> **You've been crying.** — your eyes are red  
> **I've been painting** — sorry about the hands.  
> **He's been running.** — he is out of breath at the door  
> **Someone's been smoking** in here.  
> **You've been eating** my chips.  
> **The kids have been playing** in the mud again.  
> **It's been raining.** — the street is wet; it has stopped

*You've been crying* is not a report of an ongoing cry. It is a deduction from present evidence, and the continuous form is what carries the deduction: the crying explains the face. The simple would not work — *You've cried* is not an English sentence anyone says in that situation, because it points at a completed act rather than at the visible aftermath.

This is also the form for accusation and for discovery, which is why it turns up so often with *someone*:

> **Someone's been sitting** in my chair.  
> **Someone's been using** my account.  
> **Who's been messing** with the thermostat?

### 3. Duration for its own sake

Even when the activity is finished and nothing visible is left, the continuous is the form that puts the **length** of the thing in the foreground. The sentence is about how long, not about what came of it.

> **I've been meaning** to do that for months.  
> **We've been talking** about this for an hour and we're no closer.  
> **She's been asking** the same question all week.  
> **I've been on my feet since six.** — note: perfect simple of *be*, but the same job  
> **They've been at it** since noon.

Compare the pair directly. *I've worked on this report* reports that work happened. *I've been working on this report* reports that work has been going on, and invites the follow-up *how long?*

### 4. Repeated or continuing activity over a period

The activity need not be literally uninterrupted. If a set of repeated actions fills a period that reaches the present, the continuous covers the whole period as a single stretch.

> **I've been going to the gym.** — not right now; on and off, lately  
> **He's been calling** me every day this week.  
> **She's been taking** piano lessons since September.  
> **We've been eating** out too much.  
> **I've been thinking** about what you said.  
> **They've been having** problems with the landlord.  
> **I've been getting** headaches in the afternoons.

*I've been going to the gym* does not claim you are in the gym, or that you have been in the gym continuously since March. It claims that gym-going is an activity currently characterizing your life. The period is continuous; the events inside it are scattered.

This is the use that carries *lately*, *recently*, and *these days*:

> **I've been sleeping** badly **lately**.  
> **Have** you **been seeing** much of Dana **recently**?  
> **Prices have been going** up.  
> **The team's been losing** a lot.

### 5. Complaints, irritation, and pointed observation

The form has a social life. Because it foregrounds the stretch of time, it is the natural vehicle for saying that something has gone on too long, and speakers reach for it when they are annoyed.

> **I've been waiting** for twenty minutes.  
> **You've been saying** that for a year.  
> **She's been promising** to fix it since we moved in.  
> **I've been asking** for that file all week.  
> **They've been ignoring** every email I send.  
> **He's been leaving** his dishes in the sink again.

*Again* and *all week* are the giveaways. None of these sentences is neutral, and swapping in the simple drains the complaint out: *I've waited twenty minutes* is a statement of fact, *I've been waiting for twenty minutes* is an accusation aimed at whoever is responsible.

### 6. Explanations and apologies

The same duration-focus makes the form the standard way of accounting for yourself — explaining an absence, a delay, or a silence.

> **I've been meaning to call you.**  
> **Sorry — I've been swamped.**  
> **I've been trying** to get hold of you.  
> **We've been dealing** with a family thing.  
> **I've been putting** this off.  
> **He's been looking** for work since the layoffs.

***I've been meaning to*** deserves its own line, because it is a fixed apology formula and it has no simple counterpart. There is no ✗ *I've meant to call you* in ordinary use. The whole point of the sentence is the stretch of unfulfilled intention, and only the continuous carries it.

### 7. Temporary arrangements

The continuous marks a situation as provisional. Put it against a present perfect simple and the simple reads as settled.

> **I've been staying** with my sister while the floors are done.  
> **She's been covering** for me while I'm out.  
> **We've been using** the back entrance since the renovation started.  
> **He's been driving** his brother's truck.

Nothing in the grammar says these will end, but the form implies it, and a listener will assume it.

### The time expressions it lives with

| Expression | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ***for*** + a length | *I've been waiting **for** an hour.* | The stretch itself. *For* can be dropped: *I've been waiting an hour.* |
| ***since*** + a starting point | *She's been working here **since** 2019.* | A point on the calendar or clock, never a length. |
| ***since*** + a clause | *I've been dizzy **since I stood up**.* | The clause verb is past simple. |
| ***how long*** | ***How long** have you been learning Spanish?* | The single most common question frame for this form. |
| ***all*** + a period | *It's been raining **all day**.* | *all morning, all week, all year, all this time* |
| ***lately*** | *I've been sleeping badly **lately**.* | A vague recent period. |
| ***recently*** | *We've been getting complaints **recently**.* | End position is the natural one; mid-position is stiff. |
| ***these days***, ***this week*** | *I've been working from home **this week**.* | The period must include now. |
| ***ever since*** | *He's been odd **ever since** the interview.* | Emphatic *since*. |
| ***since when*** | ***Since when** have you been smoking?* | Always challenging in tone. |

Two rules hide in that table.

***For* takes a length. *Since* takes a point.** *For three hours*, *since three o'clock*. ✗ *since three hours* is the error, and it is among the most common in the language. The entries for [*for*](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/02-core-relational.md#for) and [*since*](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/05-time.md#since) have the full picture.

**The period has to include now.** *I've been working all morning* is fine at eleven o'clock and wrong at nine in the evening, when the morning is over and the sentence needs a past tense: *I worked all morning*. Any finished time expression — *yesterday*, *last week*, *in 2019*, *at three o'clock* — throws the sentence out of the perfect entirely.

## The contrast with the present perfect simple

This is the whole difficulty of the form, and it is worth slowing down for.

### The question to ask

**Is the sentence about the activity, or about what the activity produced?**

> **I've been painting the kitchen.** — there is paint in my hair; I may not be done  
> **I've painted the kitchen.** — it is painted; go look at it

Both sentences describe the same afternoon. They report different things about it. The first reports the painting; the second reports the kitchen.

### Activity against result

> **I've been reading** your manuscript. — I am in the middle of it  
> **I've read** your manuscript. — I finished it; I have an opinion

> **She's been fixing** the car. — that is where she has been  
> **She's fixed** the car. — it runs

> **They've been building** a deck. — it is going up  
> **They've built** a deck. — it is up

> **I've been making** dinner. — that is why I look like this  
> **I've made** dinner. — it is on the table

The simple looks past the activity at its outcome, and if there is no outcome the simple has nothing to report. That is why *I've been trying* is idiomatic and *I've tried* means something else — *I've tried* announces a result, usually a failure.

### Duration against quantity

Here is a rule sharp enough to use as a test.

**A stated quantity of what was produced forces the simple. A stated quantity of time takes the continuous.**

> **I've been reading all day.** — duration  
> **I've read three chapters.** — quantity  
> ✗ *I've been reading three chapters.*

> **I've been writing emails all morning.**  
> **I've written eleven emails.**  
> ✗ *I've been writing eleven emails.*

> **She's been calling** him all week.  
> **She's called** him six times.  
> ✗ *She's been calling him six times.*

> **We've been drinking** coffee since nine.  
> **We've drunk** four pots of coffee.

The reason is aspectual, not arbitrary. *Three chapters* draws a boundary around the reading and makes it a completed, countable thing. The continuous refuses boundaries — it presents the activity as open and unbounded — so the two cannot sit in the same clause. Anything that counts a finished output behaves the same way: *three pages*, *five miles*, *two loads of laundry*, *six times*, *half the book*.

Watch the same verb switch forms as the object changes:

| Sentence | Form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I've been running.* | continuous | activity, no boundary |
| *I've been running for an hour.* | continuous | duration |
| *I've run five miles.* | simple | a measured, completed quantity |
| *I've run three marathons.* | simple | countable completed events |
| *I've been running marathons for years.* | continuous | the activity across a period, not a count |

That last row is the escape hatch: a plural object with no number on it is not a quantity, and the continuous is available again.

### Temporary against permanent

With verbs that can describe either, the continuous suggests a temporary arrangement and the simple a settled one.

> **I've been living** in Austin. — for now; there is probably an end to it  
> **I've lived** in Austin **all my life.** — this is where I am from

> **He's been working** at a bakery. — a job, currently  
> **He's worked** there **for thirty years.** — a career

> **She's been teaching** third grade this year.  
> **She's taught** third grade **since 1998.**

The implication is soft and context can overrule it, but it is real, and a long stated period tends to pull the sentence toward the simple all by itself. *I've been living here for forty years* is grammatical and sounds faintly odd, because forty years is not a temporary arrangement.

### State verbs have no continuous

Verbs that describe a state rather than an activity do not take the continuous at all, in any tense. There is no activity to be in the middle of.

> ✓ **I've known** her **for years.**  
> ✗ *I've been knowing her for years.*

> ✓ **We've had** this car **since 2018.**  
> ✗ *We've been having this car since 2018.*

> ✓ **He's owned** the building **for a decade.**  
> ✗ *He's been owning the building for a decade.*

> ✓ **I've always wanted** to see Alaska.  
> ✗ *I've always been wanting to see Alaska.*

> ✓ **She's believed** that **since college.**  
> ✗ *She's been believing that since college.*

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: **with a state verb, the present perfect simple does the continuous's job.** *I've known her for years* has exactly the meaning that *I've been waiting for years* has — it starts in the past, runs to now, and is still true. The form is simple only because the verb leaves no choice.

The core list: *know, believe, understand, mean, remember, forget, want, need, like, love, hate, prefer, own, belong, contain, consist of, seem, resemble, cost, weigh, matter, exist, deserve, doubt, recognize*. The full inventory, and the verbs with both a stative and a dynamic sense, are in [the verbs reference](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md#22-stative-verbs).

That second group matters here, because a verb with two senses takes the continuous in one sense and refuses it in the other:

| Verb | Stative — simple only | Dynamic — continuous available |
|---|---|---|
| *think* | *I've always thought so.* | *I've been thinking about it.* |
| *have* | *We've had the car since 2018.* | *I've been having trouble sleeping.* |
| *see* | *I've seen what you mean.* | *She's been seeing a specialist.* |
| *feel* | *I've felt this way for months.* | *I've been feeling off.* |
| *taste* | *It's tasted odd since Tuesday.* | *He's been tasting the sauce all evening.* |
| *expect* | *I've expected this for a while.* | *We've been expecting you.* |
| *look* | *It's looked bad from the start.* | *I've been looking for my keys.* |

### Verbs where both forms work, and barely differ

With a handful of durative verbs, the two forms are close to interchangeable when a *for* or *since* phrase is present. This is not laxity; it is a genuine overlap in the system.

| Verb | Continuous | Simple | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| *live* | *I've been living here for six years.* | *I've lived here for six years.* | almost none; continuous hints temporary |
| *work* | *She's been working here since May.* | *She's worked here since May.* | almost none |
| *study* | *He's been studying Russian for two years.* | *He's studied Russian for two years.* | almost none |
| *teach* | *I've been teaching since 2010.* | *I've taught since 2010.* | simple sounds more like a career |
| *feel* | *I've been feeling tired.* | *I've felt tired.* | almost none |
| *stay* | *We've been staying at my aunt's.* | *We've stayed at my aunt's.* | simple can read as "on occasion" |
| *wear* | *He's been wearing that shirt all week.* | *He's worn that shirt all week.* | almost none |
| *rain* | *It's been raining all day.* | *It's rained all day.* | almost none |
| *wait* | *I've been waiting for an hour.* | *I've waited for an hour.* | simple is stiffer; continuous is the default |
| *sleep* | *I've been sleeping badly.* | *I've slept badly.* | continuous spreads it over a period |

Two cautions about this list. The overlap requires the duration phrase — remove *for six years* and the two sentences split apart again (*I've been living here* against *I've lived here*, which now sounds incomplete). And the overlap is one-directional in the other cases: where the verb is not durative, the forms do not converge at all. *I've been breaking the window* is not a variant of *I've broken the window*; it is close to nonsense.

### The decision table

| If the sentence is about… | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **how long** something has gone on | continuous | *I've been waiting for an hour.* |
| **how many** or **how much** was produced | simple | *I've written three pages.* |
| the **activity** itself | continuous | *She's been cooking.* |
| the **result** of the activity | simple | *She's cooked dinner.* |
| something **still going on** | continuous | *It's been raining since noon.* |
| something **finished** | simple | *It's rained twice this week.* |
| **visible evidence** of a recent activity | continuous | *You've been crying.* |
| an **achievement**, a first, a completed whole | simple | *She's climbed Rainier.* |
| a **temporary** arrangement | continuous | *I've been staying with friends.* |
| a **permanent** situation | simple | *I've lived here all my life.* |
| a **repeated activity** filling a recent period | continuous | *I've been going to the gym.* |
| a **counted number of occasions** | simple | *I've gone to the gym four times.* |
| a **state** | simple — no choice | *I've known her for years.* |
| something with ***ever***, ***never***, ***always***, ***yet***, ***already*** | simple — almost always | *Have you ever been to Japan?* |

That last row is a useful shortcut. The adverbs of the present perfect simple — *ever*, *never*, *yet*, *already*, *just*, *once*, *twice*, *so far* — are all about whether something has happened, which is a question the continuous does not answer. ✗ *Have you ever been going to Japan?* fails on exactly that ground. (*Always* is the one with an exception: *He's always been complaining about the noise* is fine, where *always* means "constantly, and it is irritating.")

### Where the difference disappears

With the durative verbs in the overlap list, and with a *for* or *since* phrase attached, the two forms converge to the point where the choice is a matter of rhythm. Do not go looking for a meaning difference in *I've lived here for six years* against *I've been living here for six years*; there is a whisper of one, and in most contexts it is not audible. The places where the choice genuinely carries meaning are the ones above: result, quantity, evidence, and state.

## The passive

The passive of the present perfect continuous is ***has been being*** + past participle.

> The road **has been being repaired** for months.

That sentence is grammatically well formed. It is also, in practice, one English does not use. Two forms of *be* stacked back to back — *been being* — are heavy enough that speakers and writers route around them essentially always, and the construction is rare enough in edited American prose to count as an oddity rather than an option.

What English says instead, in rough order of frequency:

| Instead of | English says | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *The road has been being repaired.* | **They've been repairing the road.** | Go active. An indefinite *they* costs nothing. |
| *The house has been being built since May.* | **The house has been under construction since May.** | A prepositional phrase replaces the verb. |
| *My application has been being reviewed.* | **My application is still being reviewed.** | Present continuous passive, which is unremarkable. |
| *The bill has been being debated all week.* | **The bill has been debated all week.** | Present perfect simple passive; loses a shade of duration and nobody notices. |
| *The system has been being tested.* | **The system has been undergoing testing.** | *Undergo* is the stock replacement in technical prose. |

Note what does work, and works freely: the present perfect **simple** passive (*has been repaired*) and the present **continuous** passive (*is being repaired*). It is only the combination of the two that English declines to build. If you find yourself needing *has been being*, the sentence wants restructuring.

## What the negative actually means

The negative of the present perfect continuous is not simply the negative of the affirmative, and the difference from the simple is sharper here than anywhere else.

> **I haven't been sleeping well.** — across a period; some sleep, all of it bad  
> **I haven't slept.** — not at all; zero

> **He hasn't been answering** his phone. — repeatedly unavailable, over days  
> **He hasn't answered** my message. — one message, no reply

> **They haven't been paying** rent. — a pattern of non-payment  
> **They haven't paid** the March rent. — one specific payment, missing

The continuous negative describes the **character of a period**; the simple negative describes the **absence of an event**. Choosing wrong is not a grammar error so much as a factual one — *I haven't slept* when you mean *I haven't been sleeping well* claims something considerably more dramatic than you intend.

## *How long* and the questions this form owns

*How long* is the frame the present perfect continuous was built for, and getting it right fixes a large fraction of the errors in the section.

> **How long have you been waiting?**  
> **How long has she been working** here?  
> **How long have they been married?** — state; simple, and no continuous exists  
> **How long have you been learning English?**  
> **How long has it been raining?**

The answer comes back in the same tense:

> "How long have you been waiting?" — "**About twenty minutes.**" / "**I've been here since two.**"

Two related frames use *it* as a dummy subject and are worth memorizing whole:

> **It's been three hours since she left.** — a length, plus *since* + past simple  
> **How long has it been since you've seen him?** — the informal American version; *since you saw him* is the careful one

And the challenge question:

> **Since when have you been driving a truck?**

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **am waiting** since two o'clock.* | *I**'ve been waiting** since two o'clock.* | A stretch of time reaching now requires the perfect. The present continuous cannot host *since* or a duration. |
| ✗ *I **am waiting** for an hour.* | *I**'ve been waiting** for an hour.* | Same error without *since*. The present continuous has no depth in the past. |
| ✗ *I **wait** an hour.* | *I**'ve been waiting** for an hour.* | The present simple is for habits and facts, not for a span running up to now. |
| ✗ *How long **are** you **studying** English?* | *How long **have** you **been studying** English?* | The most common *how long* error there is. |
| ✗ *How long **do** you **know** her?* | *How long **have** you **known** her?* | A state verb still needs the perfect — just the simple perfect. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been knowing** her for years.* | *I**'ve known** her for years.* | State verbs have no continuous. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been having** a car since 2018.* | *I**'ve had** a car since 2018.* | Possessive *have* is stative. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been reading** three chapters.* | *I**'ve read** three chapters.* | A counted quantity closes the activity and forces the simple. |
| ✗ *She**'s been calling** him six times.* | *She**'s called** him six times.* | Same rule, with a count of occasions. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been working** here **since three years**.* | *…**for** three years* / *…**since** 2022.* | *For* takes a length; *since* takes a point. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been working** here **yesterday**.* | *I **worked** here yesterday.* | A finished time expression is incompatible with any perfect. |
| ✗ *Have you **ever been going** to Japan?* | *Have you **ever been** to Japan?* | *Ever* asks whether it has happened, which the continuous does not answer. |
| ✗ *I **have been not** sleeping.* | *I **haven't been** sleeping.* | *Not* attaches to *have*, not to *been*. |
| ✗ *I**'ve been being** here for an hour.* | *I**'ve been** here for an hour.* | *Been* is already the participle of *be*. No continuous exists for it. |
| ✗ *The road **has been being repaired**.* | *They**'ve been repairing** the road.* | Technically legal, effectively unused. Recast it. |
| ✗ *He **been waiting** an hour.* | *He**'s been waiting** an hour.* | The dropped auxiliary is spoken only. |
| ✗ *It **has been rain** all day.* | *It **has been raining** all day.* | The `-ing` is not optional. |

## For Russian speakers

This is the English form with the least Russian behind it, and the interference is correspondingly heavy. Five points.

### 1. Russian has no perfect at all

Russian distributes among tense and aspect what English distributes among tense, aspect and the perfect. For an activity that started earlier and is still running, Russian uses the **present tense** — usually imperfective — plus a time expression, often with **уже** or **всё ещё**:

| Russian | English | ✗ |
|---|---|---|
| «Я **жду** уже час.» | *I**'ve been waiting** for an hour.* | ✗ *I wait an hour.* ✗ *I am waiting an hour.* |
| «Я **живу** здесь пять лет.» | *I**'ve been living** here for five years.* | ✗ *I live here five years.* |
| «Она **работает** тут с мая.» | *She**'s been working** here since May.* | ✗ *She works here since May.* |
| «Дождь **идёт** со вторника.» | *It**'s been raining** since Tuesday.* | ✗ *It is raining since Tuesday.* |
| «Я **учу** английский три года.» | *I**'ve been learning** English for three years.* | ✗ *I am learning English three years.* |

The rule that fixes all five at once is worth writing on the wall:

**A Russian present-tense verb with a stretch of time attached to it is an English present perfect — never an English present.**

That is the single highest-value sentence in this section for a Russian speaker. The Russian present is doing a job English gives to the perfect, and the learner's instinct produces *I am waiting since two* because it is a faithful word-for-word rendering of a sentence that is perfectly correct in Russian.

### 2. The *how long* frame

The question form has the same problem, and it is even more visible because *how long* questions are so frequent in conversation.

| Russian | English | ✗ |
|---|---|---|
| «Сколько времени ты **учишь** английский?» | *How long **have** you **been learning** English?* | ✗ *How long are you learning English?* |
| «Сколько ты уже **ждёшь**?» | *How long **have** you **been waiting**?* | ✗ *How long do you wait?* |
| «Как давно ты её **знаешь**?» | *How long **have** you **known** her?* | ✗ *How long do you know her?* |
| «Сколько вы здесь **живёте**?» | *How long **have** you **been living** here?* | ✗ *How long are you living here?* |

Note the third row. The Russian is identical in shape to the others, and the English changes form because *know* is a state verb. Russian gives the learner no warning about that, because **знать** behaves exactly like **ждать** in this frame.

### 3. **Уже** and **всё ещё** do what the English form does

Russian marks "and it is still going on" lexically, with a particle. English marks it grammatically, with the form itself, and then usually leaves the particle out:

> «Я **уже** час жду.» → *I've been waiting for an hour.* — no word for *уже*  
> «Она **всё ещё** спит.» → *She's still asleep.* / *She's been asleep all morning.*

Translating **уже** as *already* is the standard trap. *I've already been waiting for an hour* is grammatical but means something else — it is an argument about promptness, not a report of duration. In most cases **уже** in this frame translates to nothing at all, and the perfect continuous absorbs it.

### 4. Imperfective and perfective map onto the contrast — roughly

The Russian aspect pair lines up with the English simple/continuous contrast often enough to be useful and not often enough to be trusted.

| Russian | English | Fits the rule? |
|---|---|---|
| «Я **читал** весь день.» (impf.) | *I**'ve been reading** all day.* | yes |
| «Я **прочитал** три главы.» (pf.) | *I**'ve read** three chapters.* | yes |
| «Я **красил** кухню.» (impf.) | *I**'ve been painting** the kitchen.* | yes |
| «Я **покрасил** кухню.» (pf.) | *I**'ve painted** the kitchen.* | yes |
| «Я **знал** его много лет.» (impf.) | *I**'ve known** him for years.* | **no** — state verb, simple |
| «Я **звонил** ему шесть раз.» (impf.) | *I**'ve called** him six times.* | **no** — a count forces the simple |

The first four rows make the mapping look reliable. The last two are why it is not: Russian imperfective covers repeated completed events and states, and English assigns both of those to the simple. Use the aspect pair as a first guess and the decision table above as the check.

### 5. The evidence use has no Russian counterpart at all

«Ты плакала» is a past-tense statement. The English equivalent is a present-tense one:

> «Ты **плакала**.» → ***You've been crying.***  
> «Кто-то **курил** здесь.» → ***Someone's been smoking** in here.*  
> «Он **бегал**.» → ***He's been running.***

Russian reports the past event and leaves the listener to infer the present relevance. English builds the present relevance into the verb, which is exactly what the perfect is for. A Russian speaker who renders these with a past simple — *You cried*, *Someone smoked here* — produces grammatical English that means something different: a report about an earlier occasion rather than a deduction about right now.

## Quick reference

**The form.**

```
   have / has  +  been  +  V-ing

   I've been waiting.          Have you been waiting?
   She's been working.         Has she been working?
   They haven't been paying.   Haven't they been paying?
```

**Choose between the two perfects in three questions.**

```
   Is the verb a state verb — know, have, own, want, believe, mean?

   YES ──── present perfect SIMPLE. There is no other option.
            I've known her for years.

   NO ───── Does the sentence state a finished quantity — three chapters,
            six times, five miles, the whole book?

            YES ── present perfect SIMPLE.
                   I've read three chapters.

            NO ─── Is the point the activity, its duration, or the mess
                   it left behind?

                   YES ── present perfect CONTINUOUS.
                          I've been reading all day.

                   NO ─── present perfect SIMPLE.
                          I've read your email.
```

**The six sentences to remember.**

> **I've been waiting for an hour.** — duration running up to now

> **You've been crying.** — a recent activity with visible evidence

> **I've been painting the kitchen.** — activity … against … **I've painted the kitchen.** — result

> **I've been reading all day.** — duration … against … **I've read three chapters.** — quantity

> **I've known her for years.** — a state; the continuous does not exist

> **I've been meaning to call you.** — the apology formula, continuous only

**Four rules that cover most of the errors.**

1. **A Russian present plus a time span is an English perfect.** ✗ *I wait an hour*, ✗ *I am waiting since two*.
2. ***For* + a length, *since* + a point.** ✗ *since three hours*.
3. **A counted quantity forces the simple.** ✗ *I've been reading three chapters*.
4. **State verbs take the simple and mean the continuous.** *I've known her for years* is the duration sentence for *know*.

| 3 | Your eyes are red. **Have** you **been crying**? | Recent activity with visible evidence. |
| 4 | We**'ve lived** / We**'ve been living** in this apartment since 2019. | Both fine; *live* is in the overlap group. The simple leans settled. |
| 5 | How long **have** you **been studying** Japanese? | The *how long* frame. Not ✗ *are you studying*. |

### One more pass

Say what changes when the second sentence replaces the first.

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| *I've cleaned the garage.* → *I've been cleaning the garage.* | Result becomes activity: the first says it is clean, the second says that is where I have been and possibly why I am filthy. |
| *He's been drinking.* → *He's drunk the whole pot.* | Activity with evidence becomes a completed quantity. |
| *I've worked here for ten years.* → *I've been working here for ten years.* | Almost nothing; a faint hint that the arrangement is provisional, which after ten years sits oddly. |
| *They haven't paid the rent.* → *They haven't been paying the rent.* | One missing payment becomes a pattern. |
| *She's seen the doctor.* → *She's been seeing a doctor.* | One appointment becomes an ongoing course of treatment. |
