# The past continuous

> *Was* and *were* plus *-ing* — the tense that holds a past moment open: what was going on when something else happened, what the scene looked like, and why a Russian speaker reaches for the past simple instead.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The past continuous — also called the **past progressive** — puts an action in the middle of itself at a moment in the past.

> At eight o'clock last night I **was eating** dinner.

The sentence does not say when the dinner started, and it does not say whether it was finished. It says that eight o'clock fell **inside** it. That is the whole tense in one idea, and every use below is a version of it:

**The past continuous does not report an event. It reports a moment as being inside an event.**

A frame needs something to frame, so this tense almost never works alone. It comes with a point in time (*at eight*), with another verb (*when the phone rang*), or with a stretch of narrative that supplies the moment for it. A past continuous sentence with nothing to anchor it sounds unfinished — *I was eating dinner*, said out of nowhere, makes a listener wait for the rest — and that is the first practical thing to know about the form.

Drawn on a line, the difference from the past simple is the difference between a segment and a dot:

```
        the past simple                    the past continuous

   ────────●──────────────           ──────▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬──────
        the phone rang                    I was cooking
            one point                 a stretch, seen from inside

   ──────▬▬▬▬▬▬▬●▬▬▬▬▬▬▬──────       I was cooking when the phone rang.
                                      the dot lands inside the stretch
```

The two tenses are not long and short versions of each other, and they are not formal and informal versions of each other. They are two different camera positions on the same past. The past simple stands outside an event and reports that it happened; the past continuous stands inside one and reports that it was going on. Which one you need depends entirely on what the rest of the sentence is doing, which is why [the contrast with the past simple](#the-contrast-with-the-past-simple) below is the longest part of this file.

## Form

### *was* / *were* + the *-ing* form

Two words, always. The first is the past tense of *be* and carries the person and number; the second is the present participle and never changes.

| Person | Auxiliary | + *-ing* |
|---|---|---|
| I | **was** | working |
| you | **were** | working |
| he / she / it | **was** | working |
| we | **were** | working |
| you (plural) | **were** | working |
| they | **were** | working |

The split is **was for the singular, were for the plural — except *you*, which takes *were* in both numbers.** That exception catches Russian speakers constantly, because Russian «ты» and «вы» are distinct and the English pair is not. *Were you waiting?* is the form whether you are addressing one person or a room.

Two more places the auxiliary is decided by something other than the visible subject:

> **There were** three people **waiting** in the hall. — the auxiliary agrees with *three people*, not with *there*  
> **Neither of them was listening.** — *neither* is singular  
> **The team was practicing** when it started to rain. — a collective noun takes the singular in American English

The British habit of treating *team*, *staff*, *government*, and *band* as plurals — *the team were practicing* — is not American. Write *the team was practicing*.

### The complete paradigm

Verb: *work*. Every cell of the tense in one table.

| Subject | Affirmative | Negative | Question | Negative question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I **was working** | I **wasn't working** | **Was** I **working**? | **Wasn't** I **working**? |
| you | you **were working** | you **weren't working** | **Were** you **working**? | **Weren't** you **working**? |
| he / she / it | she **was working** | she **wasn't working** | **Was** she **working**? | **Wasn't** she **working**? |
| we | we **were working** | we **weren't working** | **Were** we **working**? | **Weren't** we **working**? |
| they | they **were working** | they **weren't working** | **Were** they **working**? | **Weren't** they **working**? |

Nothing in the table requires *do*. The past continuous already contains an auxiliary, so the question inverts that auxiliary and the negative attaches *not* to it. ✗ *Did you were working?* and ✗ *I didn't was working* are the errors this produces when the rule is forgotten.

### Affirmative

> I **was reading** when you called.  
> They **were arguing** about money again.  
> The rain **was coming** down in sheets.  
> We **were staying** at a motel outside Flagstaff.

### Negative

*Not* goes between the auxiliary and the participle. In speech and in almost all writing it contracts.

> I **wasn't listening** — say it again.  
> They **weren't expecting** us until Sunday.  
> She **wasn't feeling** well, so she left early.  
> Nobody **was saying** anything.

The uncontracted *was not* and *were not* are emphatic or formal, and in speech they carry heavy stress on *not*:

> I **was not** driving that car. — a denial, not a report

### Questions

Invert the auxiliary and the subject. Everything else stays put.

> **Were** you **waiting** long?  
> **Was** she **crying**?  
> **What were** you **doing** at midnight?  
> **Who was she talking** to? — no inversion: *who* is the subject  
> **Where were** they **going** in such a hurry?  
> **Why wasn't** the alarm **working**?

Word order in a *wh*-question is the one to drill: the question word, then the auxiliary, then the subject, then the participle. ✗ *What you were doing?* is the commonest form error in the tense, and it is a Russian-speaker error specifically, because Russian «Что ты делал?» needs no inversion at all.

### Negative questions

Contracted, the *n't* rides forward with the auxiliary. Uncontracted, *not* stays behind the subject and the result is formal to the point of being stiff.

> **Wasn't** he **supposed** to call? — ordinary  
> **Was** he **not supposed** to call? — formal, or indignant  
> **Weren't** you **listening**?  
> **Wasn't** it **raining** when you left?

Like all negative questions, these are not neutral. They carry the speaker's expectation and usually a little surprise: *Weren't you listening?* means the speaker believes you were not and is not happy about it. Unlike *am I not*, which has no contraction in American English, *was I not* has a perfectly ordinary one — **wasn't I** — so the [*amn't* gap](../02-contractions/README.md) never comes up here.

### Short answers

The short answer repeats the auxiliary and nothing else. Never *did*.

> **Were** you sleeping? — **Yes, I was.** / **No, I wasn't.**  
> **Was** it raining? — **Yes, it was.** / **No, it wasn't.**  
> **Were** they still working at six? — **Yes, they were.** / **No, they weren't.**

> ✗ *Were you sleeping? — Yes, I did.*  
> ✗ *Was it raining? — Yes, it was raining it.*

### Contractions, and the ones that do not exist

| Form | Contraction | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *was not* | [***wasn't***](../02-contractions/catalog/04-negative.md#wasnt) /ˈwʌzənt/ | two syllables |
| *were not* | [***weren't***](../02-contractions/catalog/04-negative.md#werent) /wɚnt/ | **one** syllable, rhymes with *burnt* |
| *I was* | — | there is no ✗ *I's*, no ✗ *I'as* |
| *we were* | — | there is no ✗ *we'ere*; ***we're*** is the **present** |

That last row is a real trap and not a pedantic one. ***We're working*** is the present continuous and ***we were working*** is the past, and in fast speech they are separated by almost nothing: *we're* is /wɪr/, one syllable, while *we were* is /wi wɚ/, two syllables with the second one reduced nearly to a schwa-colored *r*. The same goes for *they're* /ðɛr/ against *they were* /ðeɪ wɚ/, and *you're* /jʊr/ against *you were* /ju wɚ/.

Both auxiliaries reduce heavily when unstressed, which is the reason learners so often fail to hear this tense at all:

> *What **were** you doing?* → /ˈwʌt wɚ jə ˈduɪŋ/ — *were you* comes out as one blurred syllable  
> *She **was** waiting outside.* → /ʃi wəz ˈweɪtɪŋ/ — *was* is /wəz/, never /wɑz/, unless it is stressed

They un-reduce the moment they are stressed, and stress on the auxiliary is how English says *yes it really did happen*: *I **was** /wɑz/ listening.* The full account of these reductions is in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

### The *-ing* spelling, in brief

The participle is the same form the present continuous uses, so the spelling rules are identical. The short version:

| Ending | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| most verbs | add *-ing* | *work → working*, *play → playing*, *wait → waiting* |
| silent *-e* | drop the *e* | *make → making*, *write → writing*, *come → coming* |
| *-ee*, *-ye*, *-oe* | keep the *e* | *see → seeing*, *dye → dyeing*, *canoe → canoeing* |
| one stressed vowel + one consonant | double the consonant | *sit → sitting*, *run → running*, *begin → beginning*, *prefer → preferring* |
| the same but **unstressed** | do not double | *offer → offering*, *listen → listening*, *open → opening* |
| *-ie* | *ie* becomes *y* | *lie → lying*, *die → dying*, *tie → tying* |
| *-c* | add *k* before the *-ing* | *panic → panicking*, *picnic → picnicking* |

One American-British split belongs here because it shows up in every other sentence. British English doubles a final *l* whatever the stress — *travelling*, *cancelling*, *modelling*. **American English does not**: *traveling*, *canceling*, *modeling*, *labeling*, *signaling*, *totaling*. Likewise *focusing* and *benefiting*, not *focussing* or *benefitting*.

**The complete set of spelling rules, with the edge cases and the irregular participles, is in [section 2](02-present-continuous.md#spelling-the--ing-form).** Nothing about the spelling changes when the auxiliary goes into the past; if you can spell the present continuous, you can spell this.

## What it is used for

Nine uses. The first four are the core of the tense and account for most of the past continuous anybody produces; the last five are narrower but none of them is rare.

### 1. In progress at a past moment

The base case. A point in the past is named, and the action was underway when it arrived.

> At eight o'clock I **was eating** dinner.  
> This time last week I **was sitting** on a plane.  
> At midnight we **were still driving**.  
> **What were you doing** at three in the morning?  
> When the results came in, half the office **was already celebrating**.

The moment can be a clock time (*at eight*), a date (*on Tuesday morning*), a phrase like *at that moment* or *just then*, or another sentence that has already put the reader somewhere in time. What it cannot be is nothing at all. *I was eating dinner*, with no anchor anywhere in the paragraph, is a sentence waiting for its other half.

The action need not have been continuous in any literal sense. *I was writing a book that year* does not claim you had a pen in your hand for twelve months; it claims the writing was the thing in progress across that stretch. The tense measures a state of being underway, not uninterrupted physical activity.

### 2. A long action interrupted by a short one

The most-taught use, and the one the tense exists for. Two past events, one of them brief, and the brief one lands inside the long one.

> I **was cooking** when the phone **rang**.  
> She **was crossing** the street when a cab **ran** the light.  
> We **were just sitting** down to eat when the power **went out**.  
> He **was telling** the story for the third time when somebody finally **stopped** him.  
> They **were boarding** when the gate agent **announced** the delay.

The division of labor is fixed and it is worth stating as a rule:

**The long, framing action takes the past continuous. The short, interrupting action takes the past simple.**

"Interrupt" is a loose word here. The short event does not have to stop the long one — the phone rang, and very likely the cooking continued. All the pattern claims is that one event happened while the other was in progress.

### *when* and *while*: which tense follows each

This is where the interruption pattern goes wrong, and the reason is that Russian «когда» and «пока» do not divide the territory the same way.

| Conjunction | What follows it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***when*** + past simple | the **short** event | *I was cooking **when the phone rang**.* |
| ***when*** + past continuous | a background period or state | ***When I was living in Denver**, I walked everywhere.* |
| ***while*** + past continuous | the **long** framing action | ***While I was cooking**, the phone rang.* |
| ***while*** + past simple | only for a durative state or for *whereas* | *While I **lived** in Denver, I walked everywhere.* |
| ***as*** + past continuous | like *while*, a shade more literary | ***As I was leaving**, I heard a crash.* |
| ***just as*** + either | exact coincidence | ***Just as I was leaving**, the phone rang.* |
| ***as soon as*** + past simple | immediately after, not during | ***As soon as I sat down**, the phone rang.* |

Read the table in one sentence: ***while* wants the continuous; *when* takes whichever tense the meaning needs.** That asymmetry is the whole rule. *When* is the flexible one, which is why it is always the safe choice, and *while* is the one that commits you to a long action.

So the interruption sentence has exactly two correct shapes, and they mean the same thing:

> I **was cooking** **when** the phone **rang**.  
> **While** I **was cooking**, the phone **rang**.

And one shape that is wrong, which is the two halves put in the wrong clauses:

> ✗ *While I **cooked**, the phone **was ringing**.*

That sentence is not merely awkward. It says the ringing was the long background and the cooking was the brief event landing inside it, which is the opposite of what happened. The full inventory of time subordinators — [*when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#when), [*while*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#while), [*as*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#as), [*before*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#before), [*until*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#until) and the rest — is in the [conjunction dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md).

The comma rule is the ordinary one for fronted subordinate clauses, the same rule the [conditionals](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#the-comma-rule) run on: **comma when the time clause comes first, no comma when it comes second.**

> **While I was cooking,** the phone rang.  
> The phone rang **while I was cooking**.

### 3. Two actions in progress at once

Both actions were underway across the same stretch, and neither one interrupts the other. Both verbs go into the past continuous.

> **While** I **was cooking**, she **was setting** the table.  
> **While** he **was talking**, I **was watching** the clock.  
> I **was driving** and the kids **were fighting** in the back seat.  
> The dog **was barking**, the baby **was crying**, and the doorbell **was ringing**.  
> She **was reading** her email **while** he **was making** coffee.

*While* is the default connector here, and *as* works too. Note that this pattern uses two continuous verbs, which is exactly what the interruption pattern must not do — the tenses tell you which of the two relationships the sentence means:

> **While I was cooking, the phone rang.** — one brief event inside one long one  
> **While I was cooking, she was setting the table.** — two long ones side by side

One honest caveat: for two habitual or routine activities, the past simple in both clauses is also normal and means something slightly different. *While I cooked, she set the table* reads as the division of labor every night; *while I was cooking, she was setting the table* reads as one particular evening seen in progress.

### 4. Setting the scene in a narrative

A story does not begin with its first event. It begins with the world the first event lands in, and the past continuous is the tense that world is described in.

> The sun **was setting** over the water. Birds **were calling** from somewhere in the reeds, and a dog **was barking** two houses down. Nobody **was saying** anything.

Then the past simple arrives and the story starts moving:

> Then the screen door **slammed**, and my father **walked** out onto the porch.

That alternation is the engine of English narrative prose. **The past continuous holds the clock still and describes; the past simple moves the clock forward and narrates.** A paragraph built only out of past simple reads as a list of events with no world around them; a paragraph built only out of past continuous never gets anywhere.

> It **was raining** when we **pulled** into the lot. The wipers **were losing** the fight, the heater **was blowing** cold air, and somewhere under the hood something **was making** a noise it **had not made** that morning. I **turned** off the engine and **sat** there.

Count the forms in that passage: five continuous verbs describing one single moment, then two past simples that finally do something. This is also why openings and closings of scenes attract the continuous — *The band was playing when we got there*, *Everyone was leaving by the time I found my coat*.

### 5. Temporary situations in the past

A situation that was true for a limited stretch and is understood to have ended takes the continuous, where a permanent or long-term one takes the simple.

> I **was living** with my parents that spring.  
> He **was working** at a bookstore when I met him.  
> She **was teaching** in Prague for a year.  
> We **were renting** until the closing went through.  
> They **were using** my office while theirs was being painted.

Compare:

> My grandmother **lived** in that house for fifty years. — a fact about her life  
> My grandmother **was living** with us that winter. — a temporary arrangement

Neither sentence is more correct. The continuous adds *and then that stopped*, which is why it fits an arrangement and not a biography. Note also that the continuous here quietly supplies the frame that use 1 demanded: *He was working at a bookstore when I met him* has the meeting as its moment.

### 6. Changing and developing situations

Something was in the process of becoming something else. The verb is one of change, and the continuous says the change was still running.

> It **was getting** dark.  
> The kids **were growing** so fast that year.  
> Prices **were rising** faster than anyone had predicted.  
> The neighborhood **was changing**, and not slowly.  
> I **was beginning** to think you'd forgotten.  
> His hearing **was going**, though he'd never have admitted it.  
> The line **was getting** longer, not shorter.

The verbs that show up here are a small, recognizable set: *get*, *become*, *grow*, *rise*, *fall*, *improve*, *increase*, *decrease*, *change*, *turn*, *begin*, *start*, *go* (in *going bad*, *going gray*). With these verbs the past simple reports the change as finished — *it got dark* — and the continuous reports it as underway — *it was getting dark*, which is the version you want when something else is about to happen in that dusk.

### 7. *always*, *constantly*, *forever*: the annoyance pattern

With a frequency adverb of this kind, the past continuous stops describing a moment and starts making a complaint. The action was repeated, the speaker found it too much, and the tense is doing the editorializing.

> He **was always losing** his keys.  
> She **was constantly interrupting** me.  
> They **were forever asking** to borrow the car.  
> My roommate **was always leaving** dishes in the sink.  
> He **was continually reminding** everyone how busy he was.

The adverb goes **between the auxiliary and the participle**. ✗ *He always was losing his keys* is not how it is said.

The meaning is not frequency, and this is the part that needs stating outright. Compare:

> He **always lost** his keys. — a neutral statement of habit  
> He **was always losing** his keys. — an exasperated one

Both describe the same behavior. Only the second one has an attitude in it, and the attitude is carried by nothing but the aspect. The claim is also hyperbolic: *always* here means "too often," not "on every occasion," which is why *He was always losing his keys — well, twice a month* is not a contradiction.

The attitude follows the content rather than the grammar, so warmth works the same way:

> She **was always bringing** us cookies. — affectionate, not annoyed  
> He **was always offering** to drive people home.

The adverbs that do this are [*always*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/07-frequency-duration.md#always), [*constantly*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/07-frequency-duration.md#constantly), [*continually*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/07-frequency-duration.md#continually) and [*forever*](../../parts-of-speech/05-adverbs/catalog/07-frequency-duration.md#forever). *Continuously* is not one of them — it means "without a break," and *He was continuously losing his keys* describes an impossible situation.

### 8. Polite and tentative openings

The past continuous softens a request, and it softens it more than the past simple does. Nothing about these sentences is past.

> **I was wondering if** you could take a look at this.  
> **I was hoping** you'd say that.  
> **I was thinking** we might push the meeting to Thursday.  
> **I was going to ask** whether the offer is still open.  
> **We were wondering whether** you'd be free on Friday.

The politeness ladder runs the same way it does in a [second conditional](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#the-other-job-politeness), and for the same reason — distance from the here and now stands in for deference:

| Version | Register |
|---|---|
| *Can you look at this?* | direct |
| *Could you look at this?* | polite |
| *I wonder if you could look at this.* | tentative |
| ***I was wondering if** you could look at this.* | more tentative still |
| *I was wondering whether you might possibly be able to look at this.* | as soft as English gets |

The tense does not commit the speaker to anything. *I was wondering if you could help* does not report a completed episode of wondering; it is a live request wearing a past-tense coat. Note that the clause after it takes *could* rather than *can* — ✗ *I was wondering if you can help* mixes the registers and lands somewhere awkward.

One regional note, flagged because learners meet it and then get corrected for using it: ***I was wanting to ask you something*** is genuinely common in parts of the American South and Midland, where *want* takes the continuous freely. It is not general American English, it does not appear in edited writing, and elsewhere it sounds like an error. Use *I was hoping* or *I wanted to ask*.

### 9. Past arrangements, and the future in the past

The present continuous states a fixed arrangement — *I'm meeting him at six.* Put the auxiliary in the past and you get the same arrangement reported from a past vantage point.

> I **was meeting** him at six, so I left the office early.  
> We **were flying** out on Sunday, but the airline canceled.  
> She **was starting** the new job in March.  
> They **were getting** married in the fall — at least, that was the plan.

Notice how often a *but* follows. Like *was going to*, this pattern leans toward a plan that did not come off, and speakers exploit that: *I was meeting him at six* invites *…and then he canceled* in a way that *I met him at six* does not.

This is one member of a family of forms that describe the future as it looked from a point in the past — *was going to*, *was to*, *was about to*, and *would* — and the family has its own treatment. **See [section 9](09-the-future.md#the-future-in-the-past) for the future in the past in full.**

### And one that is not a use: reported speech

A past continuous frequently turns up in a report not because the speaker chose it but because [backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md) produced it. The present continuous drops one rung and lands here:

> "I**'m waiting** outside," he said.  →  He said he **was waiting** outside.

The past continuous itself drops to the past perfect continuous when the report is fully shifted — *He said he **had been** waiting outside* — though in ordinary American speech it very often just stays put. That row, and the reasons for leaving it alone, are in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md#past-continuous--past-perfect-continuous).

## The contrast with the past simple

Everything above can be compressed into a single choice made over and over: is this event a dot or is it a frame? Here is that choice worked through in the five places it actually comes up.

### Interrupted versus completed

> I **was reading** when she called. — the reading was underway; it may have continued  
> I **read** for an hour and then went to bed. — the reading is reported as a finished block

> She **was crossing** the street when the cab hit her. — she never got across  
> She **crossed** the street and went into the bank. — she got across

That second pair is the clearest demonstration the tense offers, because the two sentences make opposite claims about the same event. The continuous reports an action underway and says nothing about its completion; the simple reports the action as an accomplished whole. Two more of the same kind:

> He **was drowning** when the lifeguard reached him. — and was pulled out  
> He **drowned** in 1974. — he died

> They **were persuading** her to sign. — the outcome is open  
> They **persuaded** her to sign. — she signed

### Background versus foreground

In a narrative the two tenses divide the labor without being asked to. The past simple carries the plot; the past continuous carries everything the plot is happening in.

> The band **was playing**, people **were dancing**, and someone **was shouting** over the music. Then the lights **went** out.

Three continuous verbs for the scene, one past simple for the event. Reverse the tenses and the paragraph stops making sense:

> ✗ *The band **played**, people **danced**, and someone **shouted** over the music. Then the lights **were going** out.*

That version reads as four consecutive events, the last of which takes a strangely long time. Nothing in it is ungrammatical; it is simply about something else.

### *When I arrived, she was cooking* versus *When I arrived, she cooked*

Change nothing but the tense of the second verb and the order of events flips.

| Sentence | What happened |
|---|---|
| *When I arrived, she **was cooking**.* | The cooking started **before** I got there and was still going. |
| *When I arrived, she **cooked**.* | The cooking started **after** I got there. My arrival came first. |
| *When I arrived, she **had cooked**.* | The cooking was **finished** before I got there. |

This is worth sitting with, because it is the single most useful thing the past continuous does and the thing learners most often lose. Three tenses, three positions of one event relative to another:

```
                         I arrived
                             │
   was cooking      ▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬●▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬      in progress, before and after
   cooked                    ●───▬▬▬▬▬       started afterward
   had cooked       ▬▬▬▬▬●               │   over before I got there
```

The past simple version of the pair carries a faint implication of consequence — *When I arrived, she cooked* sounds like she cooked **because** I arrived — which is the natural reading of two past simples joined by *when*. The past perfect belongs to [section 7](07-past-perfect.md); it is in the table only so the three are not confused with each other.

A second pair of the same shape:

> **When the meeting ended, everyone was leaving.** — they were already on their way out  
> **When the meeting ended, everyone left.** — the ending is what made them go

### Duration versus point

A past continuous can carry a duration phrase, and this is where the "long action" description of the tense turns out to be too crude. Both of these are correct:

> We **talked** for two hours.  
> We **were talking** for two hours.

The first states a fact about the conversation: it lasted two hours and it is over. The second offers those two hours as a stretch that something else can be located in or measured against — *We were talking for two hours before anyone mentioned the money*. On its own, without such a follow-up, the second sentence often carries a note of complaint, which is the same editorializing the [*always* pattern](#7-always-constantly-forever-the-annoyance-pattern) does.

Length by itself decides nothing:

> I **slept** for ten hours. — long, and the past simple is the natural tense  
> I **blinked**. — instantaneous, past simple  
> I **was blinking** back tears. — instantaneous action, repeated, seen from inside

The continuous is not the tense of long events. It is the tense of events viewed from the inside, and any event at all can be viewed that way if the sentence gives a reason to.

### The pairs, side by side

| Past simple | Past continuous |
|---|---|
| *I **read** a book last night.* — got through it | *I **was reading** a book last night.* — maybe not finished |
| *When I arrived, she **cooked**.* — after | *When I arrived, she **was cooking**.* — already underway |
| *He **drowned**.* — he died | *He **was drowning**.* — someone got to him |
| ***What did you do?*** — what happened | ***What were you doing?*** — what were you busy with |
| *She **crossed** the road.* — she reached the other side | *She **was crossing** the road when it happened.* — she didn't |
| *I **lived** in Austin for six years.* — a fact of my life | *I **was living** in Austin that summer.* — a temporary setup |
| *The bell **rang** and we **left**.* — two ticks of the clock | *The bell **was ringing** as we left.* — one framing the other |
| *He **spoke** to the reporters.* — the event | *He **was speaking** to the reporters when the news broke.* — the frame |
| *It **got** dark.* — the change completed | *It **was getting** dark.* — the change underway |
| *He **always lost** his keys.* — a habit | *He **was always losing** his keys.* — a complaint |
| *I **wondered** whether you could help.* — I genuinely did | *I **was wondering** whether you could help.* — a polite request |
| *I **worked** all day Saturday.* — a completed stretch | *I **was working** all day Saturday.* — the day as a backdrop |

**The two-question test.** When you are unsure which tense a sentence needs:

1. **Is there a moment — a clock time, another verb, a described scene — that falls inside the action?** If yes, the past continuous.
2. **Is the action itself the event being reported, or one of a sequence of events?** If yes, the past simple.

If both are true, the sentence probably has two clauses and needs one of each. The past simple's own full treatment, including the irregular forms and the *used to* / *would* habitual patterns, is [section 5](05-past-simple.md).

## State verbs

The past continuous is barred from the same verbs the present continuous is barred from, and for the same reason: **a state has no inside to be in the middle of.**

> ✗ *I **was knowing** the answer.* → ✓ *I **knew** the answer.*  
> ✗ *She **was owning** three restaurants then.* → ✓ *She **owned** three restaurants then.*  
> ✗ *We **were needing** more time.* → ✓ *We **needed** more time.*  
> ✗ *It **was meaning** something different back then.* → ✓ *It **meant** something different back then.*

The core list is short and worth having by heart: *know, believe, understand, realize, remember, forget, want, need, prefer, like, love, hate, own, belong, contain, consist, include, mean, seem, cost, weigh, deserve, matter*.

Several verbs have a stative sense that refuses the continuous and a dynamic sense that welcomes it. The split is exactly the one the present continuous has, one step back in time:

| Verb | Stative — past simple | Dynamic — past continuous |
|---|---|---|
| *think* | *I **thought** you were joking.* — an opinion | *I **was thinking** about quitting.* — mental activity |
| *have* | *We **had** two cars then.* — possession | *We **were having** dinner when he called.* — an activity |
| *see* | *I **saw** the problem right away.* — perceived | *She **was seeing** someone from work.* — dating |
| *taste* | *The soup **tasted** burnt.* — a property | *He **was tasting** the sauce.* — an action |
| *smell* | *The kitchen **smelled** like smoke.* | *The dog **was smelling** my shoes.* |
| *look* | *She **looked** exhausted.* — appeared | *He **was looking** at the map.* — directed his eyes |
| *feel* | *I **felt** sick.* — both are normal | *I **was feeling** sick.* — both are normal |
| *weigh* | *The box **weighed** forty pounds.* | *She **was weighing** the options.* |
| *be* | *He **was** rude.* — that is his character | *He **was being** rude.* — that is how he acted that day |

That last row earns a paragraph of its own. ***Was being*** puts a personality trait on temporary duty:

> He **was** difficult. — he is a difficult person  
> He **was being** difficult. — that afternoon, he chose to be

> You **were** very patient with her. — a compliment about you  
> You **were being** very patient with her. — a compliment about that conversation

**The full stative-verb table, with the dozen borderline cases and the American usage notes, is in [section 2](02-present-continuous.md#state-verbs-and-the-continuous).** It is not repeated here because nothing in it changes when the tense moves into the past — the constraint is a fact about the verbs, not about the tense.

One exception worth flagging so it is not mistaken for an error. With a stative verb and an adverb of change, the continuous is sometimes exactly right, because what is being described is not the state but the movement into it: *I **was beginning** to understand*, *The house **was becoming** a problem*, *She **was starting** to matter to him.* The continuous is on *begin*, *become*, and *start* — all dynamic — and the stative verb sits safely in an infinitive after them.

## The passive

*Was* or *were* + ***being*** + the past participle. Three words, and the middle one is what makes it continuous.

> The house **was being painted** when we went to see it.  
> Two of the machines **were being repaired**.  
> I could tell I **was being watched**.  
> The bridge **was being resurfaced** all that summer.

Negatives and questions behave exactly as they do in the active — the first auxiliary carries everything:

> The car **wasn't being used**, so we took it.  
> **Was** the road **being resurfaced** when you drove through?  
> **Weren't** those files **being reviewed** by legal?

The contrast with the past simple passive is the same contrast as everywhere else in this file:

| Form | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| past simple passive | *The house **was painted** last year.* | the event, complete |
| past continuous passive | *The house **was being painted** last year.* | in progress across that stretch |

*Was painted* is an event with a date; *was being painted* is a frame with something else due to arrive inside it. Watch what the difference does to a whole sentence: *The car was repaired while I waited* says the repair finished; *The car was being repaired while I waited* says only that the work was going on.

This is a heavy form — three auxiliary-ish words before the content arrives — and good writers use it sparingly, recasting into the active where the agent is worth naming. It is also the deepest the passive goes in this direction: there is no passive of the past perfect continuous, so ✗ *The house had been being painted* is not a sentence anyone writes.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **was knowing** the answer.* | *I **knew** the answer.* | A state has no inside. State verbs refuse the continuous in every tense. |
| ✗ *I **was having** a headache.* | *I **had** a headache.* | *Have* is dynamic only when it means an activity — *having dinner*, *having a meeting*. |
| ✗ *While I **cooked**, the phone **was ringing**.* | *While I **was cooking**, the phone **rang**.* | The two halves are in the wrong clauses. *While* takes the long action; the short one goes in the past simple. |
| ✗ *I **was go** to the store.* | *I **was going** to the store.* | The auxiliary takes the *-ing* form, never the base form. |
| ✗ *I **reading** when you called.* | *I **was reading** when you called.* | The auxiliary is not optional. Russian «я читал» is one word; English needs two. |
| ✗ *Was you at home?* | ***Were** you at home?* | *You* takes *were* in the singular as well as the plural. |
| ✗ ***What you were doing?*** | ***What were you doing?*** | The auxiliary inverts with the subject in a *wh*-question. |
| ✗ *Did you were waiting?* | ***Were** you waiting?* | The tense has its own auxiliary. No *do*-support. |
| ✗ *When I **was arriving**, she was cooking.* | *When I **arrived**, she was cooking.* | An arrival is a point. Two continuous verbs make it two simultaneous activities. |
| ✗ *Every day I **was going** to school.* | *Every day I **went** to school.* / *I **used to go**…* | A habit is not an action in progress. This is the over-correction that follows learning the tense. |
| ✗ *I **was sitting** on the sofa and suddenly the light **was going** out.* | *…and suddenly the light **went** out.* | *Suddenly* marks a point event. Points take the past simple. |
| ✗ *He **always was losing** his keys.* | *He **was always losing** his keys.* | The adverb goes between the auxiliary and the participle. |
| ✗ ***We're** waiting for an hour yesterday.* | ***We were** waiting for an hour yesterday.* | *We're* is the present. The past has no affirmative contraction. |
| ✗ *I was wondering if you **can** help.* | *I was wondering if you **could** help.* | The tentative opening takes a tentative modal. Mixing the two registers sounds odd. |
| ✗ *It was raining, so I **was taking** an umbrella.* | *It was raining, so I **took** an umbrella.* | Taking the umbrella is the event, not the background. |
| ✗ *The house **was painting**.* | *The house **was being painted**.* | The house is not doing the painting. The continuous passive needs *being* + the participle. |
| ✗ *Yesterday I **was reading**, then I **was watching** TV, then I **was going** to bed.* | *Yesterday I **read**, then I **watched** TV, then I **went** to bed.* | A sequence of events is a chain of dots, and dots take the past simple. |

## For Russian speakers

### One Russian past covers two English ones

Russian marks **aspect** on the verb itself, and it marks it everywhere, in every tense. English marks it with an auxiliary, and only sometimes. The two systems overlap enough to be dangerous.

| Russian | What it covers in English |
|---|---|
| **Imperfective past** — «читал», «готовил», «шёл» | the past simple **and** the past continuous **and** *used to* |
| **Perfective past** — «прочитал», «приготовил», «пришёл» | the past simple, and essentially nothing else |

The perfective side is easy. «Я прочитал книгу» is *I read the book*, finished, and no other tense is in play.

The imperfective side is where the trouble is, because one Russian form has to be sorted into two English ones and Russian gives the speaker no reason to notice the difference:

> «Я **читал** книгу.» → *I **read** a book.* / *I **was reading** a book.* / *I **used to read** books.*

All three are legitimate translations. Context decides, and Russian leaves it to context — which means the learner has no habit of making the choice, and defaults to whichever English form came first, which is nearly always the past simple.

**The practical consequence: Russian speakers systematically under-use the past continuous and over-use the past simple.** The sentences they produce are not ungrammatical. They are just thinner than what they meant — *When you called I cooked dinner* instead of *When you called I was cooking dinner*, which says something else entirely.

### The test: was it in progress at that moment?

One question, asked of every imperfective past verb before it becomes English:

**Is there a moment — a clock time, another event, a described scene — that falls inside this action?**

If yes, the past continuous. If the action *is* the event, or is a habit, or is a whole finished stretch with nothing landing in it, the past simple.

A second form of the same test, which some people find easier: **can you put *in the middle of* in front of it?** In Russian, «был в процессе» or «был занят тем, что».

> *When he called I was **in the middle of** cooking.* → ✓ past continuous  
> ✗ *Every Sunday I was **in the middle of** cooking.* → nobody is in the middle of every Sunday → past simple

### Parallel pairs

| Russian | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| «Когда ты позвонил, я **готовил** ужин.» | *When you called, I **was cooking** dinner.* | imperfective with a point inside it |
| «Когда ты позвонил, я **приготовил** ужин.» | *When you called, I **cooked** dinner.* | perfective — the cooking came after the call |
| «В восемь я **ужинал**.» | *At eight I **was having** dinner.* | a clock time lands inside the action |
| «Вчера я **читал** весь вечер.» | *I **read** all evening.* | a complete stretch, nothing inside it |
| «Каждое утро я **ходил** в спортзал.» | *Every morning I **went** to the gym.* / *I **used to go**…* | habitual imperfective — **not** the continuous |
| «**Шёл** дождь.» | *It **was raining**.* | a scene, not an event |
| «Пока я **готовил**, она **накрывала** на стол.» | *While I **was cooking**, she **was setting** the table.* | two long actions side by side |
| «Он постоянно **терял** ключи.» | *He **was always losing** his keys.* | repeated + exasperated |
| «Я **хотел** вас спросить…» | *I **was wondering**…* | the past used for politeness in both languages |
| «Она **работала** в книжном, когда мы познакомились.» | *She **was working** at a bookstore when we met.* | a temporary situation with a moment in it |
| «Она **проработала** там десять лет.» | *She **worked** there for ten years.* | perfective, a closed stretch |
| «**Темнело**.» | *It **was getting** dark.* | a change still underway |

### Where Russian aspect actually helps

For once, the interference runs partly in the learner's favor. Russian aspect already encodes almost exactly the distinction English is asking for in the interruption pattern:

> «Когда я **пришёл**, она **готовила**.» — perfective + imperfective  
> *When I **arrived**, she **was cooking**.* — past simple + past continuous

The two sentences line up verb for verb. A Russian speaker already knows, without being told, that «пришёл» is a point and «готовила» is a stretch; the only thing to learn is which English forms carry those two jobs. That is a much smaller task than the one an English speaker faces going the other way, and it is worth knowing you have the advantage.

### Three traps

**1. The habitual imperfective.** This is the over-correction that arrives about a week after the tense is learned. Russian uses the imperfective for habits, the learner has just learned that imperfective often means continuous, and the result is:

> ✗ *Every summer we **were going** to the lake.*  
> ✓ *Every summer we **went** to the lake.* / *We **used to go** to the lake every summer.*

The continuous is about one stretch of time seen from inside, not about repetition. A habit repeated over twenty years is twenty dots, not one stretch.

**2. The missing auxiliary.** Russian «я читал» is a single word, and nothing in it corresponds to *was*. The English sentence needs two words, and the first one is the one that gets dropped:

> ✗ *I **reading** when you called.*  
> ✗ *She **cooking** dinner at the time.*  
> ✓ *I **was reading** when you called.*

This one is reinforced by the sound: the unstressed *was* is /wəz/ and the unstressed *were* is /wɚ/, so the word the learner is failing to produce is also the word they are failing to hear.

**3. «когда» doing the work of both *when* and *while*.** Russian has «когда» and «пока», and the division is not the English one. «Когда» maps onto *when*, which is the flexible conjunction and always safe, so the trap is not that *when* gets used too much. The trap is *while*: it takes the **long** action, always, and a learner reaching for it as a stylistic variant of *when* produces

> ✗ *While the phone **rang**, I **was cooking**.*

when they meant *While I was cooking, the phone rang*. If you are not sure which clause is which, use *when* and put the short event after it. It is never wrong.

## Quick reference

**The form.** *was* / *were* + *-ing*. *Was* for I, he, she, it. *Were* for you, we, they — including singular *you*.

**The idea.** A moment in the past, seen from inside the action that was going on at it.

**The decision, in two steps.**

```
   Does a moment — a clock time, another verb, a scene — fall INSIDE the action?
       │
       ├── YES ── Is the verb a state verb (know, want, own, mean, belong)?
       │            │
       │            ├── yes .... use the PAST SIMPLE anyway. States have no inside.
       │            └── no ..... PAST CONTINUOUS
       │
       └── NO ─── Is it a habit, a sequence of events, or a closed stretch?
                    │
                    └── yes .... PAST SIMPLE
```

**The interruption pattern, both orders.**

> I **was cooking** when the phone **rang**.  
> **While** I **was cooking**, the phone **rang**.

**The four sentences to remember.**

> **At eight I was eating dinner.** — a moment inside an action

> **I was cooking when the phone rang.** — long frame, short event

> **While I was cooking, she was setting the table.** — two frames at once

> **He was always losing his keys.** — repeated, and the speaker is tired of it

**Five rules that cover most of the errors.**

1. **The short event takes the past simple; the long one takes the past continuous.** *While* always introduces the long one.
2. **State verbs stay simple.** *Knew*, *wanted*, *owned* — not ✗ *was knowing*.
3. **A sequence of events is a chain of past simples.** The continuous does not advance a story.
4. **A habit is not an action in progress.** *Every day I went*, not ✗ *was going*.
5. **The auxiliary is not optional and never *do*.** *Were you waiting?*, not ✗ *Did you waiting?* or ✗ *You waiting?*

## Practice

**A. Put each verb in brackets into the past simple or the past continuous.**

1. I (watch) TV when the doorbell (ring).
2. While she (drive) home, she (hear) the news on the radio.
3. At six o'clock yesterday we (still / sit) in the conference room.
4. When I (get) to the party, everyone (dance).
5. He (break) his arm while he (ski) in Colorado.
6. Every Saturday that winter, we (drive) up to the lake.
7. It (get) dark, so we (turn) on the headlights.
8. She (not / listen), so I (say) it again.
9. What (you / do) when the lights (go) out?
10. The kids (fight) in the back seat the whole way there.
11. I (know) the answer, but I (not / want) to say it out loud.
12. While I (make) coffee, he (read) the paper.
13. She (work) at a bookstore when I (meet) her.
14. My roommate (always / leave) dishes in the sink.
15. The house (paint) when we (go) to look at it. (passive)

**B. One of the two versions is wrong. Say which, and why.**

16. *While I cooked, the phone was ringing.* / *While I was cooking, the phone rang.*
17. *When I arrived, she was cooking.* / *When I was arriving, she was cooking.*
18. *I was wondering if you can help.* / *I was wondering if you could help.*
19. *He was having a headache all morning.* / *He had a headache all morning.*
20. *Every summer we were going to the lake.* / *Every summer we went to the lake.*

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | I **was watching** TV when the doorbell **rang**. | long frame, short event |
| 2 | While she **was driving** home, she **heard** the news. | *while* takes the long action |
| 3 | At six o'clock yesterday we **were still sitting** in the conference room. | a clock time inside the action |
| 4 | When I **got** to the party, everyone **was dancing**. | the dancing had already started |
| 5 | He **broke** his arm while he **was skiing** in Colorado. | the break is the point, the skiing the frame |
| 6 | Every Saturday that winter, we **drove** up to the lake. | a habit, not an action in progress |
| 7 | It **was getting** dark, so we **turned** on the headlights. | a change underway, then an event |
| 8 | She **wasn't listening**, so I **said** it again. | background, then event |
| 9 | What **were** you **doing** when the lights **went** out? | inversion; the outage is the point |
| 10 | The kids **were fighting** in the back seat the whole way there. | a stretch offered as a backdrop — *fought* is also possible and flatter |
| 11 | I **knew** the answer, but I **didn't want** to say it out loud. | both are state verbs |
| 12 | While I **was making** coffee, he **was reading** the paper. | two simultaneous actions |
| 13 | She **was working** at a bookstore when I **met** her. | a temporary situation with a moment in it |
| 14 | My roommate **was always leaving** dishes in the sink. | the annoyance pattern; the adverb sits inside the verb phrase |
| 15 | The house **was being painted** when we **went** to look at it. | continuous passive: *was* + *being* + participle |
| 16 | ***While I was cooking, the phone rang.*** | The other version puts the short event after *while* and the long one in the continuous — backwards. |
| 17 | ***When I arrived, she was cooking.*** | An arrival is a point. *Was arriving* would make it a second simultaneous activity. |
| 18 | ***I was wondering if you could help.*** | A tentative opening takes a tentative modal. *Can* clashes with it. |
| 19 | ***He had a headache all morning.*** | *Have* is stative here. It takes the continuous only when it means an activity. |
| 20 | ***Every summer we went to the lake.*** | A habit is a row of dots, not one stretch. *Used to go* also works. |
