# The past perfect

> *Had* + past participle — the tense that says one past event came before another, why a single past event almost never needs it, and the *'d* that is sometimes *had* and sometimes *would*.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The past perfect is made of two pieces you already have: **had**, which is the past tense of *have*, and the **past participle**, which is the third form of the verb — *gone*, *eaten*, *written*, *finished*, *seen*. Put them together and you get *had gone*, *had eaten*, *had written*, *had finished*, *had seen*. There is no agreement to get right and no second form to learn; *had* is *had* for every subject in the language.

The difficulty is not the form. It is knowing **when a sentence needs one**, and that question has a single answer, which is worth stating before anything else.

**The past perfect is relative. It does not say when something happened — it says that it happened before something else that is already in the past.** Remove that second past point and the past perfect has nothing to measure against, and the sentence goes strange.

> ✗ *I **had eaten** breakfast this morning.* — earlier than what?  
> ✓ *I **ate** breakfast this morning.*  
> ✓ *I **had eaten** breakfast, so I wasn't hungry at the meeting.* — earlier than the meeting

Everything below is a consequence of that one property, including the reason over-use is the most common error learners make with this tense once they discover it.

## The form

### The paradigm

*Had* does not inflect. The same word serves every person and number, which makes this the simplest paradigm in the English verb system.

| Subject | Affirmative | Contracted | Negative | Contracted negative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I **had** finished | I**'d** finished | I **had not** finished | I **hadn't** finished |
| you | you **had** finished | you**'d** finished | you **had not** finished | you **hadn't** finished |
| he | he **had** finished | he**'d** finished | he **had not** finished | he **hadn't** finished |
| she | she **had** finished | she**'d** finished | she **had not** finished | she **hadn't** finished |
| it | it **had** finished | it**'d** finished | it **had not** finished | it **hadn't** finished |
| we | we **had** finished | we**'d** finished | we **had not** finished | we **hadn't** finished |
| they | they **had** finished | they**'d** finished | they **had not** finished | they **hadn't** finished |
| there | there **had** been | there**'d** been | there **had not** been | there **hadn't** been |

Compare the present perfect, which has two forms — *I have finished*, *he has finished* — and the past simple of an irregular verb, which has one form but a different one for every verb. The past perfect asks you to remember only the participle.

### The participle is the third form, not the past

This is where the errors in the form come from, and they are all the same error.

> ✗ *I had **went** home.*  ✓ *I had **gone** home.*  
> ✗ *She had **took** the car.*  ✓ *She had **taken** the car.*  
> ✗ *We had **ate** already.*  ✓ *We had **eaten** already.*  
> ✗ *He had **saw** it before.*  ✓ *He had **seen** it before.*

For regular verbs the two forms are spelled the same — *worked*, *worked* — so nothing can go wrong. For the three-form irregulars it can, and does. The complete list, grouped by how the three forms change, is the [verb dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/README.md); [*go*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/10-participle-base-plus-n.md#go) and [*eat*](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#eat) are the two that get broken most often.

### Affirmative

> By the time we **got** there, the food **had run** out.  
> She **had written** three novels before anyone published one.  
> The train **had already left** when I reached the platform.  
> They **had lived** in Tulsa for nine years before the move.

### Negative

*Not* goes after *had*, exactly as it goes after any auxiliary. There is no *do*-support and never has been.

> I **hadn't heard** about it until Tuesday.  
> She **had not told** anyone, not even her sister.  
> We **hadn't been** to that part of the state before.  
> He **hadn't finished** the report, so the meeting was short.

The uncontracted *had not* is the formal or emphatic version — *I had not been told* carries weight that *I hadn't been told* does not. In speech, *hadn't* is what people say.

### Questions

Invert *had* and the subject. Nothing else moves.

> **Had** you **met** him before that night?  
> **Had** the store **closed** by the time you got there?  
> **What had** she **done** to make him so angry?  
> **How long had** they **been** married when he wrote it?  
> **Who had** she **told**?

Note the last one. When the *wh*-word is the subject, there is no inversion — *Who had told her?* is subject position, *Who had she told?* is object position, and the difference is visible only in the word order after *had*.

In real conversation, straight past perfect questions are less frequent than the textbooks suggest. Americans very often ask the question in the past simple and let the context carry the ordering: *Did you meet him before that night?* is more likely than *Had you met him before that night?* The past perfect question survives best after *by then*, *before that*, and *already*.

### Negative questions

The contracted form fronts *hadn't*. The uncontracted form leaves *not* after the subject, and it is noticeably formal.

> **Hadn't** she **told** you?  
> **Hadn't** they **fixed** that already?  
> **Had** she **not told** you? — formal, or pointed  
> **Why hadn't** anyone **called** the police?

A negative question here does what negative questions always do: it carries the speaker's expectation. *Hadn't she told you?* means the speaker assumed she had, and is surprised.

### Short answers

The short answer repeats *had*, and *had* cannot be contracted when it is the last word — the same rule that rules out ✗ *Yes, I'm*.

> "**Had** you ever flown before?" — "**Yes, I had.**" / "**No, I hadn't.**"  
> "**Had** they left?" — "**Yes, they had.**" / "**No, they hadn't.**"

> ✗ *Yes, I**'d**.*  ✓ *Yes, I **had**.*

Tag questions work off the same auxiliary: *She'd already gone, **hadn't she**?* — *You hadn't met before, **had you**?*

### *Had had*

When the lexical verb is *have*, the result is *had had*, and it is correct, ordinary, and not a typo.

> By noon I **had had** three cups of coffee.  
> She **had had** the same car since college.  
> We **hadn't had** any trouble with it until that winter.

The first *had* is the auxiliary, the second is the main verb. There is no ✗ *had had had*: the past perfect is the bottom of the English tense ladder, and it does not stack.

### Contractions, and the *'d* problem

Contracted *'d* attaches to every pronoun and to *there*, *who*, and *that*. It is what speech uses almost exclusively.

> **I'd** never seen anything like it.  
> **He'd** forgotten his own birthday.  
> **There'd** been a fire in the building the year before.  
> **Who'd** taken the keys?

And now the trap. **Written *'d* is two different words.** It is *had*, and it is also *would*, and nothing in the apostrophe tells you which. What tells you is the word that comes next.

| What follows *'d* | The word is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a **past participle** | *had* | *He**'d** **finished**.* = he had finished |
| a **bare infinitive** | *would* | *He**'d** **finish**.* = he would finish |
| *have* + participle | *would* | *He**'d have** finished.* = he would have finished |
| *been* | *had* | *She**'d been** waiting.* = she had been waiting |
| *be* | *would* | *She**'d be** waiting.* = she would be waiting |
| *had* | *had* | *I**'d had** enough.* = I had had enough |
| *better* | *had* — frozen | *You**'d better** go.* = you had better go |
| *rather* | *would* — frozen | *I**'d rather** not.* = I would rather not |

Two pairs are worth saying out loud, because they differ by one letter and mean entirely different things:

> **I'd liked** the first draft. — past perfect: I had liked it, at some earlier point  
> **I'd like** the first draft. — a polite request: I would like it

> **They'd left** by six. — they had left  
> **They'd leave** by six. — they would leave, habitually or hypothetically

The negative contractions are not ambiguous at all, which is a small mercy: *hadn't* is only *had not*, and *wouldn't* is only *would not*. And there is no contraction of *had* in a short answer, as above.

One more, because it is the single most common American misspelling in the neighborhood: ✗ ***I'd of known***. What is being misheard is *I'd have known* — *would have*, reduced to /ˈwʊdəv/. *Of* is not a verb and never has been. The full inventory of these forms, with pronunciations and the ambiguity flagged on each entry, is the [contractions dictionary](../02-contractions/catalog/README.md) — see [*I'd*](../02-contractions/catalog/02-have-and-had.md#id) and [*he'd*](../02-contractions/catalog/02-have-and-had.md#hed).

### The passive

*Had been* + past participle. The auxiliary chain gets one link longer and nothing else changes.

> The room **had been cleaned** before we got there.  
> By the time the story ran, the charges **had been dropped**.  
> She **hadn't been told** that the meeting was canceled.  
> **Had** the contract **been signed** before he left?  
> Nothing **had been decided**, which is why the memo was so vague.

A past perfect continuous passive — ✗ *had been being repaired* — is grammatically derivable and effectively never written. English recasts instead: *they had been repairing it*, or *it had been under repair*.

### Continuous

*Had been* + `-ing` is the **past perfect continuous**, and it puts the emphasis on the duration or the ongoing nature of the earlier event rather than its completion.

> She **had been waiting** for almost an hour when he finally showed up.  
> They **had been arguing** about it for months before anyone else noticed.

The continuous has its own section — [the past perfect continuous](08-past-perfect-continuous.md) — and everything in this file about *when* to use a past perfect applies to it unchanged. The choice between the two is a question of aspect, not of time.

## The core idea

### Two points, not one

An ordinary past tense measures from **now**. *I left* means the leaving is before this moment, and that is all it claims.

The past perfect measures from **a point in the past**, and that point has to come from somewhere else in the sentence, the paragraph, or the situation. So a past perfect sentence always involves three moments, not two:

```
        the earlier event        the past reference point        now
   ---------•----------------------------•------------------------|--->
         had left                     I arrived
        past perfect                 past simple
```

*When I arrived, she had left.* The arriving is the reference point; the leaving is placed before it. The past simple does the anchoring and the past perfect does the stepping back, which is why the two tenses nearly always appear together.

### It is not "a long time ago"

The most persistent misunderstanding about this tense is that it means *very* past — the deep past, the distant past. It does not. The past perfect is about **order**, not distance.

> The Romans **built** roads all over Europe. — two thousand years ago, past simple, no perfect in sight  
> I **had** just **hung up** when the phone rang again. — four seconds earlier, past perfect

The first sentence has no second past point, so however ancient the event is, the past simple is the only option. The second has one — *the phone rang* — and the gap between the two events is a few seconds. Age has nothing to do with it.

### Which is why a single past event almost never needs it

Say something happened. Say when. Use the past simple.

> ✓ *I **graduated** in 2014.*  ✗ *I **had graduated** in 2014.*  
> ✓ *She **called** me yesterday.*  ✗ *She **had called** me yesterday.*  
> ✓ *We **moved** to Denver last spring.*  ✗ *We **had moved** to Denver last spring.*

None of those is ungrammatical in isolation — each one could be made to work inside a larger past narrative with a reference point in it. Standing alone they are wrong, and they are wrong in a specific way: they leave the reader waiting for a second clause that never arrives. *I had graduated in 2014…* and then what?

**The test is a question you can ask of any sentence: earlier than what?** If you can point at the answer — another past event, stated or obvious from the context — the past perfect is doing work. If you cannot, delete it.

### The reference point can be implied

It does not have to be in the same sentence. Context supplies it constantly, and this is why the past perfect is so much more common in narrative writing than in conversation.

> She opened the door and stopped. Someone **had been** in the apartment. Books **had been pulled** off the shelves; the desk drawers **hung** open.

The reference point is *she opened the door*, two sentences earlier. Everything after it steps back to fill in what came before. Notice the last clause reverting to the past simple — *hung* — once the flashback frame is established: that is normal practice, and stacking *had* onto every verb of a long backstory is one of the things that makes learner prose read as heavy. Two or three past perfects set the frame, and the rest of the passage can stay in the past simple.

## What it is used for

### 1. The earlier of two past events

The core case, and the one every other use is a version of.

> By the time we **arrived**, the movie **had** already **started**.  
> He **couldn't** get in because he **had lost** his key.  
> The ground **was** wet — it **had rained** overnight.  
> She **recognized** him at once, though they **hadn't met** in twenty years.  
> When the police **got** there, the driver **had disappeared**.  
> I **didn't** recognize him. He **had shaved** his beard.  
> The restaurant **was** empty by nine; the dinner rush **had ended** early.

In each pair, the past simple is the anchor and the past perfect is the thing that came first. Note that **the order of the clauses on the page has nothing to do with the order of the events** — *He couldn't get in because he had lost his key* puts the later event first, and the tense is what sorts it out.

### 2. With *after*, *before*, *when*, *by the time*, *as soon as*

These are the conjunctions the past perfect lives among, and they do not all behave the same way. The dividing question is whether **the conjunction itself already tells you the order**.

| Conjunction | Does it state the order? | Is the past perfect needed? |
|---|---|---|
| [*after*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#after) | yes — its clause is earlier | **optional** |
| [*before*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#before) | yes — its clause is later | **optional** |
| [*by the time*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#by-the-time) | yes — its clause is later | **strongly preferred** in the main clause |
| [*as soon as*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#as-soon-as) / [*once*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#once) | yes — its clause is earlier | **optional** |
| [*when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#when) | **no** | **often obligatory** |
| [*until*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#until) | yes — sets a boundary | optional |

#### *After* and *before*: the word has already done the job

*After* means its clause came first. *Before* means its clause came second. The order is in the conjunction, so the tense is not carrying any information, and English lets you drop it.

> **After** she **had finished** the dishes, she went to bed.  
> **After** she **finished** the dishes, she went to bed. — identical meaning

> He left **before** I **had said** a word.  
> He left **before** I **said** a word. — identical meaning

Both versions are standard American English. The past perfect is slightly more formal and slightly more deliberate about the completion; in speech the past simple is more common after *after*, and much more common after *before*. Neither is a mistake, and an editor will not change one to the other.

One genuine difference is worth having. With *after*, the past perfect can insist that the first thing was **completely finished** before the second started:

> **After** I **had read** the contract, I signed it. — read all of it, then signed  
> **After** I **read** the contract, I signed it. — the same, less insistent

And *before* has a use of its own, which looks backwards until you see the logic. The clause after *before* is the **later** event, so a past perfect there is not marking anteriority at all — it is marking something that never got as far as happening:

> She was gone **before** I **had** a chance to say goodbye.  
> The engine died **before** we **had gone** ten miles.  
> He was transferred **before** he **had learned** anyone's name.

These mean "before the point at which it would have been true that…" The past perfect is there to make the non-event vivid, and dropping it — *before I had a chance* → *before I got a chance* — requires rewording rather than just changing the tense.

#### *When*: this is where it matters

*When* says nothing whatever about order. It puts two events in the same neighborhood of time and leaves you to work out the sequence — and the only thing that can do that work is the tense. **This is the one conjunction where the past perfect is doing real, non-redundant, meaning-bearing work.**

> **When I arrived, she left.** — she left after I got there. I saw her go.  
> **When I arrived, she had left.** — she was already gone. I never saw her.

Two sentences, one word of difference, opposite stories. A few more pairs, because the mechanism is worth over-learning:

> **When the police arrived, the thief ran off.** — he was still there; he ran when they showed up  
> **When the police arrived, the thief had run off.** — he was long gone

> **When we got to the theater, they turned off the lights.** — we made it in time  
> **When we got to the theater, they had turned off the lights.** — we missed the opening

> **When I called, she was making dinner.** — in progress  
> **When I called, she had made dinner.** — it was already done

And the past perfect can just as easily sit in the *when*-clause, in which case *when* comes to mean *after*:

> **When I had washed the dishes, I went to bed.** — the washing came first  
> **When I had finished the report, I emailed it over.**

This is the symmetry to hold on to: **the past perfect goes on whichever event is earlier, whichever clause that happens to be.** Learners who put it on the wrong clause produce sentences that are grammatical and say the opposite of what was meant, which is worse than an error a reader can spot.

#### *By the time*

*By the time* fixes a deadline in the past and asks what was already true when it arrived. The main clause takes a past perfect almost without exception — this is the most reliable past-perfect trigger in the language.

> **By the time** we got to the airport, the flight **had boarded**.  
> **By the time** anyone noticed, the file **had already been deleted**.  
> **By the time** she was thirty, she **had published** four books.  
> **By the time** I finished, everyone else **had gone** home.

*By then*, *by that point*, and *by the following week* do the same job without a clause: *The meeting was at nine. By then I **had been** awake for five hours.*

#### *As soon as*, *once*, *until*

*As soon as* and *once* put their clause first, so the past perfect is again optional, and the past simple is the everyday choice.

> **As soon as** he **had signed** it, he regretted it.  
> **As soon as** he **signed** it, he regretted it. — more usual in speech

> **Once** they **had agreed** on a price, the rest went quickly.  
> **Once** they **agreed** on a price, the rest went quickly.

*Until* takes a past perfect when the point is that a state ran up to a past boundary:

> He **hadn't spoken** to his brother **until** the funeral.  
> Nobody **had noticed** the mistake **until** the auditor came through.

### 3. Reason and background for a past situation

A past state gets explained by something that happened before it. The explanation goes into the past perfect; the state stays in the past simple. This is the everyday, non-narrative use of the tense, and it is extremely common.

> He **was** tired because he **had been** up all night.  
> She **was** furious — someone **had parked** across her driveway.  
> I **couldn't** pay for lunch. I **had left** my wallet at home.  
> They **were** nervous because they **had never flown** before.  
> The house **felt** cold; the furnace **had quit** sometime in the night.  
> He **didn't** want to talk about it. Something **had happened** at work.  
> We **weren't** surprised. She **had warned** us twice.

The pattern is easy to recognize once you see it: a past-simple statement of how things *were*, and a past-perfect statement of what *made* them that way.

### 4. With *already*, *just*, *never*, *yet*, *ever*, *before*

These adverbs all say something about where an event sits relative to a reference point, which is exactly what the past perfect is for. In present contexts they take the present perfect (*I've already eaten*); shift the reference point into the past and they take the past perfect.

**Position:** *already*, *just*, *never*, and *ever* sit between *had* and the participle. *Yet* goes at the end of a negative or a question. *Before* goes at the end.

| Adverb | Present context | Past context |
|---|---|---|
| *already* | *I**'ve already eaten**.* | *I **had already eaten** when they called.* |
| *just* | *She**'s just left**.* | *She **had just left** when I got there.* |
| *never* | *I**'ve never seen** it.* | *I **had never seen** anything like it.* |
| *ever* | *Have you **ever** been?* | *Had you **ever** been before that trip?* |
| *yet* | *He hasn't called **yet**.* | *He **hadn't** called **yet**, so we waited.* |
| *before* | *I've done this **before**.* | *I **had** done it **before**, so it was easy.* |

> The train **had already pulled** out when we reached the platform.  
> I **had just sat** down when the doorbell rang.  
> She **had never been** out of the state before that summer.  
> They **hadn't decided** anything **yet**, which was the whole problem.  
> It was the strangest thing I **had ever seen**.

One American note. In the present, American English allows the past simple where British English requires the present perfect — *Did you eat yet?* alongside *Have you eaten yet?* The same relaxation reaches into past contexts, and *I already ate when they called* turns up in speech. It is informal, it is regional in its acceptance, and in writing the past perfect is the form to use.

### 5. Reported speech

A past-tense reporting verb pushes the tense of the reported clause one step back, and both the past simple and the present perfect land on the past perfect when they are pushed.

> "I **saw** the email." → She said she **had seen** the email.  
> "I**'ve finished**." → He said he **had finished**.  
> "I**'d already left**." → She said she **had already left**. — no change; already at the floor

Three originals, one reported form, which is why a reported past perfect is genuinely ambiguous — and why American speakers so often leave the past simple in place instead. The whole mechanism, including the cases where the shift does not happen, is [Backshift: the tense changes](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md). Nothing about it is repeated here.

### 6. Third conditionals, *wish*, and *if only*

The past perfect is also the form English uses for an **unreal past** — something that did not happen. This is not past-in-the-past at all; it is the tense system being borrowed to mark distance from fact.

> **If I had known**, I would have called.  
> **I wish I had said** something.  
> **If only we hadn't sold** the house.

The form is identical to the one in this file; the job is different, and the difference is that these clauses are not claiming that anything happened. The full treatment is [Conditionals](../03-conditionals/README.md) — the [third conditional](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#third-conditional) for *if*, and [Wishes and regrets](../03-conditionals/05-wishes-and-regrets.md) for *wish*, *if only*, and *would rather have*. That is also where the one-way constraint lives: an unreal past cannot be backshifted again, because it is already at the bottom.

### 7. *It was the first time I had…*

A small family of fixed frames, all of them built on a superlative or an ordinal, all of them taking the past perfect in the clause that follows.

> **It was the first time** I **had** ever **flown**.  
> **It was the third time** he **had called** that morning.  
> **It was the only time** she **had** ever **raised** her voice.  
> **That was the best meal** I **had** ever **eaten**.  
> **It was the worst storm** the county **had seen** in forty years.  
> **That was the farthest** any of us **had gone**.

Shift the frame into the present and everything moves up one rung, to the present perfect: *It's the first time I've ever flown.* The two are worth seeing side by side, because the frame is the same and only the anchor has moved:

| Anchor | Frame | Following clause |
|---|---|---|
| present — *is* | *It **is** the first time* | *I**'ve** ever **flown**.* |
| past — *was* | *It **was** the first time* | *I **had** ever **flown**.* |

The related frames are catalogued with the time subordinators: [*the first time*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#the-first-time), *the last time*, *every time*.

### 8. *Hardly… when*, *scarcely… when*, *no sooner… than*

Three correlative patterns that exist to say that two past events came almost on top of each other. All three put the earlier event in the past perfect and the later one in the past simple, and all three can invert.

**The plain order**, with the adverb in its normal mid-position:

> I **had hardly sat** down **when** the phone rang.  
> We **had scarcely** left the house **when** it started pouring.  
> She **had no sooner** hung up **than** it rang again.

**The inverted order**, with the adverb fronted and *had* pulled in front of the subject. This is the form you will meet in writing, and it is markedly literary or formal:

> **Hardly had** I **sat** down **when** the phone rang.  
> **Scarcely had** we **left** the house **when** it started pouring.  
> **No sooner had** she **hung up than** it rang again.  
> **No sooner had** the ink **dried than** they were arguing about it again.

Fronting a negative or near-negative adverb forces subject–auxiliary inversion. It is the same rule that gives *Never had I been so embarrassed* and *Not until later did we understand*, and it is set out in full in [Inversion and formal conditionals](../03-conditionals/04-inversion-and-formal.md).

**The pairings are fixed, and mixing them is the standard error:**

| Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|
| *hardly … **when*** | ✗ *hardly … than* |
| *scarcely … **when*** | ✗ *scarcely … than* |
| *barely … **when*** | ✗ *barely … than* |
| *no sooner … **than*** | ✗ *no sooner … when* |

*No sooner* takes *than* because *sooner* is a comparative, and comparatives take *than*. The other three take *when*. Both patterns have dictionary entries: [*hardly … when*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#hardly--when) and [*no sooner … than*](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#no-sooner--than).

### 9. Unfulfilled hopes and intentions

A small use, easy to miss, and it does not fit the "earlier than" description at all. With *hope*, *intend*, *plan*, *mean*, *want*, *expect*, and *think*, the past perfect says that the intention was there and **did not come off**.

> I **had hoped** to have this finished by Friday. — I didn't  
> We **had intended** to leave earlier. — we didn't  
> She **had planned** to study medicine. — she didn't  
> They **had meant** to call. — they didn't  
> I **had thought** the meeting was at three. — it wasn't

Compare the past simple, which is neutral about the outcome: *I hoped to finish by Friday* leaves open whether you did. The past perfect closes it, and closes it in the direction of disappointment. This is a useful, quietly idiomatic construction, and it shades into the unreal past of use 6 above.

## When not to use it

This is the section that matters most in practice, because **over-use is the commonest error once a learner discovers this tense**. The rule is short.

### A plain sequence of events, told in order, takes the past simple

If the sentence tells you what happened and then what happened next and then what happened after that, the order is already clear from the order of the clauses. Nothing needs marking, and the past simple is the whole answer.

> ✓ *I **woke up**, **got** dressed, **ate** breakfast, and **left** for work.*  
> ✗ *I **had woken up**, **had got** dressed, **had eaten** breakfast, and **left** for work.*

> ✓ *She **opened** the letter, **read** it twice, and **put** it in a drawer.*  
> ✗ *She **had opened** the letter, **had read** it twice, and **put** it in a drawer.*

> ✓ *He **parked**, **walked** up to the door, and **knocked**.*

This is what narrative English sounds like. A whole paragraph of past simples in chronological order is not a paragraph missing its past perfects — it is a correctly written paragraph. The past perfect exists precisely for the moments when the order **departs** from the order of telling, and using it when it does not is like putting a detour sign on a straight road.

### A single past event takes the past simple

Already covered above, and worth the repetition because it is half of the over-use problem.

> ✗ *I **had visited** Chicago in 2019.*  ✓ *I **visited** Chicago in 2019.*  
> ✗ *The company **had been founded** in 1974.*  ✓ *The company **was founded** in 1974.* — unless there is a past reference point in the surrounding text

### Where the conjunction already says it, it is a style choice, not a requirement

With *after*, *before*, *as soon as*, and *once*, dropping the past perfect loses nothing. Keeping it is fine; keeping it four times in a paragraph is not.

### Once the flashback is established, come back out of it

Two or three past perfects are enough to signal that the narrative has stepped backward. After that, the past simple carries the rest of the passage and the reader stays oriented.

> He remembered the first apartment. They **had found** it through a friend of a friend, and they **had signed** the lease the same afternoon. It **was** on the fourth floor with no elevator, and the radiator **banged** all winter.

Four verbs in the backstory, two of them past perfect, two of them past simple. Put *had* on all four and the paragraph starts to drag.

### A quick rule

**Use the past perfect only when dropping it would make the order of events unclear or wrong.** In edited prose that threshold is a little lower — writers mark the order more often than speakers do — but the principle is the same, and it correctly predicts almost every real case.

## Past perfect, past simple, present perfect

Three tenses that learners route between, with the distinction stated once.

| | Past simple | Present perfect | Past perfect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form | *left* | *have/has left* | *had left* |
| Measured from | now | now | **a past point** |
| What it says | it happened, and it is over | it happened, and it bears on now | it happened **before** that past point |
| Goes with | *yesterday*, *in 2019*, *last week* | *already*, *just*, *yet*, *since*, *ever* | *by then*, *before that*, *already*, *by the time* |
| Needs another clause? | no | no | **usually yes** |
| Example | *She **left** at six.* | *She**'s left** — you just missed her.* | *She **had left** by the time I got there.* |

The move from present perfect to past perfect is purely a move of the anchor. Everything the present perfect does relative to *now*, the past perfect does relative to *then*:

> *I**'ve** already **eaten**.* → *I **had** already **eaten**.* — already eaten, as of then  
> *This is the first time I**'ve been** here.* → *It was the first time I**'d been** there.*  
> *She**'s never flown**.* → *She **had never flown** before that trip.*

The full inventory of the twelve tense–aspect combinations and where this one sits among them is in [the verbs reference](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md#36-the-12-tense-aspect-combinations).

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **had went** home.* | *I **had gone** home.* | The past perfect takes the past participle, not the past simple. |
| ✗ *After I **had ate**, I left.* | *After I **had eaten**, I left.* | Same error. *Ate* is the past; *eaten* is the participle. |
| ✗ *I **had visited** Paris in 2018.* | *I **visited** Paris in 2018.* | A single past event with a date. Nothing to be earlier than. |
| ✗ *When I **had arrived**, she left.* | *When I **arrived**, she **had left**.* | The past perfect belongs on the earlier event. As written, it says she left after you got there. |
| ✗ *When I arrived, she **left**.* (meaning: she was gone) | *When I arrived, she **had left**.* | *When* does not mark order. Without the past perfect the sentence says the opposite. |
| ✗ *I **had woken up**, **had showered**, and went to work.* | *I **woke up**, **showered**, and went to work.* | A sequence told in order takes the past simple throughout. |
| ✗ *She **had had had** enough.* | *She **had had** enough.* | One *had* is the auxiliary, one is the verb. The tense does not stack. |
| ✗ *Yes, I**'d**.* | *Yes, I **had**.* | *Had* cannot contract when it is the last word of the clause. |
| ✗ *I**'d of** known.* | *I**'d have** known.* | *Of* is never a verb; *would've* is what is being misheard. |
| ✗ *He **didn't had** finished.* | *He **hadn't finished**.* | The past perfect makes its own negative on *had*. No *do*-support. |
| ✗ *By the time we arrived, the flight **left**.* | *By the time we arrived, the flight **had left**.* | *By the time* asks what was already true. Main clause takes the past perfect. |
| ✗ *No sooner had I sat down **when** the phone rang.* | *No sooner had I sat down **than** the phone rang.* | *No sooner* is a comparative and takes *than*. |
| ✗ *Hardly had I sat down **than** it rang.* | *Hardly had I sat down **when** it rang.* | *Hardly*, *scarcely*, and *barely* take *when*. |
| ✗ *Hardly **I had** sat down…* | *Hardly **had I** sat down…* | A fronted negative adverb forces inversion. |
| ✗ *It was the first time I **went** there.* | *It was the first time I **had** been there.* | The *first time* frame takes the perfect, matched to the anchor. |
| ✗ *He was tired because he **was** up all night.* | *He was tired because he **had been** up all night.* | The cause precedes the state, and the order is not otherwise marked. |

## For Russian speakers

### Russian has no past perfect at all

This is the whole of the difficulty, and it is a genuine structural gap rather than a matter of choosing the wrong word. Modern Russian has **one** past tense. Where English has *left* and *had left*, Russian has «ушёл» and nothing else. The old pluperfect is gone; the only trace of it left in the language is the «было» of «пошёл было» — and that construction means something entirely different (an action begun and abandoned), so there is nothing there to transfer.

Russian shows the order of past events in two ways instead, and neither one is the verb:

- **Lexically** — «сначала», «уже», «до того как», «перед тем как», «после того как», «к тому времени», «ещё раньше», «прежде».
- **By leaving it to context**, which Russian does far more comfortably than English does.

### Parallel pairs

| Russian | English | Which event is earlier |
|---|---|---|
| «Когда я **пришёл**, он **ушёл**.» | *When I **arrived**, he **left**.* | my arriving |
| «Когда я **пришёл**, он **уже ушёл**.» | *When I **arrived**, he **had** already **left**.* | his leaving |
| «Он **устал**, потому что **не спал** всю ночь.» | *He **was** tired because he **had been** up all night.* | the sleepless night |
| «**К тому времени** поезд **ушёл**.» | *By then the train **had** already **left**.* | the train leaving |
| «Я **никогда** такого **не видел**.» | *I **had never seen** anything like it.* (in a past story) | everything before |
| «Я **не узнал** его — он **сбрил** бороду.» | *I **didn't** recognize him — he **had shaved** his beard.* | the shaving |
| «**После того как** я **поел**, я **ушёл**.» | *After I **had eaten** / **ate**, I **left**.* | the eating |
| «Она **сказала**, что **видела** письмо.» | *She said she **had seen** the email.* | her seeing it |

Read down the middle column and notice what English is doing that Russian is not: in rows 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8, the *tense* is carrying the order. Russian carries it with «уже», with «потому что», with «к тому времени», or with nothing at all.

### The test

One question, asked of the event you are unsure about:

> **Was this already finished before the other past thing happened?**

If yes, past perfect. If no — if the two happened in the order you are telling them, or if there is no other past thing — past simple. It is a two-second check and it resolves almost every case.

Applied:

> *Когда я пришёл, он уже ушёл.* — Was his leaving finished before my arriving? **Yes.** → *he **had left***  
> *Когда я пришёл, он ушёл.* — Was his leaving finished before my arriving? **No**, it happened after. → *he **left***  
> *Я был в Чикаго в 2019 году.* — Before what other past thing? **There isn't one.** → *I **was** in Chicago in 2019*

### The two errors, in order of appearance

**First, under-use**, before the tense has been learned. The learner produces *When I arrived, she left* for «Когда я пришёл, она уже ушла», because Russian would have carried it on «уже» and the adverb feels optional in English. The sentence is grammatical and says the wrong thing, so nobody corrects it.

**Then, over-use**, immediately after the tense has been learned. Every completed past action gets a *had*, because Russian perfective aspect — «сделал», «прочитал», «ушёл» — marks completion, and completion is what the word *perfect* seems to promise. It does not.

**The past perfect does not mark completion. It marks anteriority.** The past simple already marks completion perfectly well: *I read the contract* says it was read, all of it, and finished. The past perfect adds only one thing, which is *before something else*. A Russian perfective maps onto the English past simple by default, and onto the past perfect only when there is a second past point in play.

> «Я **прочитал** договор.» → *I **read** the contract.* — not ✗ *I had read the contract*  
> «Я **прочитал** договор **до того как** подписал.» → *I **had read** the contract before I signed it.*

### Two smaller points

**Reported speech.** Russian does not backshift: «Он сказал, что **не знает**» keeps the present. English moves the tense back, and a Russian past inside a report usually comes out as an English past perfect — «Он сказал, что **видел** письмо» → *He said he **had seen** the email.* The full mechanism is in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md), and the honest news is that Americans often leave the past simple there anyway.

**«Бы» and the unreal past.** «Если бы я знал» has no tense in it at all, so Russian gives no signal whether you mean *If I knew* (now) or *If I had known* (then). English forces the choice, and choosing wrong does not sound vague — it sounds like a different claim. That split is worked through in [Real, unreal, and time](../03-conditionals/06-real-unreal-and-time.md).

## Quick reference

**The form.** *had* + past participle, for every subject. Negative *hadn't*. Question *Had you…?* Passive *had been* + participle. Continuous *had been* + `-ing`.

**The one question.**

```
   Is there a second past event or past time point in play?

   NO ──────────────────────────────────  past simple. Stop.

   YES ─── Did the event you are marking come BEFORE it?
       │
       ├── yes ..............................  past perfect
       └── no, or same time .................  past simple
```

**The four sentences to remember.**

> **When I arrived, she had left.** — she was gone before I got there

> **When I arrived, she left.** — she left after I got there

> **He was tired because he had been up all night.** — the cause came first

> **I woke up, got dressed, and left.** — a plain sequence needs nothing

**Five things that trigger it almost automatically.**

1. ***by the time*** and ***by then*** — *By the time we got there, they had eaten.*
2. ***already***, ***just***, ***never***, ***yet*** with a past anchor — *I had already eaten when they called.*
3. ***It was the first time…*** and every superlative frame — *the best meal I had ever eaten*
4. ***no sooner… than***, ***hardly… when*** — *No sooner had I sat down than it rang.*
5. **A reason clause under a past statement** — *because he had lost his key*

**Three places it is optional.** After *after*, after *before*, after *as soon as* — the conjunction has already stated the order.

**Two places it is obligatory.** After *when*, where dropping it flips the meaning. After *by the time*, where dropping it sounds wrong.

**The *'d* rule.** Participle after it → *had*. Bare infinitive after it → *would*. *'d have* → *would have*.

## Practice

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple or the past perfect. Some items take the past simple in both slots, and one of them is a trap.

1. By the time we (get) to the theater, the film (start).
2. I (wake) up, (make) coffee, and (read) the paper.
3. She (be) angry because someone (take) her parking spot.
4. When the guests (arrive), he (finish) cooking. (The cooking was done first.)
5. He (visit) Boston in 2017.
6. I (not recognize) her. She (cut) her hair.
7. No sooner (I / sit) down ___ the phone rang. (Invert, and supply the connector.)
8. It (be) the first time I (ever / fly).
9. After she (read) the contract, she (sign) it.
10. When I (call) her, she (make) dinner. (She was in the middle of it.)
11. They (live) in Tulsa for nine years before they (move) to Denver.
12. The room (clean) before we (get) there. (Use the passive.)
13. Hardly (we / leave) the house ___ it started to rain. (Invert, and supply the connector.)
14. I (have) three cups of coffee by noon.
15. She (say) she (see) the email. (Report it.)
16. We (hope) to have it finished by Friday, but we (not).
17. When I (arrive), she (leave). (She was still there, and left while I watched.)
18. He (not speak) to his brother until the funeral.
19. I (just / hang) up when it rang again.
20. If I (know), I would have called.
21. They (be) nervous because they (never / fly) before.
22. She (open) the letter, (read) it twice, and (put) it in a drawer.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | By the time we **got** to the theater, the film **had started**. | *By the time* — main clause takes the past perfect. |
| 2 | I **woke** up, **made** coffee, and **read** the paper. | A plain sequence in order. No past perfect anywhere. |
| 3 | She **was** angry because someone **had taken** her parking spot. | Cause precedes the state. |
| 4 | When the guests **arrived**, he **had finished** cooking. | The past perfect goes on the earlier event. |
| 5 | He **visited** Boston in 2017. | Single past event with a date. Nothing to be earlier than. |
| 6 | I **didn't recognize** her. She **had cut** her hair. | The haircut came first and explains the failure. |
| 7 | **No sooner had I sat** down **than** the phone rang. | *No sooner* takes *than*, and fronting forces inversion. |
| 8 | It **was** the first time I **had** ever **flown**. | The *first time* frame, anchored in the past. |
| 9 | After she **had read** / **read** the contract, she **signed** it. | Both correct. *After* has already stated the order. |
| 10 | When I **called** her, she **was making** dinner. | Past continuous — in progress, not earlier. The trap. |
| 11 | They **had lived** in Tulsa for nine years before they **moved** to Denver. | Or *lived* — *before* states the order. The perfect is the more usual choice with a duration. |
| 12 | The room **had been cleaned** before we **got** there. | Past perfect passive: *had been* + participle. |
| 13 | **Hardly had we left** the house **when** it started to rain. | *Hardly* takes *when*, and inverts. |
| 14 | I **had had** three cups of coffee by noon. | Auxiliary *had* + lexical *had*. Correct, and not a typo. |
| 15 | She **said** she **had seen** the email. | Backshift: past simple → past perfect. (*She said she saw it* is also standard in speech.) |
| 16 | We **had hoped** to have it finished by Friday, but we **didn't**. | The unfulfilled-intention past perfect. |
| 17 | When I **arrived**, she **left**. | No past perfect — the leaving came second. Compare item 4. |
| 18 | He **hadn't spoken** to his brother **until** the funeral. | A state running up to a past boundary. |
| 19 | I **had just hung** up when it rang again. | *Just* between *had* and the participle. |
| 20 | If I **had known**, I would have called. | Third conditional — unreal past, not past-in-the-past. |
| 21 | They **were** nervous because they **had never flown** before. | Cause clause, with *never* and *before*. |
| 22 | She **opened** the letter, **read** it twice, and **put** it in a drawer. | A sequence in order. Past simple throughout. |
