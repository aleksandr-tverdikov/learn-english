# The present continuous

> *Am/is/are* + *-ing* — the full spelling of the participle, every use from "right now" to "next Thursday," the state verbs that refuse the form and the ones that change meaning inside it.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The present continuous — also called the **present progressive**; the two names mean the same thing and this library uses the first — is built from a present-tense form of *be* and the *-ing* participle of the main verb.

> **I'm waiting** outside.  
> **She's writing** a book.  
> **They aren't listening.**

What the form claims is not "now." It is **in progress and not finished** — a situation viewed from the inside, while it is still running. That is why the same form covers an action happening at this second (*the phone is ringing*), a project that occupies weeks (*she's writing a book*), a trend (*prices are rising*), and an appointment next Thursday (*we're meeting at six*). None of those is more or less "present" than the others. All four are unfinished, and English has one form for unfinished.

Here is the idea to carry through the whole section, because every use below is a version of it:

**The present simple says that something is a fact. The present continuous says that something is in progress.** *I work downtown* is a fact about my life. *I'm working downtown* is a stretch of time I am in the middle of, with edges. The difference is not when — both are about now — it is whether the speaker is presenting the situation as settled or as running.

That single distinction is the one Russian does not make with a separate verb form, and the interference it causes has [its own section below](#for-russian-speakers).

## The form

| Person | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | *I am working* | *I am not working* | *Am I working?* |
| you (sg. and pl.) | *you are working* | *you are not working* | *Are you working?* |
| he / she / it | *he is working* | *he is not working* | *Is he working?* |
| we | *we are working* | *we are not working* | *Are we working?* |
| they | *they are working* | *they are not working* | *Are they working?* |

Three things to notice before anything else.

**Only *be* carries the tense.** The *-ing* form never changes — not for person, not for number, not for anything. Whatever agreement English demands is spent on *am*, *is*, or *are*, and the participle rides along.

**The auxiliary is not optional.** ✗ *She working today* and ✗ *She is work today* are both missing exactly one piece, and both are among the most recognizable learner errors in the language.

**There is no *do*-support.** Questions are made by moving *be* in front of the subject, and negatives by putting *not* after it. *Do* has no part in this tense at all: ✗ *Do you working?*, ✗ *I don't working*.

### Contractions, as they are actually said

Nobody says *I am waiting outside*. The contracted form is the default in speech and in most writing, and the full form is emphatic, formal, or careful.

> **I'm** waiting outside.  
> **You're** standing on my foot.  
> **He's** making dinner, and **she's** running late.  
> **It's** getting dark.  
> **We're** leaving — **they're** staying.

The negative contracts two different ways for every person but the first, and both are standard:

| Full | Contracted on *be* | Contracted on *not* |
|---|---|---|
| *I am not working* | *I'm not working* | — no ✗ *amn't* |
| *you are not working* | *you're not working* | *you aren't working* |
| *he is not working* | *he's not working* | *he isn't working* |
| *we are not working* | *we're not working* | *we aren't working* |
| *they are not working* | *they're not working* | *they aren't working* |

The first-person row is the famous hole in the system. English has no contraction for *am not* — ✗ *amn't* exists in Irish and Scottish English and nowhere in standard American usage — so *I'm not* is the only option, and it is the one form in the table with no alternative. This gap and what English does about it are treated in full in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

Note also that *'s* in *he's working* is *is*, while *'s* in *he's worked* is *has*. The word after it decides: an *-ing* form means *is*, a past participle means *has*. The same two letters, two different verbs — see [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

### Questions

*Be* moves to the front. Nothing else changes.

> **Are you** coming?  
> **Is she** still at the office?  
> **Am I** interrupting?  
> **Are they** hiring?

With a wh-word, the wh-word comes first and *be* still inverts:

> **What are** you doing?  
> **Where is** he going?  
> **Why are** they arguing?  
> **How long are** you staying?

The one exception is a question **about the subject**, where there is nothing to invert around and the word order stays flat:

> **Who is** knocking? — *who* is the subject, so nothing moved  
> **What is** making that noise?

In *Who's calling?* the subject is *who*, so the sentence looks like a statement with a question mark on it. Compare *Who are you calling?*, where *who* is the object and *are* does invert.

### Negative questions

The contracted negative question is the ordinary one. The uncontracted version is formal to the point of sounding stiff, and it puts *not* after the subject.

> **Aren't you** cold?  
> **Isn't she** coming with us?  
> **Are you not** seeing what I'm seeing? — formal or indignant

A negative question is rarely a neutral request for information. It carries an expectation — the speaker thinks the answer is yes and is half-arguing for it — or surprise that the answer might be no. *Aren't you coming?* means "I assumed you were."

And then the irregular one, which has to be memorized because no rule produces it:

> ***Aren't I*** late?  
> I'm next, ***aren't I***?

**The negative question and the tag for *I am* are *aren't I*.** It is grammatically indefensible — *are* does not go with *I* anywhere else in English — and it is completely standard. ✗ *Amn't I* is not American English. *Am I not late?* is correct but formal and slightly theatrical. Use *aren't I* and do not think about it.

### Short answers

The affirmative short answer **never contracts**. This is a hard rule with no exceptions.

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| *Are you working?* | *Yes, I am.* — ✗ *Yes, I'm.* | *No, I'm not.* |
| *Is he coming?* | *Yes, he is.* — ✗ *Yes, he's.* | *No, he isn't.* / *No, he's not.* |
| *Are they waiting?* | *Yes, they are.* — ✗ *Yes, they're.* | *No, they aren't.* / *No, they're not.* |
| *Am I next?* | *Yes, you are.* | *No, you aren't.* / *No, you're not.* |

The reason is phonetic rather than logical: a contraction cannot carry stress, and the last word of a short answer is stressed. The same rule blocks ✗ *Yes, I've*, ✗ *Yes, he'd*, and ✗ *I don't know where he's*. The negative contracts freely because *not* is a full syllable and can take the stress itself.

### Tag questions

The tag reverses the polarity of the main clause and reuses *be*:

> You**'re** coming, **aren't you**?  
> She**'s not** working today, **is she**?  
> I**'m** on the list, **aren't I**?

## Spelling the *-ing* form

Seven rules, in the order you should apply them. They cover every verb in English.

### 1. The default: add *-ing*

Most verbs need nothing done to them.

| Verb | *-ing* form | Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|---|
| work | *working* | open | *opening* |
| eat | *eating* | sleep | *sleeping* |
| go | *going* | help | *helping* |

### 2. Drop a silent final *-e*

If the verb ends in a consonant plus a silent *-e*, the *-e* disappears.

| Verb | *-ing* form | Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|---|
| make | *making* | write | *writing* |
| come | *coming* | take | *taking* |
| have | *having* | give | *giving* |
| dance | *dancing* | smile | *smiling* |

✗ *makeing*, ✗ *writeing*, ✗ *haveing*. The *-e* was never pronounced; it was there to lengthen the vowel, and the *-ing* does that job instead.

American spelling drops the *-e* in a few words where British spelling keeps it: **aging** (BrE *ageing*), **judging**, **acknowledging**. Write *aging*.

### 3. But keep the *-e* after another vowel

When the final *-e* is preceded by a vowel — *-ee*, *-ye*, *-oe* — it stays, because dropping it would wreck the pronunciation.

| Verb | *-ing* form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| see | *seeing* | ✗ *seing* |
| agree | *agreeing* | |
| be | *being* | |
| dye | *dyeing* | to color something |
| eye | *eyeing* | *eying* also occurs, less common |
| canoe | *canoeing* | |
| singe | *singeing* | to scorch — keeps the *-e* to protect the soft *g* |

Two of those rows produce famous minimal pairs. *Dyeing* is coloring fabric; *dying* is what rule 4 does to *die*. *Singeing* is scorching; *singing* is what *sing* does. In both cases the surviving *-e* is the only thing keeping two unrelated verbs apart.

### 4. *-ie* becomes *-y-*

Four verbs in common use end in *-ie*, and all four change it to *y* before *-ing*.

| Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|
| die | *dying* |
| lie | *lying* |
| tie | *tying* |
| vie | *vying* |

✗ *dieing*, ✗ *lieing*. The reason is that English does not tolerate *-ii-*, and *die* + *-ing* would produce exactly that; *y* is the spelling English uses for that sound when *i* is unavailable.

### 5. Double the final consonant under stress

This is the rule that generates the most errors, and it has three conditions. All three must hold.

**Double the final consonant when the verb ends in a single consonant, preceded by a single vowel, in a stressed syllable.**

One-syllable verbs are nearly always stressed, so they nearly always double:

| Verb | *-ing* form | Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|---|
| run | *running* | sit | *sitting* |
| stop | *stopping* | get | *getting* |
| swim | *swimming* | plan | *planning* |
| put | *putting* | shop | *shopping* |

Longer verbs double only if the **last** syllable carries the stress. This is the part that has to be listened for:

| Verb | Stress | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|
| begin | be-**GIN** | *beginning* |
| forget | for-**GET** | *forgetting* |
| prefer | pre-**FER** | *preferring* |
| admit | ad-**MIT** | *admitting* |
| control | con-**TROL** | *controlling* |

And when the stress falls earlier, the consonant stays single:

| Verb | Stress | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|
| offer | **OF**-fer | *offering* — ✗ *offerring* |
| open | **O**-pen | *opening* |
| happen | **HAP**-pen | *happening* |
| visit | **VIS**-it | *visiting* |
| benefit | **BEN**-e-fit | *benefiting* |
| develop | de-**VEL**-op | *developing* |

*Offer* and *prefer* are the pair to hold onto. They differ by one letter in the infinitive and by two in the participle, and the only thing separating them is where the voice lands.

**Three situations block the doubling regardless of stress.**

- **Two consonants at the end.** *help → helping*, *work → working*, *start → starting*, *jump → jumping*.
- **Two vowels before the consonant.** *rain → raining*, *sleep → sleeping*, *wait → waiting*, *read → reading*, *meet → meeting*, *shout → shouting*, *need → needing*.
- **A final *w*, *x*, or *y*.** These letters never double in English. *snow → snowing*, *show → showing*, *fix → fixing*, *relax → relaxing*, *play → playing*, *enjoy → enjoying*.

**The American *-l* exception.** British English doubles a final *l* whether or not the syllable is stressed. American English does not — it follows the stress rule like every other consonant.

| Verb | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| travel | *traveling* | *travelling* |
| cancel | *canceling* | *cancelling* |
| label | *labeling* | *labelling* |
| model | *modeling* | *modelling* |
| signal | *signaling* | *signalling* |

Write the single *l*. Note that *control* and *patrol* double in both dialects, because their stress really is on the last syllable — *con-**TROL***, *pa-**TROL*** — so they never were exceptions.

### 6. *-c* becomes *-ck*

A handful of verbs end in *-c*. Add a *k* before the *-ing*, or the *c* would be read as /s/.

| Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|
| panic | *panicking* |
| picnic | *picnicking* |
| mimic | *mimicking* |
| traffic | *trafficking* |

✗ *panicing* would rhyme with *dancing*.

### 7. A final *-y* is always kept

Whatever the letter before it, a final *y* survives into the *-ing* form untouched. There are no exceptions.

| Verb | *-ing* form | Verb | *-ing* form |
|---|---|---|---|
| study | *studying* | try | *trying* |
| carry | *carrying* | hurry | *hurrying* |
| fly | *flying* | say | *saying* |
| buy | *buying* | enjoy | *enjoying* |

This is worth stating loudly because the *other* two endings do change the *y*. *Study* gives *studies* and *studied* — but *studying*. *Carry* gives *carries* and *carried* — but *carrying*. The *-ing* is the one suffix that leaves *y* alone, and learners who have just drilled the third-person *-s* routinely produce ✗ *studing* and ✗ *carring*.

## What the present continuous is used for

Eight uses. The first two are the ones textbooks lead with; the rest account for at least as much of the form's actual work.

### 1. Happening at this moment

The action is running as the sentence is spoken. This is the use that gives the tense its reputation, and it is frequently signaled by *now*, *right now*, *at the moment*, *currently*, *Look!*, or *Listen!*

> Be quiet — the baby**'s sleeping.**  
> **Look**, it**'s snowing.**  
> What **are** you **doing**? — I**'m making** coffee.  
> The phone **is ringing.**  
> She**'s** not **answering** her phone.

### 2. Happening around now, but not at this second

The action is in progress over a stretch of time that includes the present, though nothing may be happening at the exact moment of speaking. A person who says *I'm reading a great book* is very likely not holding the book.

> I**'m reading** a great book at the moment.  
> She**'s writing** her dissertation.  
> He**'s training** for a marathon.  
> We**'re looking** for a bigger apartment.  
> They**'re working** on a new album.  
> I**'m taking** Spanish this semester.

This use is what makes *I'm reading* and *I read* genuinely different claims rather than stylistic variants. *I read a lot of history* describes the kind of reader I am. *I'm reading a history of the Civil War* describes an unfinished project. Neither sentence could replace the other.

### 3. Temporary situations, against permanent ones

The continuous frames a situation as having edges — it started, it will stop, and the speaker knows it. The simple frames the same kind of situation as just being the case.

| Continuous — temporary | Simple — permanent |
|---|---|
| *I**'m living** with my parents until the lease starts.* | *I **live** in Denver.* |
| *She**'s teaching** third grade this year.* | *She **teaches** third grade.* |
| *I**'m driving** my brother's car while mine is in the shop.* | *I **drive** a Corolla.* |

Nothing in the left column says the situation is brief — *she's teaching third grade this year* covers nine months. What it says is that the speaker has an endpoint in mind. That is the whole of the difference, and it is why a temporary arrangement stated in the present simple sounds subtly wrong: ✗ *I live with my parents for a few weeks* reports a permanent address and then contradicts it.

Time expressions that pull toward the continuous: *this week*, *this month*, *this semester*, *these days*, *at the moment*, *for now*, *until*, *while*, *temporarily*, *for the time being*.

### 4. Changing and developing situations

The continuous is the form English uses for a trend — a situation that is not where it was and is not finished moving.

> It**'s getting** colder.  
> Prices **are rising.**  
> The city **is growing** fast.  
> My Spanish **is improving.**  
> Things **are changing** around here.  
> The population of the county **is falling** for the first time since 1940.

The verbs that live in this use are the verbs of change: *get*, *grow*, *become*, *turn*, *rise*, *fall*, *increase*, *decrease*, *improve*, *worsen*, *change*, *develop*, *expand*, *shrink*, *widen*, *deteriorate*, *begin*.

Two patterns intensify it. The doubled comparative — *colder and colder*, *worse and worse* — and *more and more*:

> The traffic **is getting worse and worse**, and it**'s becoming more and more** expensive to park.

### 5. Fixed future arrangements

The present continuous is one of English's main future forms. It reports an arrangement that already exists — something with a time, a place, and usually another person involved.

> We**'re meeting** at six.  
> I**'m flying** to Denver on Thursday.  
> She**'s starting** her new job in August.  
> They**'re getting** married in June.  
> What **are** you **doing** this weekend?  
> He**'s** not **coming** to the party.

Two things make this use work.

**A time expression, or a context that supplies one.** *I'm flying to Denver* on its own is ambiguous between "right now, from seat 14C" and "on Thursday." *On Thursday* settles it, and in practice a future continuous almost always has a time word attached or recoverable from the conversation.

**An arrangement, not a prediction.** Something has to have been arranged — a booking, an appointment, an agreement with someone else. This is why weather cannot take the form: ✗ *It's raining tomorrow* is wrong not because of the tense machinery but because rain does not make plans. *It's going to rain tomorrow* is the sentence.

The verbs that take this use most naturally are verbs of movement, meeting, and scheduled events: *go*, *come*, *leave*, *arrive*, *fly*, *drive*, *meet*, *see*, *start*, *finish*, *play*, *stay*, *have* (in the sense of hosting or attending).

#### Present continuous, *going to*, and *will*

Three forms, three different claims. The times can be identical; what differs is where the future comes from.

| Form | What it reports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **present continuous** | an arrangement that already exists | *I**'m meeting** Dana at six.* |
| ***be going to*** | an intention already formed, or a prediction from present evidence | *I**'m going to call** her tonight.* / *Look at those clouds — it**'s going to** pour.* |
| ***will*** | a decision made at the moment of speaking, or a neutral prediction | *That's the door — I**'ll get** it.* / *You**'ll like** her.* |

Worked through:

> **I'm having** lunch with Sam tomorrow. — it is arranged; we picked the restaurant  
> **I'm going to have** lunch with Sam sometime. — I intend to; nothing is set  
> **I'll have** lunch with Sam, then. — decided just now, in response to something

The continuous and *going to* overlap heavily, and in most sentences either is acceptable. The continuous is the more confident of the two — it presents the thing as settled — which is why it dominates in calendars, invitations, and answers to *What are you doing this weekend?* Where there is no arrangement at all, only intention, *going to* is the safer choice.

**A schedule is not an arrangement.** Timetabled events — trains, flights, class times, opening hours, sunrise — take the **present simple**, not the continuous, because they are facts about a published schedule rather than plans anybody made:

> The train **leaves** at 6:40.  
> The museum **opens** at ten on Sundays.  
> Our flight **gets in** at 11:15.  
> The sun **sets** at 7:42 tomorrow.

The two can appear in one sentence, each doing its own job: *The train **leaves** at 6:40, so we**'re meeting** at the station at six.*

### 6. *Always*, *constantly*, *forever* — annoyance and surprise

With a frequency adverb of the "too often" family, the present continuous stops being about progress and becomes a comment. The adverb sits between *be* and the participle.

> He**'s always losing** his keys.  
> You**'re always complaining** about the weather.  
> She**'s constantly interrupting** people.  
> They**'re forever changing** the deadline.  
> My computer **is always crashing.**  
> He**'s never listening** when I talk to him.

Compare each of those with the plain present simple and the difference is immediate:

| Present simple — neutral | Present continuous — loaded |
|---|---|
| *He **always loses** his keys.* | *He**'s always losing** his keys.* — and it drives me crazy |
| *She **interrupts** people.* | *She**'s constantly interrupting** people.* — a complaint |
| *My computer **crashes**.* | *My computer **is always crashing**.* — exasperation |

The simple version states a frequency. The continuous version states an attitude, and it is usually an exaggeration — *always* here does not mean every single time, it means **more often than I can stand**. That hyperbole is built into the construction; speakers are not being imprecise.

The same pattern can carry admiration or amused surprise rather than irritation, and context is the only thing that tells you which:

> She**'s always finding** money on the sidewalk.  
> He**'s always helping** somebody move.  
> You**'re always turning up** at exactly the right moment.

The adverbs that trigger it: *always*, *constantly*, *continually*, *forever*, *all the time*, *never*, *perpetually*. (*Continuously* does not belong in this list; it means "without stopping," which is a different claim.) *All the time* goes at the end rather than in the middle: *He's losing his keys all the time.*

### 7. Commentary, demonstration, and narrative

**Sports commentary** splits the two present tenses by speed. A quick, complete action takes the present simple; a background situation or a longer action takes the continuous:

> Rodriguez **passes** to Kim, Kim **shoots** — and he **scores!**  
> The rain **is coming** down harder now, and the crowd **is starting** to leave.  
> He**'s limping.** He **doesn't look** good.

Learners are often told commentary "uses the present simple," and that is half the story. The simple handles the ball; the continuous handles everything around it.

**Demonstrations and instructions** work the same way — the simple for each discrete step, the continuous for what the demonstrator is doing while talking:

> Now I**'m adding** the eggs, one at a time.  
> You **see** how the batter **is thickening**?

**Conversational narrative** in American English switches into the present for immediacy, and the continuous supplies the scenery the story happens in:

> So I**'m standing** in line at the DMV, and this guy **is yelling** at the clerk, and she just **looks** at him and **says**, "Sir."

Notice the division of labor. *I'm standing*, *is yelling* set the scene; *looks* and *says* move the plot. The events are simple; the background is continuous. The same pattern runs through written fiction in the historical present.

### 8. Verbs of posture

*Sit*, *stand*, *lie*, *kneel*, *crouch*, *lean*, *hang*, *perch*, and *wear* describe positions rather than actions, but they take the continuous freely and naturally.

> She**'s sitting** by the window.  
> He**'s standing** in the doorway.  
> The cat **is lying** on my keyboard.  
> A coat **is hanging** on the hook.  
> She**'s wearing** a red coat.

With these verbs the **present simple** is not wrong; it changes the register and the claim, marking the position as permanent and the sentence as descriptive:

> The house **stands** on a hill overlooking the river.  
> A portrait of the founder **hangs** over the fireplace.

*The cat is lying on my keyboard* is a report of the present nuisance. *The house stands on a hill* is a description of the house. You cannot swap them: ✗ *The house is standing on a hill* suggests it may get up.

*Wear* deserves a line of its own because it looks like a state verb and is not. *She's wearing a red coat* is the ordinary way to say it; *She wears a red coat* means she habitually does. And the idiomatic *wear out*, *wear off*, *wear down* are all actions: *The anesthetic is wearing off.*

## State verbs and the continuous

### The rule, and why it exists

Some verbs describe a **state** — something that is the case — rather than an **action** — something someone does. A state has no beginning, middle, or end to be in the middle of, so there is nothing for the continuous to report.

> ✗ *I **am knowing** the answer.*  ✓ *I **know** the answer.*  
> ✗ *She **is wanting** a coffee.*  ✓ *She **wants** a coffee.*  
> ✗ *This box **is containing** the files.*  ✓ *This box **contains** the files.*  
> ✗ *I **am belonging** to a gym.*  ✓ *I **belong** to a gym.*

The inventory, grouped by what kind of state they name:

| Group | Verbs |
|---|---|
| **Perception** | *see, hear, smell, taste, feel, notice, sound* |
| **Opinion and mental state** | *think* (opinion), *believe, know, understand, remember, forget, recognize, suppose, mean, doubt, realize, imagine, agree, disagree, consider* (opinion), *mind, find* (= consider) |
| **Emotion and preference** | *like, love, hate, prefer, want, need, wish, care, adore, detest, dislike, envy, value* |
| **Possession and relation** | *have* (possess), *own, belong, possess, contain, consist of, include, involve, comprise, lack, owe, deserve, depend on, matter, concern, cost, weigh, measure, fit, suit, resemble, equal* |
| **Being and seeming** | *be, exist, seem, appear* (= seem), *look* (= appear) |

**The *can* workaround.** With perception verbs, English does not use the continuous to say something is happening right now — it uses *can*, which sounds like an ability but is not.

> I **can see** the ocean from here. — not ✗ *I am seeing the ocean*  
> I **can hear** somebody in the hallway.

*I see the ocean* is also fine. What is unavailable is the continuous.

### The verbs that change meaning in the continuous

Here is the part that matters, because the "ban" above is not really a ban on verbs — it is a ban on **state meanings**. Many of these verbs have a second, action meaning, and in that meaning they take the continuous like any other verb. The form is not optional decoration: it tells you which of the two verbs you are dealing with.

| Verb | State — present simple | Action — present continuous |
|---|---|---|
| **think** | hold an opinion: *I **think** it's a mistake.* | use the mind: *I**'m thinking** about moving.* |
| **have** | possess: *I **have** two brothers.* | experience or do: *I**'m having** lunch. We**'re having** a great time. She**'s having** a baby. He**'s having** trouble with the form.* |
| **see** | perceive or understand: *I **see** what you mean.* | meet, consult, or date: *I**'m seeing** the dentist at three. She**'s seeing** someone.* |
| **taste** | have a flavor: *This soup **tastes** salty.* | sample deliberately: *The chef **is tasting** the sauce.* |
| **smell** | have an odor: *Your coat **smells** like smoke.* | sniff deliberately: *I**'m smelling** the milk to see if it's still good.* |
| **feel** | have a texture or an opinion: *This **feels** soft. I **feel** we should wait.* | touch or grope: *He**'s feeling** around in his pocket for the key.* |
| **be** | a permanent quality: *He **is** rude.* | behaving that way right now: *He**'s being** rude.* |
| **look** | appear: *You **look** tired.* | direct the eyes, or search: *I**'m looking** at the map. I**'m looking** for my phone.* |
| **appear** | seem: *It **appears** to be broken.* | perform, or come into view: *She**'s appearing** at the Fillmore in June.* |
| **weigh** | have a weight: *It **weighs** ten pounds.* | measure a weight: *The nurse **is weighing** the baby.* |
| **measure** | have a dimension: *The room **measures** twelve by fourteen.* | take a measurement: *He**'s measuring** the window.* |
| **expect** | assume: *I **expect** she's stuck in traffic.* | await: *We**'re expecting** a delivery. She**'s expecting**.* |

**Health and emotion with *feel*** are the one row where both forms are equally standard and the difference is almost nothing:

> I **feel** sick. / I**'m feeling** sick.  
> How **do** you **feel**? / How **are** you **feeling**?

The continuous leans very slightly more temporary and more solicitous — *How are you feeling?* is what you ask someone who is ill. Neither is an error.

***Be* + continuous is the row to study**, because it makes a distinction English has no other way of making:

| Permanent quality | Behaving that way now |
|---|---|
| *He **is** selfish.* — that's the kind of person he is | *He**'s being** selfish.* — about this, right now |
| *She **is** difficult.* | *She**'s being** difficult.* |
| *You **are** silly.* | *You**'re being** silly.* |

*He is stupid* is an insult about a person. *He is being stupid* is a complaint about a decision. Only adjectives describing behavior work this way — ✗ *He's being tall*, ✗ *She's being American*, ✗ *I'm being hungry*.

### *I'm loving it*: the marketing continuous

*Love*, *like*, *hate*, and *want* are state verbs, and American speech puts them in the continuous anyway.

> I**'m loving** this weather.  
> I**'m liking** the new layout.

The meaning the continuous adds is **right now, and it may not last** — a reaction to something current rather than a standing preference. *I love this weather* says something about my taste; *I'm loving this weather* says something about this week.

The honest assessment: this is genuinely widespread in American speech, it was not invented by an advertising campaign (though "I'm lovin' it" made it conspicuous), and nobody will misunderstand you. It is also unmistakably **informal**. It does not belong in a cover letter, an academic paper, or anything edited, where *I love* and *I like* are the only options. ✗ *I am wanting a refund* has no such cover and is simply wrong.

*Enjoy*, by contrast, is not a state verb at all and takes the continuous with no reservations: *I'm enjoying the class* is neutral, standard English.

## The passive

The present continuous passive is *am/is/are* + **being** + past participle.

> The road **is being repaved.**  
> My car **is being fixed.**  
> The results **are being reviewed.**  
> He**'s being questioned** by the police.  
> Nothing **is being done** about it.

Negatives and questions behave exactly as they do in the active — *be* carries everything:

> The report **isn't being reviewed** yet.  
> **Is** the road **being repaved**, or just patched?  
> **Aren't** these **being recycled**?

Active and passive side by side:

| Active | Passive |
|---|---|
| *They**'re painting** the house.* | *The house **is being painted**.* |
| *Someone **is following** us.* | *We**'re being followed**.* |
| *The committee **is reviewing** the applications.* | *The applications **are being reviewed**.* |

Two cautions.

**One *being*, never two.** ✗ *The house is being being painted.* The construction has exactly one.

**A missing *being* changes the sentence.** ✗ *The house is building* is not a passive at all — it makes the house the builder. The full form *is being built* is not optional.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **am knowing** the answer.* | *I **know** the answer.* | *Know* is a state verb. There is no progress to report. |
| ✗ *She **is work** today.* | *She **is working** today.* | The participle needs its *-ing*. |
| ✗ *She **working** today.* | *She **is working** today.* | The auxiliary is never dropped in standard English. |
| ✗ *I**'m agree** with you.* | *I **agree** with you.* | *Agree* is a verb, not an adjective. There is no *be* in front of it at all. |
| ✗ *Do you **working** late?* | *Are you **working** late?* | This tense has no *do*-support. *Be* inverts. |
| ✗ *He is **runing**.* | *He is **running**.* | Stressed single vowel + single consonant doubles. |
| ✗ *I'm **makeing** dinner.* | *I'm **making** dinner.* | Silent *-e* is dropped. |
| ✗ *I **live** with my parents for a few weeks.* | *I**'m living** with my parents for a few weeks.* | A temporary situation takes the continuous. |
| ✗ *Yes, I**'m**.* | *Yes, I **am**.* | A short answer never contracts the affirmative. |
| ✗ ***Amn't** I next?* | ***Aren't** I next?* | The irregular form has to be memorized. |
| ✗ *What **you are** doing?* | *What **are you** doing?* | *Be* inverts in a wh-question unless *wh* is the subject. |
| ✗ *It**'s raining** tomorrow.* | *It**'s going to rain** tomorrow.* | The continuous future needs an arrangement; weather makes none. |
| ✗ *I**'m having** a car.* | *I **have** a car.* | *Have* meaning "possess" is a state verb. |
| ✗ *He is always **lose** his keys.* | *He**'s always losing** his keys.* | The adverb sits between *be* and the participle; the participle stays *-ing*. |
| ✗ *I **am seeing** the ocean from here.* | *I **can see** the ocean from here.* | Perception verbs use *can*, not the continuous. |
| ✗ *The house **is building**.* | *The house **is being built**.* | A continuous passive needs *being* + the participle. |

## For Russian speakers

Six interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

### 1. One Russian form covers both English tenses

This is the whole problem, and everything else is a consequence of it. Russian marks aspect on the verb itself, and the **imperfective** covers both English present tenses at once. «Я читаю» is *I read* and *I'm reading*, and Russian leaves the difference to context.

| Russian | English — a fact | English — in progress |
|---|---|---|
| «Я читаю» | *I **read**.* — I am a reader | *I**'m reading**.* — right now |
| «Я работаю в банке» | *I **work** at a bank.* — my job | *I**'m working** at a bank.* — for now |
| «Он живёт в Чикаго» | *He **lives** in Chicago.* — his home | *He**'s living** in Chicago.* — this year |
| «Что ты делаешь?» | *What **do** you **do**?* — what's your job | *What **are** you **doing**?* — at this moment |

The bottom row is the one that goes wrong in real conversations. A Russian speaker who walks in and asks *What do you do?* has asked about the person's career. The intended question is *What are you doing?*

### 2. The error is under-use, not misuse

Because the present simple looks like the "basic" form, and because it maps onto the Russian imperfective without any extra machinery, Russian speakers overwhelmingly default to it. The result is not ungrammatical nonsense — it is a steady stream of sentences that are grammatical and say the wrong thing.

> ✗ *I **work** on the report.* — sounds like a recurring duty  
> ✓ *I**'m working** on the report.* — what I am doing now

> ✗ *Why **do** you **look** at me like that?*  
> ✓ *Why **are** you **looking** at me like that?*

### 3. The "right now" test, and why it is not enough

The usual advice — *if it's happening right now, use the continuous* — is a good start and covers use 1. It fails on the other seven, and a learner who trusts it will still write ✗ *I read a great book* for a book they are halfway through.

The better test is two questions, asked in order:

```
   1. Is this a habit, a general truth, or a permanent state?
      YES → present simple.        I go to the gym on Tuesdays.

   2. Is it in progress, temporary, changing, or arranged?
      YES → present continuous.    I'm going to the gym more these days.
```

### 4. «Сейчас» does not force the continuous

*Now* is compatible with **both** tenses, because Russian «сейчас» has at least three meanings and English splits them across different forms.

| Russian | English | Tense, and why |
|---|---|---|
| «Сейчас я живу в Москве» | *I**'m living** in Moscow now.* | temporary situation → continuous |
| «Сейчас поезда ходят каждые десять минут» | *The trains **run** every ten minutes now.* | *now* = these days; still a habit → simple |
| «Сейчас приду» | *I**'m coming**.* / *I**'ll be** right there.* | in progress, or an instant decision |
| «Сейчас идёт дождь» | *It**'s raining** right now.* | in progress → continuous |

So *now* is not a signal word for the continuous the way textbook lists imply. What decides is the same thing that always decides: fact or progress.

### 5. ✗ *I'm agree* — the adjective trap

Russian «Я согласен» is an adjective: a short-form predicate with no verb in the present. Translated word by word it produces a *be* where English has none.

> ✗ *I**'m agree** with you.*  ✓ *I **agree** with you.*  
> ✗ *I**'m disagree**.*  ✓ *I **disagree**.*

### 6. State verbs split where Russian does not

Russian uses «думаю» for both "I hold the opinion" and "I am thinking about," and «вижу» for both "I perceive" and "I am meeting with." English forces a choice, and the choice is a difference of meaning rather than of style:

> «Я думаю, что это ошибка» → *I **think** it's a mistake.*  
> «Я думаю о переезде» → *I**'m thinking** about moving.*

> «Я вижу твою точку зрения» → *I **see** your point.*  
> «Я встречаюсь с врачом в три» → *I**'m seeing** the doctor at three.*

The [contrast table above](#the-verbs-that-change-meaning-in-the-continuous) is the list to work through, and it is short enough to learn outright.

**One thing that does transfer.** Russian uses the present for arranged near-future events — «Завтра я еду в Москву» — exactly as English does: *I'm going to Moscow tomorrow.* The instinct is already correct; it just has to be trusted rather than overridden with *will*.

## Quick reference

**The form.**

```
   am / is / are  +  verb-ing

   I'm working.        Am I working?        I'm not working.
   You're working.     Are you working?     You aren't / you're not working.
   He's working.       Is he working?       He isn't / he's not working.
```

**The spelling, in seven lines.**

1. Add *-ing*: *work → working*.
2. Drop a silent *-e*: *make → making*.
3. Keep the *-e* after a vowel: *see → seeing*, *dye → dyeing*.
4. *-ie* → *-y-*: *die → dying*, *lie → lying*.
5. Double a final consonant after a stressed single vowel: *run → running*, *begin → beginning*, *prefer → preferring* — but *offer → offering*.
6. *-c* → *-ck*: *panic → panicking*.
7. A final *-y* never changes: *study → studying*.

**Six sentences to remember.**

> **The phone's ringing.** — right now  
> **I'm reading a great book.** — around now, not this second  
> **I'm living with my parents until June.** — temporary, against *I live in Denver*  
> **It's getting colder.** — changing  
> **We're meeting at six.** — arranged  
> **He's always losing his keys.** — a complaint, not a frequency

**Four rules that cover most of the errors.**

1. **Never drop *am/is/are*, and never add *do*.** The auxiliary is the whole tense.
2. **The choice is fact versus progress, not past versus now.** Both tenses are present.
3. **State verbs take the simple — unless the verb has an action meaning**, in which case the continuous is what selects it.
4. **The continuous future needs an arrangement.** A schedule takes the simple; a prediction takes *going to* or *will*.
