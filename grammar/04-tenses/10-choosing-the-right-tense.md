# Choosing the right tense

> The twelve forms are the easy part. This is the section about picking one — the contrasts that actually come up, each with a decision procedure, and the signals that settle them.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The nine sections before this one each describe a form: how it is built, what it means, where it turns up. This one is about the gap between knowing the forms and using them, and the gap is real. A learner who can conjugate all twelve perfectly and still writes ✗ *I live here since 2019* does not have a form problem. They have a **choice** problem, and no amount of extra conjugation practice will fix it.

The good news is that the choice is not twelve-way. English does not offer you twelve independent options and ask you to pick. It offers you **one time** — which the situation has usually already decided — and then **two yes-or-no switches**:

```
        time                aspect switch 1          aspect switch 2
        ----------------------------------------------------------------
        present    ×        perfect?  yes / no   ×   continuous?  yes / no
        past
        future
```

Three times, two binary switches: 3 × 2 × 2 = twelve. That is where the twelve come from, and it means that every tense decision you will ever make is really two small decisions, each with two answers. The rest of this section is about how to answer them.

## The two questions that decide everything

### Question 1 — is the time frame closed?

Not "is the action finished" — that is a different and less useful question. **Is the stretch of time you are talking about over and sealed off from now?**

> I **wrote** three emails **this morning**. *(it is now afternoon — the morning is closed)*  
> I**'ve written** three emails **this morning**. *(it is still morning — the frame is open)*

Same three emails, same finished writing. What differs is whether the container is shut. A closed container takes a plain past; an open one takes a perfect, because the perfect is the form that reaches from a reference point back into the time before it without letting go.

The same question, asked at a past reference point, is what chooses the [past perfect](07-past-perfect.md):

> By the time the movie started, she **had eaten** dinner. *(the frame closes when the movie starts, and the dinner reaches back from there)*

And at a future one, the [future perfect](09-the-future.md):

> By Friday I**'ll have finished** it. *(the frame closes at Friday)*

**One question, three times.** *Have* + past participle always means the same thing: this event reaches back to the point I am standing on. All that changes is where you are standing — *have* for now, *had* for a past moment, *will have* for a future one.

### Question 2 — are you pointing at the activity or at the result?

This is the continuous switch, and again it is one question with the same answer everywhere.

> I**'ve painted** the kitchen. *(look at the kitchen — the result)*  
> I**'ve been painting** the kitchen. *(look at me — the activity, and the paint on my hands)*

> She **read** the report. *(the report got read)*  
> She **was reading** the report. *(that is what she was busy with)*

> He**'ll call** at six. *(the call as an event)*  
> He**'ll be calling** at six. *(what will be going on at six)*

The continuous puts you **inside** the event, watching it unfold with no view of its edges. The simple stands **outside** it and sees it whole — with a beginning and an end, even if neither is mentioned. Every simple-against-continuous contrast in English is that one difference wearing different clothes.

### Running both questions at once

Answer the two and the form falls out. Here is one verb, one subject, and all four combinations at each of two times:

| | Not perfect | Perfect |
|---|---|---|
| **Not continuous** | *I **fix** bikes.* / *I **fixed** it.* | *I**'ve fixed** it.* |
| **Continuous** | *I**'m fixing** it.* | *I**'ve been fixing** it all day.* |

| | Not perfect | Perfect |
|---|---|---|
| **Not continuous** | *She **wrote** the memo.* | *She **had written** the memo.* |
| **Continuous** | *She **was writing** the memo.* | *She **had been writing** the memo.* |

There is no thirteenth box, and there is no form that means something the two switches do not account for. When a choice feels impossible, it is almost always because one of the two questions has not actually been asked.

## Simple against continuous: one idea, not three rules

Most references teach this contrast three times — once under the present, once under the past, once under the future — and a learner comes away with three rules that look unrelated. They are one rule. Here it is in one line, and then in every place it applies.

**The simple form views the event from outside, as a complete whole. The continuous form views it from inside, as something in progress.**

### The five pairs, side by side

Read down the right-hand column and the family resemblance is unmistakable.

| Time | Simple | Continuous | The difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present | *I **work** in Denver.* | *I**'m working** in Denver.* | a fact about my life / a temporary arrangement |
| Past | *He **read** the file.* | *He **was reading** the file.* | he got through it / that was what he was doing |
| Present perfect | *I**'ve cleaned** the garage.* | *I**'ve been cleaning** the garage.* | it is clean / that is where the morning went |
| Past perfect | *She**'d waited** two hours.* | *She**'d been waiting** two hours.* | the wait as a measured block / the wait still going on then |
| Future | *I**'ll drive** to Tulsa.* | *I**'ll be driving** to Tulsa.* | I will make the trip / that is what I will be doing |

Each row is the same decision. Nothing about the present, the past, or the future changes what the `-ing` is for.

### Complete against in progress

The clearest case, and the one that generates the most misunderstandings when a learner gets it backward:

> I **read** your proposal. *(all of it — you can ask me questions)*  
> I **was reading** your proposal. *(when something interrupted, or that is just where I was)*

> She **cooked** dinner and we **ate**. *(two complete events, in order)*  
> She **was cooking** dinner when the power **went** out. *(a frame, and the event that landed inside it)*

> They **built** the deck last summer. *(finished; it exists)*  
> They **were building** the deck last summer. *(that summer's project; the deck may never have been finished)*

The continuous makes no claim that the event ever ended. That is not a side effect — it is the most useful thing about it:

> I **was writing** a novel. *(and I stopped, probably)*  
> I **wrote** a novel. *(there is a novel)*

### Permanent against temporary

The same distinction, applied to states of affairs rather than to single acts. Inside-the-event means you can see out of both ends of it, which is what makes the continuous read as *for now, not forever*.

> He **lives** with his parents. *(that is his situation)*  
> He**'s living** with his parents. *(while the apartment gets fixed; it is an arrangement)*

> She **teaches** eighth grade. *(her job)*  
> She**'s teaching** eighth grade **this year**. *(this year's assignment)*

> I **take** the bus. *(what I normally do)*  
> I**'m taking** the bus **until the car's out of the shop**. *(a stopgap)*

Notice that neither column is more polite, more formal, or more correct. They make different claims about how long the situation is expected to last, and a listener hears the claim.

### Habit against current behavior

The present simple owns habits. The present continuous, applied to a habit, stops describing the habit and starts describing a phase — or, with *always*, a complaint.

> He **complains** about the food. *(that is a thing he does)*  
> He**'s complaining** about the food. *(right now, at this table)*  
> He**'s always complaining** about the food. *(and I am tired of it)*

> She **forgets** her badge. → She**'s always forgetting** her badge.  
> The printer **jams**. → The printer**'s forever jamming**.

The *always* + continuous pattern is not about frequency. *He always complains* and *He's always complaining* can describe exactly the same number of complaints; the second adds the speaker's irritation, and it is one of the few places where a tense choice carries pure attitude. It works in the past identically — *She was always losing her keys* — and it is covered from the form side in [Present continuous](02-present-continuous.md) and [Past continuous](06-past-continuous.md).

### Where the contrast switches off

Some verbs do not offer the choice at all, because you cannot stand inside a state and watch it unfold — there is nothing to watch. ✗ *I am knowing the answer* is not a subtler version of *I know the answer*; it is not English. That is a big enough topic to have [its own consolidated table below](#state-verbs-across-every-tense).

## Present perfect against past simple

This is the choice that separates good English from nearly-good English, and it is the one Russian speakers get wrong longest, because Russian has nothing to map the present perfect onto. Both English forms come out as *я сделал*.

### The decision procedure

Run these in order and stop at the first one that applies.

1. **Is there a finished past time expression — stated or understood?** *(yesterday, last week, in 2019, at six, ago, when I was a kid, this morning if the morning is over)* → **past simple.** No exceptions in either dialect.
2. **Is the question word *when*?** → **past simple.** ✗ *When have you arrived?* is wrong everywhere.
3. **Is it a sequence of events in a story?** → **past simple**, for all of them. Narrative runs on the past simple; see [Choosing tenses in a narrative](#choosing-tenses-in-a-narrative).
4. **Does a duration reach from the past up to now — *for*, *since*, *how long*?** → **present perfect** (or its continuous). This one is compulsory; the past simple here is an outright error, not a dialect choice.
5. **Is the time frame still open — *today*, *this week*, *this year*, *so far*, *in my life*?** → **present perfect** is the safe form. American speech often uses the past simple anyway; see [below](#what-american-english-actually-does).
6. **Is the point the present result rather than the past event?** → **present perfect.**
7. **None of the above?** → either works, and the past simple is the more common choice in American speech.

### Finished time against unfinished time

The most mechanical part of the rule, and the part that has no exceptions:

| Finished frame → past simple | Open frame → present perfect |
|---|---|
| *I **saw** her **yesterday**.* | *I**'ve seen** her **today**.* *(today isn't over)* |
| *We **hired** four people **last year**.* | *We**'ve hired** four people **this year**.* |
| *He **called** **at nine**.* | *He**'s called** **three times so far**.* |
| *She **lived** in Portland **from 2015 to 2019**.* | *She**'s lived** in Portland **since 2015**.* |
| *I **worked** there **for six years**.* *(and then I left)* | *I**'ve worked** here **for six years**.* *(and I still do)* |
| *They **never met** him.* *(both are dead, or the chance has passed)* | *They**'ve never met** him.* *(the chance is still open)* |

The *for* rows are worth staring at. *For six years* is not a present-perfect marker and not a past-simple marker — it is neutral, and the tense is what tells the reader whether the six years are over. Change nothing but the verb and the meaning of the whole sentence flips.

### Present relevance

The second job of the present perfect is to hand you a past event because of what it means now. The event is finished; the consequence is not.

> I**'ve lost** my keys. *(so I can't get in — that is why I'm telling you)*  
> I **lost** my keys **on Tuesday**. *(a piece of news about Tuesday; maybe I found them)*

> She**'s broken** her wrist. *(it's in a cast)*  
> She **broke** her wrist **skiing in 2019**. *(a fact about her history)*

> I**'ve been** to Japan. *(experience I carry around — ask me anything)*  
> I **went** to Japan **in March**. *(a trip, dated)*

> The train**'s left**. *(we missed it)*  
> The train **left** **ten minutes ago**. *(the same fact, timed)*

Note the pattern in the right-hand column: adding a time phrase forces the past simple, every single time. That is rule 1 doing its work, and it is why *✗ I've lost my keys yesterday* is wrong — not because *lose* resists the perfect, but because *yesterday* closes the frame.

### *Been* against *gone*

A small but constant confusion that only exists in the present perfect:

> She**'s been** to the bank. *(and she's back)*  
> She**'s gone** to the bank. *(and she's still there — or on her way)*

### What American English actually does

Here is where most textbooks aimed at learners quietly teach British rules, so it is worth being blunt.

With **three adverbs** — *just*, *already*, and *yet* — American English accepts the past simple where British English requires the present perfect. Both columns below are standard in the United States, and the left column is what you will hear far more often in speech:

| Standard American speech | Also standard, and standard everywhere |
|---|---|
| *Did you **eat** yet?* | *Have you **eaten** yet?* |
| *I **already told** him.* | *I**'ve already told** him.* |
| *He **just left**.* | *He**'s just left**.* |
| *She **didn't call** yet.* | *She **hasn't called** yet.* |
| ***Did** you **finish** already?* | ***Have** you **finished** already?* |

The same latitude extends, more mildly, to **life-experience questions with *ever***. *Did you ever see the Grand Canyon?* is ordinary American English alongside *Have you ever seen the Grand Canyon?* — the past simple version sounds slightly more casual and often implies a closed period (*back when you lived out west*).

What American English does **not** do — and this is the part that matters, because learners over-apply the freedom:

- ✗ *I **work** here since 2019.* → ✓ *I**'ve worked** here since 2019.* The present simple is never an option for a duration reaching now.
- ✗ *I **worked** here since 2019.* *(meaning I still do)* → ✓ *I**'ve worked** here since 2019.* The past simple is not an option either. **Since always takes a perfect.**
- ✗ *How long **do** you **know** him?* → ✓ *How long **have** you **known** him?*
- ✗ *It's the first time I **saw** this.* → ✓ *It's the first time I**'ve seen** this.*
- ✗ *I**'ve seen** him **yesterday**.* → ✓ *I **saw** him yesterday.* The freedom runs one direction only.

### What to write

A short policy that will never embarrass you:

- **In edited writing**, use the present perfect wherever the strict rule calls for it, including with *just*, *already*, and *yet*. It is correct in every dialect and never sounds stilted in American prose.
- **In speech**, use whichever comes out. *Did you eat yet* is not an error, and correcting an American who says it is a mistake about English, not about them.
- **Never** use a plain past or present with *since*, *for* + a duration reaching now, *so far*, *up to now*, *lately*, or *it's the first time*. That part is not dialectal.

## Present perfect against present perfect continuous

Question 1 has already been answered — both forms are perfect, both reach back from now. This is purely Question 2: result or activity.

| Use the simple when you mean | Use the continuous when you mean |
|---|---|
| the thing is done | the activity filled the time |
| **how many**, **how much** | **how long** |
| a permanent or long-term state | something temporary or recent |
| the result is what matters | the process is what matters |

> I**'ve read** the manual. *(I know what's in it)*  
> I**'ve been reading** the manual. *(and I'm not finished)*

> She**'s written** four chapters. *(count — a result)*  
> She**'s been writing** all weekend. *(duration — an activity)*

> They**'ve painted** the house. *(it's a different color now)*  
> They**'ve been painting** the house. *(there are drop cloths everywhere)*

> Who**'s eaten** my sandwich? *(it's gone)*  
> Who**'s been eating** my sandwich? *(part of it is gone)*

The continuous is also the form for explaining a visible present state by pointing at what caused it:

> Your eyes are red. — I**'ve been chopping** onions.  
> The floor's wet. — It**'s been raining**.

**The overlap.** With a handful of verbs of duration — *live*, *work*, *study*, *teach*, *wait*, *feel* — the two forms mean nearly the same thing and both are ordinary:

> I**'ve lived** here for ten years. = I**'ve been living** here for ten years.  
> She**'s worked** at the paper since 2018. = She**'s been working** at the paper since 2018.

If there is a difference, the continuous leans a shade more temporary. Use either.

**The limit.** State verbs have no continuous, so the simple is the only option: *I**'ve known** her since college*, never ✗ *I've been knowing her*. And a countable result blocks the continuous: ✗ *I've been reading three of his books* → ✓ *I**'ve read** three of his books.*

## Past perfect against past simple

Learners split into two camps here, and both are wrong. One camp never uses the past perfect; the other uses it for everything that happened a long time ago. The past perfect is not "the very past" — it does not mean *further back*. It means **before another past point that is already in play**.

### The decision procedure

1. **Is there a second past point in the context** — a past-tense verb, a *by then*, an *already* — that this event happened before? If not, **past simple**. The past perfect needs a reference point; without one it is simply wrong.
2. **Is the order already obvious** from *after*, *before*, *then*, *first*, *as soon as*, or from plain common sense? If so, the **past simple** is normal and the past perfect optional.
3. **Would the plain past be misread** as "next in the sequence"? Then use the **past perfect** — that is exactly the job it was hired for.

### The minimal pair

> When I **got** there, she **left**. *(she left after I arrived — I probably watched her go)*  
> When I **got** there, she **had left**. *(she left before I arrived — I missed her)*

Nothing else in the sentence distinguishes those two situations. This is the case where the past perfect is compulsory, because the past simple actively communicates the wrong thing.

### Where it is optional

> After he **finished** the report, he **went** home. *(**after** has already given the order)*  
> After he **had finished** the report, he **went** home. *(the same, a little more formal)*

> She **ate** breakfast, **grabbed** her coat and **left**. *(a sequence — never past perfect)*  
> I **turned** off the light and **went** to bed.

> He **couldn't** get in because he **lost** his key. / …because he **had lost** his key. *(both fine)*

### Where it is wrong

> ✗ *I **had gone** to Italy in 2015.* → ✓ *I **went** to Italy in 2015.* *(no second past point)*  
> ✗ *She **had been** born in Ohio.* → ✓ *She **was** born in Ohio.*  
> ✗ *We **had had** dinner and then we **had watched** a movie.* → ✓ *We **had** dinner and then **watched** a movie.*

**Do not chain it.** Once you have stepped back with one past perfect, the rest of the flashback can run in the past simple — the reader knows where they are:

> He remembered the day clearly. He **had woken** at five, **driven** to the coast, and **sat** in the car until it got light.

One *had* does the work; the participles that follow ride along without repeating it.

## Past perfect against past perfect continuous

Question 2, one rung further back. Everything said about *have done* against *have been doing* applies unchanged to *had done* against *had been doing*.

| Simple: result or amount before that point | Continuous: activity running up to that point |
|---|---|
| *She**'d written** three drafts by then.* | *She**'d been writing** all night.* |
| *They**'d sold** the house before he called.* | *They**'d been trying** to sell it for two years.* |
| *I**'d waited** an hour and gave up.* | *I**'d been waiting** an hour when he finally showed up.* |

The continuous is the form that explains a past state, exactly as its present counterpart explains a present one:

> The street was wet — it **had been raining**.  
> Her eyes were red; she**'d been crying**.  
> He was out of breath because he**'d been running**.

Compare the simple in the same slot: *It **had rained** twice that week* is a count of events, not an explanation of the wet street.

## Past continuous against past simple

The one that structures almost every sentence about the past that has two things in it.

### The three configurations

| Configuration | Forms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A short event inside a longer frame | past continuous + past simple | *I **was driving** home when the tire **blew**.* |
| Two events in sequence | past simple + past simple | *I **got** home and **called** her.* |
| Two activities in parallel | past continuous + past continuous | *She **was cooking** while I **was setting** the table.* |

The first row is the workhorse, and the two clauses are not interchangeable. The continuous is the **background**; the simple is the **event that lands in it**. Swap them and you change the story:

> I **was reading** when he **came** in. *(reading was already going on; he arrived into it)*  
> I **read** when he **came** in. *(nonsense, or: his arrival was my cue to start reading)*

### *When* against *while*

> ***While** I **was cooking**, the phone rang.* — *while* introduces the frame, so it prefers a continuous.  
> ***When** the phone **rang**, I **was cooking**.* — *when* introduces the point event, so it prefers a simple.

Both sentences describe the identical situation. *While* + past simple is possible but rare; ✗ *while the phone rang* is not wrong so much as odd, because a ring is not a period you can put something inside.

### What the continuous adds on its own

Without a second clause, the past continuous sets a scene or softens a statement:

> It **was getting** dark. Somewhere a dog **was barking**. *(scene-setting)*  
> I **was wondering** whether you had a minute. *(softened — see [tense as distance](../03-conditionals/06-real-unreal-and-time.md#the-past-tense-as-distance-not-time))*  
> What **were** you **doing** in my office? *(more open-ended than **What did you do**)*

## *Will*, *going to*, and the present continuous

English has no future tense, which is why it has so many futures. Six forms compete for the job, and they divide it by **where the evidence for the future event is located right now**. The full inventory is in [The future](09-the-future.md); this is the choosing part.

| Form | Choose it when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | you are deciding as you speak; predicting from opinion; offering; promising; refusing | *I**'ll get** it.* / *You**'ll like** her.* |
| *be going to* | the intention already existed before you spoke; or present evidence points at it | *I**'m going to quit**.* / *Look — it**'s going to rain**.* |
| present continuous | it is arranged, with a time and usually another person | *I**'m meeting** Dan at four.* |
| present simple | it is on a published schedule | *The train **leaves** at 6:40.* |
| *be about to* | it is seconds away | *We**'re about to start**.* |
| future continuous | it will be in progress at that time, or as a matter of course | *I**'ll be driving** through Denver Friday.* |

### The three that actually collide

**Decision now against decision already made.** This is the cleanest contrast in the set, and it is invisible in the written sentence — you have to know when the decision happened:

> *(phone rings)* I**'ll get** it. — never ✗ *I'm going to get it.*  
> *(you already decided)* I**'m going to get** a new phone this week. — *I'll get a new phone* would sound like you thought of it mid-sentence.  
> A: We're out of milk. B: I**'ll pick** some up. *(deciding now)*  
> A: Why the list? B: I**'m going to pick** up milk on the way home. *(already planned)*

**Prediction from belief against prediction from evidence.**

> I think it**'ll rain** later. *(my opinion)*  
> Look at those clouds — it**'s going to rain**. *(the evidence is right there)*  
> He**'ll be** forty next year. *(a fact about the calendar)*  
> Slow down, you**'re going to hit** something. *(I can see it developing)*

**Intention against arrangement.** Both are already decided; the continuous adds that it is *set up*:

> I**'m going to see** the dentist about this tooth. *(intention — no appointment yet)*  
> I**'m seeing** the dentist at three. *(there is an appointment)*

### The rule that overrides all of it

**No *will* in a time clause.** After *when*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *once*, and *by the time*, a future event takes the present simple — or the present perfect, if it must finish first:

> ✗ *I'll call you when I **will arrive**.* → ✓ *I'll call you when I **arrive**.*  
> ✗ *We'll start as soon as everyone **will be** here.* → ✓ *…as soon as everyone **is** here.*  
> ✓ *I'll send it once I**'ve finished** the edits.*

This is the same constraint that governs *if*-clauses, and it is the single most common Russian-speaker error in the future system. See [Real and unreal](../03-conditionals/06-real-unreal-and-time.md#the-real-grid).

## State verbs across every tense

A **state verb** describes a condition rather than an activity: something that simply holds, with no internal process for the continuous to point at. Because there is no process, there is nothing for `-ing` to show you, and the form is rejected.

> ✗ *I **am knowing** the answer.* → ✓ *I **know** the answer.*  
> ✗ *She **was owning** three cars.* → ✓ *She **owned** three cars.*  
> ✗ *I**'ve been knowing** him for years.* → ✓ *I**'ve known** him for years.*  
> ✗ *This **is belonging** to me.* → ✓ *This **belongs** to me.*

Note the third line: the ban runs through every tense, including the perfects. A state verb takes the simple form in all twelve boxes.

### The inventory

| Category | Verbs |
|---|---|
| **Knowing and thinking** | know, believe, understand, mean, doubt, suppose, realize, recognize, remember, forget, imagine, expect, think *(= be of the opinion)*, feel *(= be of the opinion)*, agree, disagree, find *(= consider)* |
| **Wanting and liking** | want, need, wish, prefer, like, love, hate, dislike, adore, detest, mind, care, desire, hope *(often)*, intend *(often)* |
| **Senses** | see, hear, smell, taste, feel, notice, sound, look *(= appear)*, seem, appear |
| **Having and belonging** | have *(= possess)*, own, possess, belong, owe, lack, contain, include, consist of, comprise, involve, require |
| **Being and measuring** | be, exist, seem, resemble, look like, weigh, measure, cost, fit, suit, equal, matter, depend on, deserve, concern |
| **Effect on someone** | surprise, astonish, impress, please, satisfy, disappoint, mean, promise *(= give grounds for)* |

You do not need to memorize the list. The test is one question: **can you be interrupted in the middle of it?** You can be interrupted while *reading*, *waiting*, or *driving*. You cannot be interrupted while *knowing*, *owning*, or *costing*.

### The verbs that change meaning

Several verbs are stative in one sense and dynamic in another. When the dynamic sense is available, the continuous is not only possible — it is required for that meaning.

| Verb | Stative sense — simple only | Dynamic sense — continuous available |
|---|---|---|
| **have** | possess: *I **have** two brothers.* | experience, do: *I**'m having** lunch.* / *We**'re having** trouble.* |
| **think** | opinion: *I **think** you're right.* | consider: *I**'m thinking** about moving.* |
| **see** | perceive, understand: *I **see** what you mean.* | meet, date: *I**'m seeing** Dana on Friday.* |
| **feel** | opinion: *I **feel** it's a mistake.* | health, touch: *I**'m feeling** better.* / *He**'s feeling** the fabric.* |
| **taste** | quality: *This **tastes** salty.* | act: *The chef **is tasting** the sauce.* |
| **smell** | quality: *It **smells** like smoke.* | act: *She**'s smelling** the flowers.* |
| **look** | appear: *You **look** tired.* | direct the eyes: *I**'m looking** at the map.* |
| **appear** | seem: *It **appears** to be broken.* | perform, show up: *She**'s appearing** at the Fillmore.* |
| **be** | permanent quality: *He **is** rude.* | behaving that way now: *He**'s being** rude.* |
| **weigh** | how heavy: *It **weighs** four pounds.* | act: *The nurse **is weighing** the baby.* |
| **measure** | dimension: *The room **measures** ten by twelve.* | act: *He**'s measuring** the doorway.* |
| **cost** | price: *It **costs** forty dollars.* | calculate: *Accounting **is costing** the project.* |
| **expect** | assume: *I **expect** she's late.* | await, be pregnant: *We**'re expecting** guests.* |
| **mind** | object: *I **don't mind**.* | look after: *She**'s minding** the store.* |
| **fit** | size: *These jeans **fit**.* | install: *They**'re fitting** a new lock.* |
| **hold** | contain: *The jug **holds** two liters.* | grasp: *She**'s holding** the baby.* |

The *be* row deserves a second look, because it is the most useful one for a learner to own. *He is rude* is a judgment about the person. *He is being rude* is a description of this afternoon, and it leaves his character alone. The same goes for *You're being unfair*, *She's being difficult*, *I was being sarcastic*.

### The exceptions Americans actually make

Textbooks say state verbs never take the continuous. Real speech has a small set of habitual violations, all of them conscious and all of them adding warmth or immediacy:

> I**'m loving** this weather. *(and the famous advertising slogan)*  
> I**'m not understanding** the instructions. *(more tentative than **I don't understand**)*  
> I**'m wanting** a second opinion on this. *(softened, chiefly in business talk)*

These are real and they are informal. Do not write them; do not be surprised by them. The rule to carry into writing is the plain one: **a state verb takes the simple form.**

## Time expressions as signals

Time adverbials are the most reliable external evidence about which tense a sentence wants — often more reliable than a learner's intuition about the situation. Some point at exactly one tense; some allow several and change meaning between them.

| Marker | Tense it calls for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *yesterday*, *last night / week / year* | past simple *(or past continuous for a frame)* | *I **called** her yesterday.* |
| *in 2019*, *in March*, *on Tuesday* | past simple; future forms if the date is ahead | *We **moved** in 2019.* |
| *… ago* | past simple only — never a perfect | *He **left** ten minutes ago.* |
| *when* + past clause | past simple, past continuous, or past perfect | *When I **arrived**, she **was leaving** / **had left**.* |
| *while* | past continuous *(or present continuous)* | *While I **was cooking**…* |
| *as soon as*, *by the time*, *once*, *until* | present simple for a future event; past perfect in a past frame | *By the time we **got** there, it **had closed**.* |
| *since* + point in time | **present perfect** or present perfect continuous *(past perfect in a past frame)* | *I**'ve worked** here since June.* |
| *for* + duration | **any tense** — the tense decides whether it is over | *I **lived** / **'ve lived** there for six years.* |
| *already* | present perfect; **past simple in American speech** | *I**'ve already sent** it.* / *I **already sent** it.* |
| *yet* | present perfect; **past simple in American speech** | *Have you **eaten** yet?* / *Did you **eat** yet?* |
| *just* | present perfect; **past simple in American speech** | *She**'s just left**.* / *She **just left**.* |
| *ever*, *never* | present perfect; past simple in a closed period | *Have you **ever been** to Rome?* |
| *so far*, *up to now*, *to date* | present perfect only | *We**'ve had** nine applicants so far.* |
| *lately*, *recently* | present perfect or present perfect continuous; past simple for one dated event | *I**'ve been sleeping** badly lately.* / *She **recently quit**.* |
| *today*, *this morning*, *this week* | present perfect if the frame is open; past simple once it closes | *I**'ve had** three meetings today.* |
| *now*, *right now*, *at the moment* | present continuous *(present simple for states)* | *He**'s working** right now.* |
| *always*, *usually*, *often*, *never*, *every day* | present simple *(continuous with **always** = complaint)* | *She **takes** the 7:10.* |
| *these days*, *nowadays*, *this month* | present continuous for a temporary phase | *I**'m taking** the bus these days.* |
| *by* + future time | future perfect | *I**'ll have finished** by Friday.* |
| *this time next week* | future continuous | *This time next week I**'ll be flying** home.* |
| *tomorrow*, *next week*, *soon* | any future form — the choice is about evidence, not time | *I**'m seeing** him tomorrow.* |
| *how long…?* | present perfect or present perfect continuous | *How long **have** you **been** waiting?* |
| *when…?* *(question word)* | past simple — **never** the present perfect | *When **did** you **get** here?* |
| *the first time*, *the last time*, *it's the third time* | present perfect after *it's the … time* | *It's the third time she**'s called**.* |

### The markers that allow several

Four of these are worth isolating, because they are the ones that trip up learners who have memorized the table as if every row were one-to-one.

***For*** is not a present-perfect word. It measures a stretch, and the tense says where the stretch sits:

> I **waited** for an hour. *(and then something ended it)*  
> I**'ve waited** for an hour. *(and I'm still waiting, or just stopped)*  
> I**'ve been waiting** for an hour. *(the same, emphasizing the wait)*  
> I**'d waited** for an hour. *(before some past point)*  
> I**'ll wait** for an hour. *(future)*

***Recently*** splits by aspect. With a single finished event, the past simple is normal — *She recently quit*. With a state or repeated activity reaching now, the perfect — *I've been feeling better recently*.

***This morning*** is the standard demonstration of the open-frame rule. At 10 a.m.: *I**'ve had** two cups of coffee this morning*. At 4 p.m.: *I **had** two cups of coffee this morning*. Nothing about the coffee changed.

***Always*** takes three tenses and means three things: *He **always parks** there* (habit), *He**'s always parking** there* (complaint), *He**'s always parked** there* (throughout the period up to now).

## Choosing tenses in a narrative

Telling a story is the one place where all the choices above have to be made in a row, and where getting one wrong scrambles the reader's sense of what happened when. Three tenses divide the work, and they divide it cleanly.

| Tense | Its job in a story | What it does to the clock |
|---|---|---|
| **Past simple** | the events, in the order they happened | moves the clock forward |
| **Past continuous** | the situation they happened inside | holds the clock still |
| **Past perfect** | what was already true when the story reached that point | jumps the clock backward |

The past simple is the spine. Every time you use one, the reader advances. The other two are both ways of *not* advancing — one by widening the current moment, one by stepping out of the line entirely — which is why a story written entirely in the past simple reads as a list, and a story with no past simple at all never gets anywhere.

### A worked paragraph

> It **was raining** when she **pulled** into the lot. She **sat** in the car for a minute with the wipers going, then **grabbed** the folder off the passenger seat and **ran** for the door. Inside, the lobby **was empty**. The receptionist **had gone** home — it **was** almost seven — and somebody **had left** a light on in the conference room at the end of the hall. She **checked** her phone. Dan **had been calling** all afternoon, four times, and she still **hadn't listened** to the messages. She **pushed** the conference room door open.

Read it again with the labels attached:

| Verb | Form | Why that one |
|---|---|---|
| *was raining* | past continuous | the weather is the frame; it was going on before and after |
| *pulled* | past simple | the first event of the story — the clock starts |
| *sat*, *grabbed*, *ran* | past simple | three events in order; each one advances the clock |
| *was empty* | past simple *(state)* | a state verb describing the scene, not an event |
| *had gone* | past perfect | the receptionist left **before** she arrived — a step backward |
| *was* | past simple | an aside, true at the story's present moment |
| *had left* | past perfect | also earlier than her arrival; the flashback continues |
| *checked* | past simple | back on the spine — the clock moves again |
| *had been calling* | past perfect continuous | a repeated activity filling the time **up to** the story's now |
| *hadn't listened* | past perfect | still earlier than the checking |
| *pushed* | past simple | forward again |

Two things are worth extracting from that. First, **the past perfect always comes in a cluster** — you step out of the line, do what you came to do, and step back. Alternating a past perfect with a past simple sentence by sentence makes the reader seasick. Second, the past continuous and the past perfect continuous are doing the same job at two different depths: both describe an activity spread across time rather than a point on the line.

### The other narrative options

Two forms outside this trio show up in storytelling and are worth naming so they are not mistaken for errors:

- **The historical present.** Conversational American storytelling switches into the present simple and continuous for immediacy: *So I walk in, and she's sitting there with my laptop.* It is standard in speech and in some journalism; it is a stylistic choice, not a mistake.
- ***Would* and *used to*** for repeated past habits, as against the past simple for single events: *Every summer we **would drive** to the lake.* *He **used to** smoke.* [Past simple](05-past-simple.md) covers the split.

## The flowchart

When a sentence stalls, run it through this. It is every question in this section, in the order that resolves them fastest.

```
  What time are you talking about?
  |
  +-- NOW ----------------------------------------------------------------+
  |     Is it happening at this moment, or a temporary phase?             |
  |       yes -> PRESENT CONTINUOUS   (I'm working on it.)                |
  |       no  -> is it a habit, a fact, a state?                          |
  |               yes -> PRESENT SIMPLE   (I work downtown.)              |
  |                                                                       |
  +-- PAST, WITH A FINISHED TIME (yesterday, in 2019, ago, when...) ------+
  |     Was it the frame around another event?                            |
  |       yes -> PAST CONTINUOUS      (I was driving when it happened.)   |
  |       no  -> did it happen BEFORE another past event already in play? |
  |               yes -> was the activity itself the point?               |
  |                        yes -> PAST PERFECT CONTINUOUS                 |
  |                        no  -> PAST PERFECT   (She had left.)          |
  |               no  -> PAST SIMPLE  (I saw her yesterday.)              |
  |                                                                       |
  +-- PAST, BUT REACHING NOW (since, for, so far, still true, result) ----+
  |     Are you counting results, or measuring an activity?               |
  |       results -> PRESENT PERFECT  (I've read it twice.)               |
  |       activity -> PRESENT PERFECT CONTINUOUS (I've been reading.)     |
  |       (state verb? -> PRESENT PERFECT, always)                        |
  |                                                                       |
  +-- FUTURE -------------------------------------------------------------+
        Is it inside a time clause (when/as soon as/until/by the time)?
          yes -> PRESENT SIMPLE  (I'll call when I get there.)
          no  -> where is the evidence right now?
                   on a schedule        -> PRESENT SIMPLE
                   in an arrangement    -> PRESENT CONTINUOUS
                   in an intention, or
                     in what you can see-> BE GOING TO
                   in your head, decided
                     as you speak       -> WILL
                   in progress at that
                     future moment      -> FUTURE CONTINUOUS
                   a deadline it beats  -> FUTURE PERFECT
```

## If you remember nothing else

| Situation | Form | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Finished time named *(yesterday, in 2019, ago)* | past simple | ✗ present perfect |
| Duration reaching now *(since, for, how long)* | present perfect | ✗ present simple, ✗ past simple |
| Question starting with *when* | past simple | ✗ present perfect |
| A sequence of story events | past simple throughout | ✗ past perfect for all of them |
| Background to a past event | past continuous | ✗ past simple |
| Earlier than a past point already mentioned | past perfect | ✗ past simple, when order is unclear |
| A state verb, any time at all | simple form | ✗ any continuous |
| After *when / as soon as / until / by the time*, future | present simple | ✗ *will* |
| Arranged plan with a time and a person | present continuous | ✗ *will* |
| Decision made as you speak | *will* | ✗ *be going to* |

## What Russian speakers get wrong

Every error below comes from the same source: Russian and English carve up time with different tools, and the tools do not line up one to one.

### Aspect is not the continuous

Russian has two aspects, **несовершенный** and **совершенный**, and they are marked on the verb itself. English has two aspects too — perfect and continuous — but they are marked with auxiliaries and they mean different things. The tempting equation *несовершенный = continuous, совершенный = simple* is wrong in both directions.

> *Я **читал** эту книгу.* — imperfective, one Russian sentence, at least five English ones:

| English | What it claims |
|---|---|
| *I **read** that book.* | I got through it, at some point |
| *I**'ve read** that book.* | I know it — experience I still have |
| *I **was reading** that book.* | it was in progress at some past moment |
| *I**'ve been reading** that book.* | and I'm not finished |
| *I **used to read** that book.* | repeatedly, back then |

Russian does not choose between those. English forces a choice every time, and no amount of attention to the Russian aspect will make it for you — the information simply is not in the Russian sentence.

Note especially that the imperfective *читал* often becomes the English **simple** past, and the perfective *прочитал* often becomes the English **present perfect**. Mapping aspect to aspect produces the wrong answer as often as the right one.

### One Russian past, five English options

| Russian | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Он **ушёл**.* *(и его нет)* | *He**'s left**.* | present result — he isn't here |
| *Он **ушёл** в шесть.* | *He **left** at six.* | finished time named |
| *Он **уходил**, когда я вошёл.* | *He **was leaving** when I came in.* | frame around an event |
| *Когда я пришёл, он уже **ушёл**.* | *When I got there, he **had** already **left**.* | earlier than a past point |
| *Он **уходил** каждый день в шесть.* | *He **used to leave** at six.* / *He **left** at six every day.* | past habit |

The middle three are all *ушёл* or *уходил* in Russian. English has a different form for each, and the Russian gives you no signal about which — only the situation does.

### One Russian future, ten English options

*Я поговорю с ней завтра* is a single Russian future. Depending on what is behind it, English offers all of these:

| English | When you would say it |
|---|---|
| *I**'ll talk** to her tomorrow.* | deciding right now |
| *I**'m going to talk** to her tomorrow.* | you had already decided |
| *I**'m talking** to her tomorrow.* | it is arranged; she knows |
| *I**'m about to talk** to her.* | in the next minute |
| *I**'ll be talking** to her tomorrow anyway.* | it is happening as a matter of course |
| *I**'ll have talked** to her by tomorrow.* | it will be done before a deadline |
| *I**'m due to talk** to her tomorrow.* | scheduled, official |
| *I**'m supposed to talk** to her tomorrow.* | expected of me, maybe reluctantly |
| *I**'m to talk** to her tomorrow.* | formal instruction from above |
| *My call with her **is** at four tomorrow.* | a fixed item on a schedule |

None of this is in the Russian. The good news: *will* and *be going to* cover most of it, and choosing wrong between those two is a small error. The bad news: using *will* for an arrangement — ✗ *I will meet Dan tomorrow at four*, when the meeting is set — is the one that consistently sounds foreign.

### The present-for-duration error

The single most frequent error Russian speakers make in English, in any topic. Russian uses the **present** tense for a situation that started in the past and continues:

> *Я **живу** здесь пять лет.* → ✗ *I **live** here for five years.* → ✓ *I**'ve lived** here for five years.*  
> *Я **работаю** здесь с 2019 года.* → ✗ *I **am working** here since 2019.* → ✓ *I**'ve been working** here since 2019.*  
> *Сколько ты его **знаешь**?* → ✗ *How long **do** you **know** him?* → ✓ *How long **have** you **known** him?*  
> *Мы **ждём** уже час.* → ✗ *We **are waiting** for an hour already.* → ✓ *We**'ve been waiting** for an hour.*  
> *Я не **видел** её сто лет.* → ✗ *I **didn't see** her for ages.* → ✓ *I **haven't seen** her for ages.*

The repair is a single reflex: **если по-русски настоящее время + *уже*, *с*, or a stretch of time — the English is a present perfect.**

### The perfective-past-for-result error

The mirror image, and it is how the present perfect is *supposed* to be reached from Russian:

> *Я **потерял** ключи.* → ✓ *I**'ve lost** my keys.* *(and I don't have them)*  
> *Я **потерял** ключи **вчера**.* → ✓ *I **lost** my keys yesterday.* *(the adverb closes the frame)*  
> *Ты **уже поел**?* → ✓ *Have you **eaten** yet?* — or, in American speech, *Did you **eat** yet?*  
> *Я **никогда не был** в Японии.* → ✓ *I**'ve never been** to Japan.*

Learners who have not met the present perfect say *I lost my keys* for all four. Learners who have just met it say *I've lost my keys yesterday* for all four. The adverb decides.

### Two smaller ones

**The future in a time clause.** Russian requires the future after *когда* and *если*; English forbids it. *Когда он **придёт**, я позвоню* → ✗ *When he **will come**, I'll call* → ✓ *When he **comes**, I'll call.* This is the same rule as in [conditionals](../03-conditionals/06-real-unreal-and-time.md#the-real-grid).

**The past perfect, underused.** Russian marks sequence with *уже*, *сначала*, *до того как* rather than with a tense, so the past perfect feels redundant and gets skipped. *Когда я пришёл, она **ушла*** is ✓ *When I arrived, she **had left***, not ✗ *she left* — which in English says she left after you got there.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I **live** here since 2019.* | *I**'ve lived** here since 2019.* | *Since* requires a perfect. The present tense cannot reach backward. |
| *I**'ve seen** her yesterday.* | *I **saw** her yesterday.* | A finished time expression forces the past simple, in every dialect. |
| *When **have** you **arrived**?* | *When **did** you **arrive**?* | *When* asks for a point in finished time; the present perfect has none to give. |
| *How long **are** you **waiting**?* | *How long **have** you **been** waiting?* | The wait started in the past and reaches now. |
| *I **am knowing** the answer.* | *I **know** the answer.* | A state has no internal process for the continuous to show. |
| *I**'ve been knowing** him for years.* | *I**'ve known** him for years.* | The ban on the continuous runs through the perfects too. |
| *She **had gone** to Italy in 2015.* | *She **went** to Italy in 2015.* | The past perfect needs a second past point. Alone, it is not "extra past." |
| *He **ate**, then he **had left**.* | *He **ate**, then he **left**.* | A sequence in order runs on the past simple throughout. |
| *I **read** when he came in.* | *I **was reading** when he came in.* | The background activity takes the continuous; the arrival is the event. |
| *I'll call you when I **will get** there.* | *I'll call you when I **get** there.* | No *will* after *when*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before*, *by the time*. |
| *I **will meet** Dan tomorrow at four.* *(already arranged)* | *I**'m meeting** Dan tomorrow at four.* | *Will* does not report an arrangement; the present continuous does. |
| *Look at those clouds — it **will rain**.* | *…it**'s going to rain**.* | Evidence in front of you calls for *be going to*. |
| *I **am going to get** it.* *(phone rings)* | *I**'ll get** it.* | A decision made at the moment of speaking takes *will*. |
| *It's the first time I **saw** this.* | *It's the first time I**'ve seen** this.* | *It's the … time* frames an open count reaching now. |
| *I**'ve been reading** three of his books.* | *I**'ve read** three of his books.* | A counted result takes the simple perfect; the continuous measures time, not quantity. |
| *He **is** rude to the waiter tonight.* | *He**'s being** rude to the waiter tonight.* | The continuous *be* describes behavior now; the simple describes character. |
| *We **have** dinner when you called.* | *We **were having** dinner when you called.* | *Have* = eat is dynamic, and it was the frame. |
| *She **teaches** fourth grade this year.* *(normally second)* | *She**'s teaching** fourth grade this year.* | A temporary arrangement takes the continuous. |
| *I **didn't finish** yet.* | *I **haven't finished** yet.* — or, in speech, *I **didn't finish** yet.* | Both are heard in America; only the perfect is safe in writing. |
| *The ground was wet — it **rained**.* | *The ground was wet — it **had been raining**.* | The activity ran up to the past moment being described. |

## Quick reference

**The two questions, one more time.**

1. **Is the time frame closed?** Closed → plain past. Open, or the event reaches the point you are standing on → add *have* / *had* / *will have*.
2. **Activity or result?** Activity in progress → add *be* + `-ing`. Complete, whole, counted, or a state → leave it simple.

**The four compulsory rules.** Everything else in this section is a preference; these four are not.

> *Since*, *for* reaching now, *how long*, *so far*, *it's the first time* → **present perfect**. Nothing else is available.  
> A named finished time — *yesterday*, *in 2019*, *ago*, *when I was ten* → **past simple**. Nothing else is available.  
> After *when*, *as soon as*, *before*, *after*, *until*, *once*, *by the time* with future meaning → **present simple**, never *will*.  
> A state verb → **simple form**, in all twelve boxes.

**The three-way past.**

> I **saw** her. — an event, in finished time  
> I**'ve seen** her. — the event matters now  
> I**'d seen** her. — before another past event you have already named

**The three-way present.**

> I **work** downtown. — my job  
> I**'m working** downtown. — for now  
> I**'ve worked** downtown for six years. — started then, still true

**The future, by where the evidence is.**

> In your head, just now → *I**'ll** do it.*  
> In a plan you already had → *I**'m going to** do it.*  
> In a calendar, with another person → *I**'m doing** it at four.*  
> In a published schedule → *It **starts** at four.*

The answers: *I **worked** in Denver for six years* → *I**'ve worked** in Denver for six years* (delete *and then I moved*); *We**'ve lived** here since 2011* → *We **lived** here from 2011 to 2019*; *They **built** the deck last summer* → *They**'ve just built** the deck*.
