# The past perfect continuous

> *Had been* + `-ing`: an activity that ran up to a moment in the past — how long it lasted, what it left behind, and why English needs a form the past perfect simple cannot replace.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The past perfect continuous puts three pieces of information into one verb phrase. There is a **past moment** — a phone call, an arrival, a discovery, a deadline. There is an **activity** that was already running before that moment. And there is a claim about that activity: it was **in progress**, not finished, and it had lasted a while.

> I **had been waiting** for an hour when she finally called.

Three things are true in that sentence and only one of them is stated outright. She called — that is the past moment, and it sits in the past simple. The waiting started an hour before the call. And the waiting was still going on when the phone rang. The tense carries the second and third by itself.

This is the form learners skip. It is long, it is easy to replace with something shorter, and nothing terrible happens when you do. But replacing it costs meaning every time. *I waited an hour* does not say the call interrupted anything. *I had waited an hour* says the waiting was over. *I was waiting an hour* is not English at all. Only *I had been waiting for an hour* says: the clock was running, and then the phone rang.

**One rule underneath the whole section.** The past perfect continuous measures a stretch of activity **up to a past point**. The past point is not optional — it is what the form is for, and a past perfect continuous with nothing to measure up to is almost always the wrong tense. Everything below is a consequence of that one sentence.

```
        earlier                         a past moment                     now
        ───────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┼──►
              ●═══════════════════════════════►●
              the activity, running up to the moment
              I had been waiting                when she finally called
```

## The form

### The pattern

| Slot | Word | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| auxiliary 1 | ***had*** | invariant — the same for *I*, *you*, *he*, *she*, *it*, *we*, *they* |
| auxiliary 2 | ***been*** | never changes; it is the past participle of *be* |
| main verb | **verb + `-ing`** | the present participle, whatever the verb |

*Had* + *been* + `-ing`. Three words, no agreement, no irregular participle to remember — the only irregular form in the phrase is *been*, and it is the same in every sentence. Whatever the main verb does in its other tenses, here it appears as the plain `-ing` form: *go* → *had been going*, *write* → *had been writing*, *put* → *had been putting*, *lie* → *had been lying*.

This is the easiest verb phrase in English to build and one of the hardest to know when to use. The rest of the section is about the second half of that sentence.

### Affirmative

> I **had been working** since six.  
> She **had been driving** all night.  
> The pipes **had been leaking** for months before anyone noticed.  
> By the time the tow truck came, we **had been sitting** on the shoulder for two hours.

### Negative

*Not* goes after *had*, and contracts with it.

> I **hadn't been sleeping** well.  
> He **hadn't been going** to class, which explained the grade.  
> They **had not been paying** attention. — the full form, formal or emphatic

There is no second place for the negative. ✗ *I had been not sleeping* and ✗ *I had not been not sleeping* are not options, and *had been* cannot be split: ✗ *I had never not been sleeping* is a different sentence about something else entirely.

The negative of this tense is less common than you might expect, and that is a fact about meaning rather than grammar. The continuous asserts that an activity was running; denying that an activity was running usually calls for something simpler — *He wasn't going to class* or *He hadn't gone to class in weeks*. When the negative does appear, it is almost always explaining a past state: *She hadn't been eating, and it showed.*

### Questions

Invert the subject with *had*. *Been* and the participle stay in order behind it.

> **Had** you **been waiting** long?  
> **Had** they **been living** there before the fire?  
> **How long had** he **been driving** when he pulled over?  
> **What had** she **been doing** all afternoon?  
> **Why had** the alarm **been going off** all night?

***How long*** is the question word this tense was built for. If you are ever unsure whether a sentence wants the past perfect continuous, try asking *how long* about it — if the question makes sense, the tense probably does too.

### Negative questions

> **Hadn't** you **been waiting** for this all year?  
> **Hadn't** she **been complaining** about that knee for months?  
> **Had** you **not been listening**? — formal, and colder

The contracted version carries an expectation: the speaker thinks the answer is yes and is half-arguing for it. The uncontracted *Had you not been listening?* is not the polite version — it is the sharper one, because separating *not* puts stress on it. This is the same split that runs through every negative question in English.

### Short answers

The answer stops at *had*. Everything after it is dropped.

> **Had** you **been waiting** long? — **Yes, I had.** / **No, I hadn't.**  
> **Had** they **been arguing**? — **Yes, they had.** / **No, they hadn't.**

*Yes, I had been* is heard, but it sounds like the opening of a longer sentence that got cut off. ✗ *Yes, I had been waiting* as a short answer is not wrong so much as not a short answer. And ✗ *Yes, I was* answers a different question — it belongs to *Were you waiting?*

### Contractions

*Had* contracts to ***'d*** on every pronoun, on *there*, and — in speech — on ordinary nouns.

| Full | Contracted | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I had been | **I'd been** | *I'd been trying to reach you.* |
| you had been | **you'd been** | *You'd been saying that for weeks.* |
| he / she / it had been | **he'd / she'd / it'd been** | *She'd been crying.* |
| we / they had been | **we'd / they'd been** | *They'd been waiting outside.* |
| there had been | **there'd been** | *There'd been people camping in the lot.* |
| a noun subject (speech only) | *Dana'd been*, *the car'd been* | heard constantly, written almost never |
| had not been | **hadn't been** | *He hadn't been feeling well.* |

Written *'d* is ambiguous between *had* and *would*, and in this tense the ambiguity resolves itself instantly: **what follows tells you which.** *Been* + `-ing` can only follow *had*. *Would* would give you *'d be waiting*, with no *been* in it.

> **I'd been waiting** — *had*, because *been* follows  
> **I'd be waiting** — *would*, because a bare *be* follows

In fast American speech the whole auxiliary cluster reduces to almost nothing: *I'd been waiting* comes out as /aɪd bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/ and in casual speech closer to /aɪd bən/, which is a single unstressed syllable. This is the main reason learners do not hear the tense in conversation even when it is being used at them all day. The reductions are catalogued in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

### Where the adverbs go

| Adverb type | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *just*, *already*, *only*, *still*, *always*, *never* | between *had* and *been* | *He **had just been** talking to her.* |
| *for* / *since* phrases | after the participle | *She **had been waiting since** noon.* |
| *all morning*, *all day*, *all week* | at the end | *They **had been arguing all morning**.* |
| *quietly*, *steadily*, *hard* | before the participle or at the end | *It **had been raining steadily**.* |

Two of those deserve a line. ***Only*** in this slot means "for no more than" and it is doing real work: *She had only been working there a month when they promoted her* is a comment about how short the month was. And ***always*** with this tense is rare and usually means something irritable — *He had always been finding reasons not to come* — because habitual *always* normally takes the simple.

### The full paradigm

Verb: *wait*. This table is the whole of the form, and there is nothing else to learn about it.

| | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | I had been waiting | I hadn't been waiting | Had I been waiting? |
| you | you had been waiting | you hadn't been waiting | Had you been waiting? |
| he / she / it | he had been waiting | he hadn't been waiting | Had he been waiting? |
| we | we had been waiting | we hadn't been waiting | Had we been waiting? |
| they | they had been waiting | they hadn't been waiting | Had they been waiting? |

No third-person `-s`, no *was* / *were* split, no participle irregularity. Compare that with the past continuous, which forces a choice between *was* and *were* on every sentence, and the past perfect continuous starts to look like a bargain.

## What it is for

Five uses, and they are five views of the same thing.

### 1. An activity continuing up to a past moment

The core use. Something was running; then something else happened.

> I **had been waiting** for an hour when she finally called.  
> We **had been driving** for six hours before we found a motel.  
> By the time the doctor saw him, he **had been sitting** in that room since eight.  
> When the fire alarm went off, they **had been rehearsing** for twenty minutes.  
> She **had been talking** for forty-five minutes when someone finally interrupted.

The past moment is supplied by a clause in the past simple — a *when*-clause, a *before*-clause, a *by the time*-clause — or by the surrounding narrative. **The activity usually stops at that moment**, interrupted or concluded by it, but it does not have to: *I had been waiting for an hour when she called, and I went on waiting for another twenty minutes.* The tense says the activity was in progress at the anchor, and stops there.

Note where the anchor clause is *not* in the past perfect. ✗ *I had been waiting for an hour when she had called* pushes both events to the same depth and loses the sequence. The anchor stays in the past simple; that is what makes it an anchor.

### 2. A past activity whose results were visible at a past moment

Here the past moment is not an event that interrupted anything. It is a moment of **observation**, and the tense explains what was observed.

> The ground was wet — it **had been raining**.  
> Her eyes were red. She **had been crying**.  
> He was out of breath because he **had been running**.  
> The kitchen smelled wonderful; someone **had been baking**.  
> There was sawdust everywhere. Somebody **had been working** in there.  
> His hands were black. He **had been changing** the oil.

Read those in order and the shape is always the same: a past-simple observation, then a past perfect continuous that accounts for it. The activity had recently stopped; what remained were the traces.

This is the use that the past perfect simple cannot cover, and the difference is worth stopping on. *It had rained* and *It had been raining* both describe rain before a past moment, but they point at different things:

> **It had rained.** — the rain happened and finished; a completed event in the past of the past  
> **It had been raining.** — the rain had been going on; that is why the ground was wet

Neither one is a stronger claim about quantity. *Had rained* is an event; *had been raining* is a scene. The evidence use needs the scene.

### 3. Duration before a past event

This is the use where the tense is close to obligatory, because nothing else expresses it. The duration is spelled out with *for*, *since*, or an *all* + time phrase, and *how long* asks about it.

| Marker | What follows it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***for*** | a length of time | *He'd been driving **for six hours**.* |
| ***since*** | a starting point | *She'd been living there **since 2015**.* |
| ***all*** + period | a whole stretch | *They'd been arguing **all morning**.* |
| ***how long*** | the question | ***How long** had you been waiting?* |
| ***the whole time*** | co-extension | *He'd been recording it **the whole time**.* |

> We **had been driving** **for** six hours when we finally stopped.  
> She **had been living** in Austin **since** 2015 when the company moved her to Dallas.  
> They **had been arguing** **all morning**, and by lunch neither one was speaking.  
> The machine **had been running** **all night**.  
> **How long had** you **been working** there when they let you go?  
> By March, he **had been looking** for a job **for** eight months.

***For*** measures the stretch; ***since*** names its start. They are not interchangeable and mixing them is one of the two or three most common errors with this tense: ✗ *for 2015*, ✗ *since six hours*. In American speech the *for* is sometimes dropped — *We'd been driving six hours* — and that is ordinary, though it is not dropped before *since*.

Note also that *since* here can take a clause, and that clause goes into the **past simple**, not the past perfect: *She'd been living there since she graduated*, not ✗ *since she had graduated*.

### 4. Explaining a past state or appearance

Use 2 gives the evidence; this is the same mechanism run as an explanation, usually with *because*, *that's why*, or a dash.

> I was exhausted — I **had been packing** boxes since six that morning.  
> He failed the class **because** he **hadn't been going** to lectures.  
> She was furious. We **had been ignoring** her emails for a week.  
> **That's why** he looked so pleased: he **had been planning** it for months.  
> The car overheated. It **had been sitting** in the sun all day.

The order can run either way — explanation first or state first — and the tense does not change.

> I **had been packing** boxes since six, so by noon I was useless.

### 5. A repeated activity up to a past point

The continuous handles repetition as well as it handles a single unbroken stretch, provided the repetition is framed as an ongoing pattern rather than counted.

> She **had been calling** him every night that week.  
> By the time he quit, he **had been taking** the same train for eleven years.  
> They **had been meeting** on Thursdays since the spring.  
> The neighbors **had been complaining** about the noise for months.  
> He **had been seeing** a specialist twice a month.

*Every night*, *on Thursdays*, *twice a month* — these are patterns, not quantities, and the continuous takes them. The moment you count the repetitions instead, the simple takes over: *She had called him nine times that week.* That split is [duration vs. quantity](#duration-vs-quantity), below, and it is the sharpest rule in the section.

### What it never does

Three things this tense is not for, stated plainly because learners reach for it in all three places.

**Not a single completed action.** ✗ *I had been finishing the report before she arrived.* The report was finished; that is a completion, and completions take the past perfect simple: *I had finished the report before she arrived.*

**Not a sequence of past events.** ✗ *He had been getting up, had been showering, and had been leaving.* Narrative sequence is the past simple's job: *He got up, showered, and left.*

**Not a state.** ✗ *I had been knowing her for years.* See [state verbs](#state-verbs-have-no-continuous).

And one structural warning that covers all three: **this tense needs a past anchor.** If there is no later past moment in the sentence or in the two sentences around it, the past perfect continuous has nothing to measure up to, and what you want is either the present perfect continuous (if the anchor is now) or the past simple (if there is no anchor at all).

> ✗ *Last summer I **had been working** at a camp.*  
> ✓ *Last summer I **worked** at a camp.*  
> ✓ *I **had been working** at the camp for three weeks when they closed it.*

## Past perfect continuous vs. past perfect simple

This is the contrast the whole tense lives or dies on, and it is one distinction applied five ways.

### The core split: activity vs. completion

**The continuous is about the activity. The simple is about the result.** The continuous says *this was going on*; the simple says *this got done*.

> I**'d been reading** the report when she came in. — I was in the middle of it  
> I**'d read** the report before the meeting. — I finished it; I knew what was in it

Everything else in this subsection follows from that pair.

### Pairs

Read each row across. The two sentences are about the same past events and make different claims.

| Continuous — the activity | Simple — the completion |
|---|---|
| *She**'d been writing** a novel.* — that was her project | *She**'d written** a novel.* — it existed |
| *They**'d been painting** the kitchen.* — drop cloths, fumes, half a wall | *They**'d painted** the kitchen.* — it was a new color |
| *He**'d been cleaning** all morning.* — how he spent it | *He**'d cleaned** the whole house.* — what he achieved |
| *We**'d been talking** about it for weeks.* — an ongoing conversation | *We**'d talked** about it twice.* — two occasions, counted |
| *I**'d been looking** for my keys.* — that was why I was late | *I**'d found** my keys.* — the search was over |
| *She**'d been studying** for the bar.* — for months, in progress | *She**'d passed** the bar.* — done, with a result |
| *The committee **had been reviewing** the proposal.* — it was under review | *The committee **had reviewed** the proposal.* — the review was complete |
| *He**'d been eating** when the phone rang.* — mid-meal | *He**'d eaten** before he left.* — not hungry |

The last pair is the cleanest test case. *He'd been eating when the phone rang* puts a fork in his hand. *He'd eaten before he left* puts food in his stomach and nothing in his hand.

### Duration vs. quantity

**A stated quantity forces the simple.** This is close to a hard rule, and it is the one to memorize if you memorize nothing else here.

> ✓ *I**'d been reading** for three hours.* — how long  
> ✓ *I**'d read** three chapters.* — how many  
> ✗ *I**'d been reading** three chapters.*

> ✓ *She**'d been calling** him all week.*  
> ✓ *She**'d called** him four times.*  
> ✗ *She**'d been calling** him four times.*

> ✓ *They**'d been writing** emails all afternoon.*  
> ✓ *They**'d written** five emails.*  
> ✗ *They**'d been writing** five emails.*

> ✓ *He**'d been running** for forty minutes.*  
> ✓ *He**'d run** six miles.*  
> ✗ *He**'d been running** six miles.*

The reason is that the continuous frames an activity as unbounded — open at both ends, viewed from inside. A counted quantity is a boundary, and you cannot put a boundary inside a frame that refuses boundaries. *For three hours* is not a boundary of that kind: it measures the frame rather than closing it.

Two things that look like exceptions and are not. *He'd been running six miles every morning* is fine, because *every morning* turns the six miles into a repeated pattern rather than a completed count. And *She'd been drinking three cups of coffee a day* works for the same reason.

| Question the sentence answers | Tense |
|---|---|
| **How long?** | continuous — *had been waiting for an hour* |
| **Since when?** | continuous — *had been waiting since noon* |
| **How many? How much?** | simple — *had made six calls* |
| **How many times?** | simple — *had called four times* |
| **Finished or not?** | simple — *had finished* |
| **What was going on?** | continuous — *had been arguing* |

### Temporary vs. permanent

Where both tenses are grammatical, the continuous leans **temporary, recent, and bounded**; the simple leans **permanent, settled, and open-ended**.

> He **had been living** with his brother. — a stopgap; everyone expected it to end  
> He **had lived** in that house all his life. — permanent, and finished only by the sentence's own past frame

> She **had been teaching** at the community college for a semester. — a fill-in position  
> She **had taught** there for thirty years. — a career

> They**'d been using** my office while theirs was painted. — borrowed  
> They**'d used** that office since the building opened. — theirs

This is a lean, not a rule. *She had been teaching there for thirty years* is perfectly good English and simply puts the emphasis on the thirty years of doing it rather than on the settled fact of it. But a reader feels the difference, and with a short duration the continuous is strongly preferred: *He had been living there for a week* is far more natural than *He had lived there for a week*.

### State verbs have no continuous

Verbs that name a state rather than an activity have no continuous form to put in this tense. The past perfect simple is not a stylistic alternative for them; it is the only form there is.

> ✗ *I **had been knowing** her for years.* ✓ *I **had known** her for years.*  
> ✗ *They **had been owning** the building since 1990.* ✓ *They **had owned** the building since 1990.*  
> ✗ *The jar **had been containing** flour.* ✓ *The jar **had contained** flour.*  
> ✗ *He **had been believing** it the whole time.* ✓ *He **had believed** it the whole time.*  
> ✗ *She **had been needing** a new car for a while.* ✓ *She **had needed** a new car for a while.*  
> ✗ *We **had been belonging** to that club for a decade.* ✓ *We **had belonged** to that club for a decade.*

The core list: **know, believe, understand, mean, realize, recognize, doubt, own, belong, possess, contain, consist of, include, need, want, prefer, seem, appear (= seem), resemble, cost, matter, deserve, hate, like, love**. The full treatment, with the reasoning, is [stative verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md#22-stative-verbs).

*Be* is the extreme case: *had been* **is** the form, and there is no ✗ *had been being* in ordinary use. *I had been there for an hour*, not ✗ *I had been being there*.

And the standard escape hatch applies here as everywhere. **A stative verb with a dynamic sense takes the continuous freely** — because in that sense it is no longer a state verb:

| Verb | State — no continuous | Activity — continuous fine |
|---|---|---|
| *think* | ✗ *I'd been thinking it was Tuesday.* | *I'd been thinking about it for weeks.* |
| *have* | ✗ *She'd been having a car since March.* | *She'd been having trouble with the car.* |
| *see* | ✗ *I'd been seeing what he meant.* | *She'd been seeing a therapist since the spring.* |
| *feel* | ✗ *The blanket had been feeling soft.* | *He'd been feeling sick all week.* |
| *taste* | ✗ *The soup had been tasting salty.* | *The chef had been tasting it all afternoon.* |
| *expect* | ✗ *I'd been expecting it was true.* | *They'd been expecting a baby since February.* |
| *weigh* | ✗ *It had been weighing ten pounds.* | *He'd been weighing his options.* |
| *look* | ✗ *She'd been looking tired.* (= appearing) | *She'd been looking for an apartment.* |

The dual-sense inventory is in [stative verbs that also have dynamic uses](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md#81-stative-verbs-that-also-have-dynamic-uses).

### The verbs where both work

A small group of verbs takes either form with a *for* or *since* phrase and almost no change of meaning. These are the durative activity verbs — things that go on and on by their nature.

**live, work, study, teach, wait, stay, sit, lie, stand, rain, snow, hope, feel, run** (a business), **drive**.

> She **had lived** in Denver for ten years. ≈ She **had been living** in Denver for ten years.  
> He **had worked** there since college. ≈ He **had been working** there since college.  
> They **had studied** together for two semesters. ≈ They **had been studying** together for two semesters.  
> It **had rained** all week. ≈ It **had been raining** all week.

The pairs are close enough that choosing wrong costs you nothing. Where a shade of difference exists, it is the temporary-vs-permanent lean from two subsections up, plus one more: **the continuous connects the activity to the moment more tightly.** *He had been working there since college, and he was still there the day it closed* reads slightly better than the simple, because the continuous keeps the activity alive right up to the anchor.

Two constraints on the group, both worth stating:

- **With *always*, *never*, or *all his life*, use the simple.** *She had always lived in Denver.* ✗ *She had always been living in Denver* is possible but sounds like a complaint.
- **The moment a quantity appears, the group loses its privilege.** *She had lived in four cities* — never ✗ *had been living in four cities*.

### The summary table

| | Past perfect continuous | Past perfect simple |
|---|---|---|
| Form | *had been* + `-ing` | *had* + past participle |
| Focus | the activity | the result |
| Finished? | not necessarily — in progress at the anchor | yes, complete before the anchor |
| Answers | how long, since when, what was going on | how many, how much, whether it was done |
| Takes a quantity? | no | yes |
| State verbs? | no | yes |
| Typical company | *for*, *since*, *all morning*, *how long* | *already*, *by then*, *just*, *never*, *twice* |
| Typical example | *I'd been waiting for an hour.* | *I'd waited long enough.* |
| Evidence use | *The ground was wet — it had been raining.* | — not available |

## Past perfect continuous vs. present perfect continuous

The two tenses are the same tense with the anchor in different places. The present perfect continuous measures up to **now**; the past perfect continuous measures up to **a past moment**. Nothing else about them differs — not the state-verb ban, not the duration-vs-quantity split, not the behavior of *for* and *since*.

```
   present perfect continuous    ●══════════════════════►│ now
                                 I've been waiting for an hour.
                                 (and I am still waiting)

   past perfect continuous       ●═════════►│ then                        │ now
                                 I had been waiting for an hour
                                 when she called.
```

### The parallel table

| | Anchored at now — present perfect continuous | Anchored at a past moment — past perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | *I**'ve been waiting** for an hour.* | *I**'d been waiting** for an hour when she called.* |
| Since a start | *She**'s been living** here since 2015.* | *She**'d been living** there since 2015 when the fire started.* |
| Evidence | *Her eyes are red. She**'s been crying**.* | *Her eyes were red. She**'d been crying**.* |
| Explanation | *He**'s** out of breath — he**'s been running**.* | *He **was** out of breath — he**'d been running**.* |
| Question | ***How long have** you **been waiting**?* | ***How long had** you **been waiting**?* |
| Repetition | *They**'ve been meeting** on Thursdays.* | *They**'d been meeting** on Thursdays until he moved.* |
| Negative | *I **haven't been sleeping** well.* | *I **hadn't been sleeping** well that whole month.* |
| Quantity — both refuse it | ✗ *I**'ve been reading** three chapters.* | ✗ *I**'d been reading** three chapters.* |
| State verbs — both refuse them | ✗ *I**'ve been knowing** her for years.* | ✗ *I**'d been knowing** her for years.* |

**The only change is the auxiliary.** *Have* / *has* becomes *had*; *been* + `-ing` rides along untouched. If you can build one of these tenses you can build the other, and the question is never how to form it but where the anchor is.

### Moving the anchor

There are exactly two reasons the anchor moves back, and both produce a past perfect continuous out of a present perfect continuous.

**Narrative.** The writer has planted the story in the past, so *now* for the reader is a past moment, and every measurement is taken from there.

> She looked around the kitchen. The counters were covered in flour. Somebody **had been baking**.

**Reporting.** Somebody said it with the anchor at their now, and you are reporting from later. That is [backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md), and it has its own subsection below.

> "I**'ve been trying** to reach you all morning," he said. → He said he **had been trying** to reach me all morning.

## Past perfect continuous vs. past continuous

These two are confused constantly, and the confusion is worth a section because the sentences look almost identical.

> I **was waiting** when she called.  
> I **had been waiting** for an hour when she called.

**The past continuous puts you inside the activity at the moment.** It says nothing about how long the activity had been going or when it started. It is a backdrop: the call happened against the waiting.

**The past perfect continuous measures the activity up to the moment.** It adds duration and a starting point, and it points backward from the anchor rather than sitting at it.

| | Past continuous | Past perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Form | *was* / *were* + `-ing` | *had been* + `-ing` |
| Relation to the past moment | in progress **at** it | running **up to** it |
| Says how long? | no | yes, usually explicitly |
| Typical company | *when*, *while*, *at the time* | *for*, *since*, *by the time*, *how long* |
| Example | *I **was cooking** when the power went out.* | *I**'d been cooking** for two hours when the power went out.* |
| Example | *They **were arguing** when I walked in.* | *They**'d been arguing** since breakfast.* |

The practical test: **if the sentence states or implies a duration, you need the perfect.** ✗ *I was waiting for an hour when she called* is the error this test catches — the *for an hour* is measuring backward from the call, and the past continuous cannot measure backward.

> ✗ *I **was waiting** for an hour when she called.*  
> ✓ *I **had been waiting** for an hour when she called.*

One honest caveat, because the rule above is tighter than real speech. *I was waiting for an hour* is perfectly good English **on its own**, meaning the waiting filled an hour — it is the past continuous with a duration of its own and no anchor to measure to. The error only appears when a *when*-clause shows up and the duration has to be counted backward from it. American speakers do produce the ✗ version in casual speech, and they also produce *I waited an hour and then she called*, which sidesteps the problem entirely and is what most people actually say.

## In reported speech

The past perfect continuous is both a **destination** and a **floor** in the backshift system, and it is worth knowing which rows in the table land on it.

**Two forms become the past perfect continuous** when a past-tense reporting verb backshifts them:

| Direct speech | Reported after *said* |
|---|---|
| present perfect continuous — *has been working* | **past perfect continuous** — *had been working* |
| past continuous — *was working* | **past perfect continuous** — *had been working* |

> "I**'ve been trying** to reach you," he said. → He said he **had been trying** to reach me.  
> "I**'ve been feeling** sick all week," she said. → She said she **had been feeling** sick all week.  
> "I **was driving** at the time," he said. → He said he **had been driving** at the time.

**And the past perfect continuous itself does not move**, because there is nothing under it. English has a past and a past-of-the-past and no third step.

> "I **had been waiting** for almost an hour," she said. → She said she **had been waiting** for almost an hour.

Only the finite verb shifts. *Has* becomes *had*; *been working since noon* is untouched — and a *for* or *since* phrase built on a fixed point (*since 2015*, *for ten years*) survives the shift unchanged, since the fixed point did not move.

The honest note about real usage: **the past-continuous row is the one American speakers break most often.** *He said he was driving at the time* is what most people say, and it is not an error. The heavy form earns its keep when the order of events needs to be unmistakable. The whole system, including when to skip the shift, is in [Backshift: the tense changes](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md), and the cases where the shift is blocked or optional are in [When not to backshift](../01-reported-speech/03-no-backshift.md).

## In conditionals

The past perfect continuous is the continuous *if*-clause of a **third conditional** — an unreal past condition, framed as an activity rather than an event.

> **If I had been paying** attention, I would have seen the sign.  
> **If we hadn't been arguing**, we would have heard the doorbell.  
> **If she'd been wearing** a helmet, it would have been a bruise instead of a concussion.

And the matching result clause, when the unreal result is itself an activity, is *would have been* + `-ing`:

> **If we'd left on time, we would have been eating** dinner in Santa Fe by eight.

Both halves are already as far back as English goes, which is why a third conditional survives reporting intact. The mechanics — why the past perfect marks unreality rather than time, what the mixed types do with it, and the full inventory of continuous and perfect forms on either side of the sentence — belong to the conditionals topic and are not repeated here: see [Mixed conditionals and variations](../03-conditionals/02-mixed-and-variations.md#perfect-continuous-on-either-side) and, for the type itself, [The four types](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#third-conditional).

## The passive

Here is the honest answer, because textbooks tend to give the tidy one.

The passive of the past perfect continuous is ***had been being*** + past participle. It is grammatical. It is also **vanishingly rare** — rare enough that most native speakers have never written one and would stumble reading one aloud.

> ? *The house **had been being painted** for three weeks when the money ran out.*

Three *be*-forms in a row is more than English wants to carry, and speakers avoid it automatically. What they use instead, in rough order of frequency:

| Instead of the passive continuous | What is written | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Past perfect simple passive** | *had been* + participle | *The house **had been painted**.* — loses the "in progress," keeps everything else |
| **Active with a vague subject** | *they*, *someone*, *the crew* | ***They'd been painting** the house for three weeks.* |
| **A noun instead of a verb** | *under* + noun | *The house **had been under renovation** for three weeks.* |
| **A process noun as the subject** | *work*, *construction*, *repairs* | ***Work had been going on** for three weeks.* |
| **Recast with *in progress*** | | *The painting **had been in progress** for three weeks.* |

The passive of every tense, this one included, is set out in [Passive, questions and clauses](11-passive-questions-and-clauses.md). The same avoidance operates one step up, in the present perfect continuous — ? *The house has been being painted* is equally awkward — and it is a general fact about English rather than anything specific to this tense. The rule to carry away: **if you find yourself writing *had been being*, rewrite the sentence.** The active version is almost always shorter and always clearer.

For contrast, the two passives that *are* ordinary and do get written:

> The report **had been reviewed** before the meeting. — past perfect simple passive  
> The road **was being repaved** when we drove through. — past continuous passive

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **had been knowing** her for years.* | *I **had known** her for years.* | *Know* is a state verb. It has no continuous in any tense. |
| ✗ *They **had been owning** the shop since 1990.* | *They **had owned** the shop since 1990.* | Same: *own*, *belong*, *contain*, *need*, *believe* are all states. |
| ✗ *I **had been finishing** the report before she arrived.* | *I **had finished** the report before she arrived.* | A completed single action takes the simple. The continuous cannot express completion. |
| ✗ *I **had been reading** three chapters.* | *I **had read** three chapters.* / *I **had been reading** for three hours.* | A counted quantity forces the simple. Duration takes the continuous. |
| ✗ *I **was waiting** for an hour when she called.* | *I **had been waiting** for an hour when she called.* | A duration measured backward from a past anchor needs the perfect. The past continuous sits at the moment; it cannot reach behind it. |
| ✗ *I **had been waiting** when she **had called**.* | *I **had been waiting** when she **called**.* | The anchor clause stays in the past simple. Two past perfects in one sentence destroy the sequence. |
| ✗ *He had been driving **since** six hours.* | *He had been driving **for** six hours.* | *For* takes a length; *since* takes a starting point. |
| ✗ *She'd been living there **for** 2015.* | *She'd been living there **since** 2015.* | Same rule, the other way. |
| ✗ *She'd been waiting since she **had arrived**.* | *She'd been waiting since she **arrived**.* | A *since*-clause takes the past simple. It names the starting point; it is not another layer of pastness. |
| ✗ *Last summer I **had been working** at a camp.* | *Last summer I **worked** at a camp.* | No past anchor, no past perfect. A finished past period takes the past simple. |
| ✗ *I **have been waiting** for an hour when she called.* | *I **had been waiting** for an hour when she called.* | The anchor is a past moment, not now. *Have* measures to the present. |
| ✗ *He **had been been** waiting.* | *He **had been** waiting.* | One *been*. The doubling comes from over-correcting a heard contraction. |
| ✗ *They **had being** working all night.* | *They **had been** working all night.* | *Been*, the past participle — never *being*, the present participle. |
| ✗ *I had been **work** all morning.* | *I had been **working** all morning.* | The main verb is always the `-ing` form. |
| ✗ *The house **had been being painted** for weeks.* | ***They'd been painting** the house for weeks.* | Grammatical but unwritable. Recast it in the active. |
| ✗ *Yes, I had been waiting.* (as a short answer) | *Yes, I had.* | The short answer stops at the auxiliary. |
| ✗ *She **had always been living** in Denver.* | *She **had always lived** in Denver.* | *Always* with a permanent situation takes the simple. |
| ✗ *He said he **has been trying** to reach me.* | *He said he **had been trying** to reach me.* | After a past reporting verb, *has* moves to *had*. Half-shifting is the error. |

## For Russian speakers

**There is no Russian equivalent. Not a difficult one — none.**

This is the honest starting point, and it explains almost every error in the table above. Russian has no perfect tenses at all. What it has is **aspect** — a choice between perfective and imperfective built into the verb itself — and aspect answers a different question from the one English tense answers. Russian asks *was this action complete?* English asks *when was this relative to that?* The two systems overlap enough to be confusing and not enough to be translatable.

What Russian uses where English uses the past perfect continuous is an **imperfective past verb**, usually with **«уже»** and a duration phrase, and usually with the past anchor carried by the surrounding clause rather than by any verb form.

### The parallel pairs

| Russian | English | What the English adds |
|---|---|---|
| «Я **ждал** уже час, когда она наконец позвонила» | *I **had been waiting** for an hour when she finally called.* | the three-word auxiliary; Russian carries it with «уже» |
| «Земля была мокрая — **шёл** дождь» | *The ground was wet — it **had been raining**.* | a tense that marks "before this, and this is the trace" |
| «Она **работала** там с 2015 года, когда компания её перевела» | *She **had been working** there since 2015 when the company transferred her.* | *had*, where Russian has only «работала» |
| «Он **бежал**, поэтому запыхался» | *He was out of breath because he **had been running**.* | the ordering — Russian leaves it to context |
| «Они **спорили** всё утро» | *They **had been arguing** all morning.* | nothing, if there is no anchor; with one, the whole perfect |
| «Сколько времени ты **ждал**?» | ***How long had** you **been waiting**?* | the anchor, again |
| «Я **знал** её уже много лет» | *I **had known** her for years.* | and here the continuous is **forbidden** |

Read the last row against the first. The Russian verbs «ждал» and «знал» are both imperfective past, both take «уже», both look identical in structure. English sends one to *had been waiting* and the other to *had known*, and there is nothing in the Russian to tell you which. That is the single most reliable source of ✗ *I had been knowing her* — the form is a faithful translation of a Russian sentence that does not distinguish states from activities.

### The four interference points

**1. Russian has one past where English has four.** «Я ждал» is *I waited*, *I was waiting*, *I have been waiting*, and *I had been waiting*, depending on where the anchor is. A learner who has only ever needed one form does not feel the question English is asking, and defaults to the past simple. *I waited an hour and she called* is not wrong, but it does not say the call interrupted anything.

**2. «уже» is not *already*.** Russian marks duration-up-to-a-point with «уже» plus an imperfective verb — «Я работаю здесь уже пять лет». English marks it with the tense and does not need *already* at all: *I've been working here for five years*, never ✗ *I am already working here five years*. Translating «уже» word for word produces a sentence that is grammatical, wrong, and very recognizable.

**3. Imperfective aspect invites a continuous that English forbids.** Russian state verbs — «знал», «хотел», «принадлежал», «содержал» — are imperfective, which feels continuous, which produces ✗ *had been knowing*, ✗ *had been wanting*, ✗ *had been belonging*, ✗ *had been containing*. The Russian aspect is telling you about the shape of the situation; it is not telling you which English form to use. Check the verb against the state list, not against the Russian.

**4. Russian does not order past events with a verb form.** Sequence comes from «уже», «до того как», «перед тем как», and word order. English can do that too — *before*, *by then*, *earlier* — but its default tool is the tense, and in edited writing the tense is expected. A paragraph of Russian-style past simples reads as flat rather than as wrong, and that is exactly why it is hard to self-correct.

### The test

Three questions, in this order. Answer them about the sentence you are trying to build.

1. **Is there a later past moment to measure up to?** If not, you do not want this tense — you want the past simple (finished past period) or the present perfect continuous (the anchor is now).
2. **Am I saying how long, or how many?** How long → continuous. How many → simple. A number of things done is always the simple.
3. **Is the verb a state verb?** If yes, the continuous does not exist; use the past perfect simple, however imperfective the Russian felt.

If all three come out "yes, how long, not a state," use *had been* + `-ing` and stop worrying.

## Quick reference

**The form.** *had* + *been* + `-ing`, for every person. Negative *hadn't been*. Question *Had you been…?* Short answer *Yes, I had.*

**The one idea.** An activity running **up to a past moment**. No past moment, no past perfect continuous.

**Choosing, in three questions.**

```
   Is there a past moment to measure the activity up to?

   NO ──── finished past period? ........................  PAST SIMPLE
       │                                                   I worked there in 2019.
       └── the anchor is now? ...........................  PRESENT PERFECT CONTINUOUS
                                                           I've been working here for a year.

   YES ─── a state verb? ...............................  PAST PERFECT SIMPLE
       │                                                   I had known her for years.
       ├── a quantity, or a completion? .................  PAST PERFECT SIMPLE
       │                                                   I had read three chapters.
       ├── in progress at the moment, no duration? ......  PAST CONTINUOUS
       │                                                   I was cooking when it happened.
       └── a duration, or visible traces? ...............  PAST PERFECT CONTINUOUS
                                                           I had been cooking for two hours.
```

**The four sentences to remember.**

> **I had been waiting for an hour when she finally called.** — duration up to a past moment

> **The ground was wet — it had been raining.** — the activity explains what was there

> **I'd been reading the report** vs. **I'd read the report** — activity vs. completion

> **I had known her for years** — a state, so no continuous exists

**Five rules that cover most of the errors.**

1. **The anchor clause stays in the past simple.** *…when she called*, never *…when she had called*.
2. **A quantity forces the simple.** *How long* → continuous. *How many* → simple.
3. **State verbs have no continuous.** Not in this tense, not in any.
4. ***For* is a length, *since* is a starting point**, and a *since*-clause takes the past simple.
5. **No past anchor, no past perfect.** A finished past period takes the past simple.
