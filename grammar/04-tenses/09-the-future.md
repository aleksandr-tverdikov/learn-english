# The future

> English has no future tense — it has about ten constructions that all point at the same stretch of time and disagree about how settled it is.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

English marks the past on the verb. *Work* becomes *worked*, *go* becomes *went*, and the ending or the vowel change carries the time. For the future it marks nothing at all. There is no future ending, no future inflection, no form of the verb that means "later" — and grammarians have been saying for a century that **English has no future tense**, which sounds like a technicality until you see what stands in its place.

What stands in its place is a toolkit: a modal (*will*), an idiom built on a verb of motion (*be going to*), two present tenses pressed into future service, three compound forms built out of aspect (*will be doing*, *will have done*, *will have been doing*), and a handful of semi-fixed phrases (*be about to*, *be due to*, *be to*). About ten constructions, and none of them is the default.

That is the shape of the problem. Because there is no neutral future form, **every future sentence forces a choice, and the choice carries meaning**. The ten are not ten ways of saying the same thing at different levels of politeness. They differ on questions the time itself never asks:

- Was this decided before you opened your mouth, or as you opened it?
- Is it arranged with somebody, or still only in your head?
- Is the prediction based on something visible right now, or on your opinion?
- How far off is it — seconds, or years?

Answer those and the form follows almost automatically. Answer them wrong and the sentence is grammatical and still says something you did not mean.

## The ten, in one table

| Construction | What it says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [*will*](#will) | decided now; predicted; promised; simply a fact | *I**'ll** get it.* |
| [*be going to*](#be-going-to) | already decided; or predicted from what you can see | *We**'re going to** repaint the kitchen.* |
| [present continuous](#present-continuous-for-the-future) | arranged, with a time and usually another person | *I**'m meeting** Dan at six.* |
| [present simple](#present-simple-for-the-future) | on a timetable somebody else published | *The train **leaves** at 6:40.* |
| [future continuous](#future-continuous) | in progress at a stated future moment | *This time tomorrow I**'ll be flying** to Dallas.* |
| [future perfect](#future-perfect) | finished before a stated future point | *By Friday I**'ll have finished** it.* |
| [future perfect continuous](#future-perfect-continuous) | duration running up to a future point | *In June she**'ll have been teaching** for ten years.* |
| [*be about to*](#be-about-to) | seconds away | *The movie**'s about to** start.* |
| [*be due to*](#be-due-to) | scheduled by an authority, expected | *The train **is due to** arrive at 6:40.* |
| [*be to*](#be-to) | official arrangement, instruction, or headline | *The President **is to** visit Ottawa.* |

Two more sit just outside the list and are covered below: [*be on the point of*](#be-on-the-point-of) and [*be set to*](#be-set-to). And the whole system has a mirror image — [the future in the past](#the-future-in-the-past), for plans that were still ahead when seen from a past vantage point.

## *will*

### The form

*Will* is a [modal](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md#will): one form for every person, no *-s*, no *to*, and a bare infinitive after it.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | subject + *will* + base | *She **will call** you.* |
| Contracted | subject + *'ll* + base | *She**'ll call** you.* |
| Negative | *will not* → *won't* | *She **won't call**.* |
| Question | *Will* + subject + base | ***Will** she **call**?* |
| Negative question | *Won't* + subject + base | ***Won't** she **call**?* |
| *Wh*- question | wh- + *will* + subject + base | *When **will** she **call**?* |
| Short answer | *Yes, she **will**. / No, she **won't**.* | never ✗ *Yes, she'll.* |
| Tag | *…, **won't** she? / …, **will** she?* | *She'll call, **won't** she?* |
| Passive | *will be* + past participle | *The check **will be mailed** Friday.* |

Two form notes worth memorizing. ***Won't*** is not pronounced like *will* plus *not*; it is /woʊnt/, rhyming with *don't*, and that irregularity is the reason it is a single memorized word rather than a predictable contraction. And a **short answer never contracts**: *Yes, I will*, not ✗ *Yes, I'll*. A contraction cannot end a clause, which is the same rule that blocks ✗ *Yes, I'm*.

### The *'ll* almost nobody hears

Nobody says *I will call you*. In ordinary American speech the full form is emphatic — *I **will** call you, I promise* — and the default is *'ll*, which attaches to far more than the textbook pronoun list:

> **I'll**, **you'll**, **he'll**, **she'll**, **it'll**, **we'll**, **they'll**  
> **there'll** be a line, **that'll** do, **who'll** drive, **what'll** happen

In speech it also attaches to full nouns, where it is never written: *The car'll start*, *Dana'll know*, *The store'll be closed*. And it reduces almost to nothing. *We'll move it* is one small syllable away from *we move it*; *it'll* comes out as /ˈɪɾəl/, and *that'll* as /ˈðæɾəl/. **This is why the future disappears from what a learner hears** — the whole tense is riding on a consonant that lasts a few milliseconds.

The full inventory of these forms, with pronunciations and the weak forms, is in the [contraction dictionary](../02-contractions/catalog/README.md); the *will* forms specifically are [here](../02-contractions/catalog/03-will.md).

### Instant decisions

The core use, and the one that is hardest to replace with anything else. **The decision is made at the moment of speaking**, in response to something that has just happened.

> The phone's ringing. — **I'll get it.**  
> I think **I'll have** the salmon.  
> Fine, **I'll do** it myself.

The test is a clock test: **if the decision already existed five seconds ago, this is the wrong form.** A waiter takes your order with *I'll have the salmon* because you decided while reading the menu; you tell a friend *I'm going to try the salmon* if you decided yesterday that this was the place to get it.

### Offers

An offer is an instant decision made on somebody else's behalf, so it takes the same form.

> That looks heavy — **I'll carry** it.  
> Don't get up. **I'll get** the door.

A first-person question offer can take *Shall I…?*, which is the one surviving non-legal use of *shall* in American English: *Shall I get the door?* Most Americans say *Should I get the door?* or *Want me to get the door?* instead. See [*shall*](#shall-and-what-americans-actually-do-with-it) below.

### Promises

> **I'll pay** you back on Friday.  
> **I'll be** there at six, I promise.  
> **I won't tell** anyone.

*Will* is what makes a sentence a promise rather than a report of a plan. *I'm going to pay you back* describes an intention you happen to have; *I'll pay you back* commits you.

### Requests

> **Will you hold** this for a second?  
> **Will you stop** doing that?

*Would you* is politer and far more common with strangers; plain *will you* ranges from casual to sharp depending on the tone, and *Will you stop that* is not a question at all.

### Refusals, and the stubborn *won't*

This is the use nobody teaches and everybody needs, because it is **not about the future at all**. *Won't* is the negative of the oldest sense of *will*, "be willing," so it reports a refusal happening **right now** — and English extends that refusal to objects as readily as to people.

> She **won't** talk to me.  
> The car **won't** start.  
> This window **won't** open.

*The car won't start* is a statement about this morning, in the driveway, with the key in the ignition. It is present time in a future-looking form, and there is no other way to say it: ✗ *The car doesn't start* is a statement about the car's general character, and ✗ *The car isn't going to start* is a prediction about a future attempt.

The past of this *won't* is ***wouldn't***: *The car wouldn't start yesterday.*

### Predictions based on opinion

> **I think it'll rain** later.  
> Prices **will probably go** up again in the spring.

This *will* almost always travels with a marker of opinion — *I think*, *I bet*, *I'm sure*, *I expect*, *I suppose*, *I doubt*, *probably*, *definitely*, *perhaps*. The prediction rests on what the speaker believes, not on what is visible, and that is exactly what separates it from [*be going to*](#will-or-be-going-to).

One idiom to note: English puts the negative on the opinion verb, not on the prediction. **I don't think it'll rain** is what people say; *I think it won't rain* is grammatical and sounds like a deliberate contradiction of somebody.

### Plain facts about the future

Some futures involve no decision, no plan, and no opinion. They are simply what is going to be the case, and *will* is the neutral form for them.

> She**'ll be** forty in June.  
> The sun **will set** at 8:14 tonight.

Nothing here is arguable, and nothing is arranged. This is the closest English gets to a neutral future tense, and it is the reason *will* feels like the default even though it is not.

### Threats, warnings, and conditional results

> If you touch that, **you'll regret** it.  
> Say that again and **I'll walk** out.

This is the result clause of a [first conditional](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#first-conditional), where *will* is the standard form.

### The *will* that is not future at all

A footnote, but a common one. *Will* also expresses a **confident deduction about the present**, in the same way *must* does:

> That**'ll be** the plumber. — the doorbell just rang  
> He**'ll be** asleep by now. — it's midnight

Nothing in those sentences is about the future. The same trick with the continuous and the perfect is covered under the [future continuous](#a-guess-about-right-now) and the [future perfect](#a-guess-about-the-recent-past).

### *Shall*, and what Americans actually do with it

British-published textbooks still teach *I shall / we shall* as the first-person future. **That is not American English.** In the United States *shall* survives in exactly two places:

| Surviving use | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Legal and regulatory prose | *The tenant **shall** provide written notice.* | Means "is required to," not "will." |
| First-person offers and suggestions | ***Shall** I get the door?* &middot; ***Shall** we?* | Slightly formal; *Should I…?* is commoner. |

Everything else is *will*. ✗ *I shall call you tomorrow* is not wrong English, but it is not American English either — it reads as British, archaic, or arch, and in ordinary American writing it will be changed. The same goes for ✗ *We shall see how it goes* outside of a deliberate flourish.

## *be going to*

### The form

*Be going to* is not a modal. It is the verb *be*, which carries all the tense and agreement, plus the fixed string *going to* plus a bare infinitive.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *am / is / are* + *going to* + base | *She **is going to** call.* |
| Contracted | *'m / 's / 're going to* | *She**'s going to** call.* |
| Negative | *'m not / isn't / aren't going to* | *She **isn't going to** call.* |
| Question | *Am / Is / Are* + subject + *going to* | ***Is** she **going to** call?* |
| Short answer | *Yes, she **is**. / No, she**'s not**.* | The answer keeps only *be*. |
| Tag | *…, **isn't** she? / …, **is** she?* | *She's going to call, **isn't** she?* |
| Passive | *be going to be* + past participle | *The road **is going to be** repaved.* |
| Past (future in the past) | *was / were going to* | *She **was going to** call.* |

In speech *going to* collapses to ***gonna*** /ˈɡʌnə/ before a verb, and that reduction is close to universal in American conversation at every level of education. It is **not written** outside dialogue and deliberately casual prose. One limit worth knowing: the reduction only happens before a verb. *I'm gonna call her* is ordinary; ✗ *I'm gonna the store* is impossible, because that *going to* is a real verb of motion plus a real preposition. See [gonna](../02-contractions/catalog/05-informal-spoken.md#gonna).

### Intentions and plans already made

> **I'm going to look** for a new job.  
> We**'re going to repaint** the kitchen this summer.  
> **I'm not going to argue** about this again.

The decision is **already in place** before the sentence starts. It need not be arranged, booked, or scheduled — it is an intention, and *be going to* is the form that reports one. If somebody asks *Why did you buy all that paint?*, the answer is *I'm going to repaint the hall*, never ✗ *I'll repaint the hall*, because the decision is visibly older than the question.

### Predictions based on present evidence

> Look at those clouds — **it's going to rain.**  
> Watch out, **you're going to spill** that.  
> He**'s going to fall** if he keeps leaning back like that.

Something **visible right now** points at the outcome. The clouds are in the sky; the glass is at the edge of the table. The speaker is not offering an opinion, and could point at the evidence if challenged. That is the difference from predictive *will*, and it is the whole difference:

> **I think it'll rain** tomorrow. — my sense of the weather  
> **It's going to rain.** — I am looking at the sky right now

### The imminent and the inevitable

> She**'s going to have** a baby in May.  
> **We're going to be** late.

*We're going to be late* is the standard sentence for a car stuck in traffic — the evidence is the dashboard clock — while *We'll be late* is a prediction or an announcement.

### *was going to*

The past form has a life of its own, mostly as a report of a plan that did not come off: *I was going to call you, but I fell asleep.* It is treated in full under [the future in the past](#was--were-going-to-the-plan-that-did-not-happen).

## *will* or *be going to*

This is the choice that costs learners the most, and it is decidable. Both point at the same future moment. What differs is **where the future came from**: *will* takes it from the present moment of speaking, and *be going to* takes it from something that already exists — a decision already made, or evidence already visible.

### The decision table

| The situation | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Decided as you speak | *will* | *The phone's ringing — **I'll** get it.* |
| Decided earlier | *be going to* | ***I'm going to** call her tonight.* |
| An offer | *will* | ***I'll** carry that.* |
| A promise | *will* | ***I'll** pay you back Friday.* |
| A refusal, now | *won't* | *The car **won't** start.* |
| A request | *will* | ***Will** you hold this?* |
| Prediction from opinion | *will* | *I think they**'ll** win.* |
| Prediction from visible evidence | *be going to* | *It's 40–3 with a minute left. They**'re going to** win.* |
| A neutral fact, nobody's doing | *will* | *She**'ll** be forty in June.* |
| A warning about what you can see | *be going to* | *Careful — you**'re going to** burn yourself.* |
| The result of a first conditional | *will* (usually) | *If it rains, we**'ll** cancel.* |
| Formal or edited prose | *will* | *The committee **will** reconvene in April.* |

### The pairs

Put side by side, the same content splits cleanly:

> A: *Are you free at six?* B: *No, **I'm going to** get my hair cut.* — booked earlier  
> A: *This is really heavy.* B: ***I'll** help you.* — decided this instant

> *I think the Rockies **will** take the division.* — my opinion  
> *They're up nine games with twelve to play. They**'re going to** take the division.* — the standings

> *Don't worry about the dishes — **I'll** do them.* — an offer  
> *I**'m going to** do the dishes and then go to bed.* — a plan I already had

> *Give me your address and **I'll** send it.* — a commitment made now  
> *I**'m going to** send it tomorrow anyway.* — already on my list

### Where either one works

For a neutral prediction with no visible evidence and no plan behind it, both are fine and mean the same thing: *I think he'll win* and *I think he's going to win* are interchangeable. Be aware that **American speech uses *be going to* considerably more than textbooks suggest**, including in places where a British-trained teacher would insist on *will*. *It's going to be fine*, *You're going to love it*, *That's going to take a while* are all completely ordinary, and none of them rests on evidence in any strict sense.

### Where only one is possible

- **Offers, promises, requests, and refusals take *will*.** There is no *going to* version of *I'll get it* as an offer.
- **A refusal by an object takes *won't*.** *The car won't start* and *The car isn't going to start* are different sentences: the second is a prediction about a future attempt.
- **Neither one goes in an *if*-clause or a time clause.** *If it rains*, *when I get home* — see [below](#after-time-conjunctions-where-the-future-is-forbidden). The exception is *be going to* reporting a plan: *If you're going to be late, call me.*
- **Edited and formal prose prefers *will*.** *Be going to* is not incorrect in writing, but it is conversational, and in a report or a contract it will usually be *will*.

## Present continuous for the future

### The form

The ordinary present continuous — *am / is / are* + *-ing* — with a future time expression attached. Nothing about the form changes; only the reading does.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *am / is / are* + *-ing* | *I**'m meeting** Dan at six.* |
| Negative | *'m not / isn't / aren't* + *-ing* | *I**'m not working** Saturday.* |
| Question | *Am / Is / Are* + subject + *-ing* | ***Are** you **coming** Friday?* |
| Short answer | *Yes, I **am**. / No, I**'m not**.* | |

### Fixed arrangements

> **I'm meeting** Dan at six.  
> We**'re having** dinner with the Kellers on Friday.  
> She**'s flying** to Denver on Tuesday.  
> **I'm not working** next Saturday — I already asked for it off.

The defining feature is that **the arrangement exists outside your head**. There is a time, usually a place, and almost always another person who also knows about it: a booking, an appointment, a ticket, a date somebody else is expecting you to keep. That is why the present continuous is the natural future for a diary and the wrong future for a resolution.

### It needs a time expression

Because the form is identical to the ordinary present continuous, the future reading depends entirely on context — and in practice that means a time expression.

> **I'm working.** — right now  
> **I'm working on Saturday.** — the future

Without *on Saturday*, *tonight*, *next week*, *at six*, or a question that has already established the time frame, a listener will take the sentence as present. This is the only one of the ten constructions that is ambiguous on its own.

### Present continuous or *be going to*?

Both report something already decided. The difference is how far the decision has been carried.

| | *be going to* | present continuous |
|---|---|---|
| What it reports | an intention | an arrangement |
| Is it fixed? | decided, not necessarily scheduled | scheduled, and someone else is involved |
| Needs a time? | no | effectively yes |
| Natural with | *sometime*, *soon*, *one of these days* | *at six*, *on Friday*, *next Tuesday* |

> **I'm going to** get my hair cut. — I've decided; no appointment yet  
> **I'm getting** my hair cut at four. — the appointment is booked

> **I'm going to** talk to her about it. — I intend to  
> **I'm talking** to her at nine tomorrow. — it's on the calendar

In most sentences either would be understood, and Americans slide between them freely. The one place the difference bites is when somebody asks whether you are free: *I'm going to study tonight* is a plan you could be talked out of, and *I'm meeting Dan at six* is not.

### Where the present continuous cannot go

**State verbs refuse it**, exactly as they refuse the continuous everywhere: ✗ *I'm knowing him next week*, ✗ *She's being forty in June*. Use *will* or *be going to*.

**Events nobody arranged refuse it**, because there is no arrangement to report: ✗ *It's raining tomorrow* is wrong for a weather forecast. Say *It's going to rain tomorrow*. The exception is anything with a schedule behind it — *The sun is rising at 6:42 tomorrow* is odd but *We're landing at 6:42* is fine, because a crew arranged it.

## Present simple for the future

### Timetables and schedules

The present simple takes a future meaning only when the event sits on a **schedule somebody else published** — a transit timetable, a program, a calendar, a season.

> The train **leaves** at 6:40 and **gets** in at 9:15.  
> The movie **starts** at 7:15.  
> The semester **ends** on May 9.  
> Christmas **falls** on a Thursday this year.  
> **What time does** the game **start**?

The verbs that appear here are a short list — *leave*, *arrive*, *land*, *depart*, *start*, *begin*, *open*, *close*, *finish*, *end*, *run*, *fall*, *be*. They are verbs of scheduling, and the pattern does not generalize beyond them.

**It does not cover personal plans.** ✗ *I go to the dentist tomorrow* is wrong; say *I'm going to the dentist tomorrow*. The one exception is itinerary-speak, where a person's day is being read off a printed schedule:

> We **leave** Denver at eight, **arrive** in Chicago at noon, and **have** three hours before the connection.

### After time conjunctions: where the future is forbidden

**English marks the future once per sentence, in the main clause.** A subordinate time clause is already located in the future by the main clause, so it takes a present form — and putting *will* there is one of the two or three most recognizable learner errors in the language.

> I'll call you **when I get** home. — ✗ *when I will get*  
> We'll start **as soon as she arrives**. — ✗ *as soon as she will arrive*  
> Wait here **until I come** back. — ✗ *until I will come*  
> **Before you leave**, lock the back door.  
> **By the time you read** this, I'll be gone.  
> **Once the check clears**, we'll ship the order.

The conjunctions that trigger this: ***when*, *while*, *before*, *after*, *as soon as*, *until*, *till*, *once*, *by the time*, *the moment*, *the minute*, *every time*, *whenever*, *as long as*, *now that***. Each has its own entry in the [dictionary of time subordinators](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md).

The same rule governs conditional clauses, where it produces ✗ *If it will rain* — the flagship error of the whole topic, worked out at length in [The four types](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#why--if-it-will-rain-is-wrong).

> If it **rains**, we'll cancel. — ✗ *If it will rain*  
> Unless you **hear** from me, assume it's on.

**The present perfect is available in the same slot**, and it insists that the event is finished before the main clause starts:

> I'll call you **when I've finished**.  
> **As soon as you've eaten**, we'll go.

### The *when* that does take *will*

Not every *when* is a time clause. When *when* is a **question word** — introducing a direct question or a noun clause — it takes whatever tense the meaning needs, *will* included.

> **When will** she arrive?  
> I don't know **when she'll** arrive.  
> Tell me **when you'll** be ready. — tell me the time  
> Tell me **when you're** ready. — tell me at that moment

That last pair is the clean test. The noun clause is the *object* of *tell*; the time clause says *when* to do the telling. The same split applies to *if* meaning *whether*: *I don't know **if it'll** rain* is a noun clause and perfectly correct, while *If it **rains**, we'll cancel* is a condition.

Two more legitimate *will*s in a subordinate clause, both covered in the conditionals topic: **willingness** (*If you'll wait here, someone will be with you*) and **insistence** (*If you will smoke, expect a cough*). In both, *will* means something other than "later."

## Future continuous

### The form

*Will be* + *-ing*. Only *will* carries the negative and the question; *be* + *-ing* rides along unchanged.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *will be* + *-ing* | *I**'ll be driving** at nine.* |
| Negative | *won't be* + *-ing* | *I **won't be driving**.* |
| Question | *Will* + subject + *be* + *-ing* | ***Will** you **be driving**?* |
| Short answer | *Yes, I **will**. / No, I **won't**.* | |

### In progress at a future moment

The basic meaning: an action will already be under way when a stated future moment arrives.

> **This time tomorrow** I**'ll be flying** to Dallas.  
> **At eight tonight** we**'ll be eating** dinner.  
> **When you land**, I**'ll be waiting** at baggage claim.

It needs a future moment to be in progress *at*: *this time tomorrow*, *at eight*, *when you arrive*, *all morning*. Without one the form has nothing to anchor to.

### Two things happening at once

> **While you're checking in**, I**'ll be getting** the rental car.  
> The kids **will be sleeping** **when we get back**.

### Something happening anyway, as a matter of course

This is the use that has no equivalent in the simple form, and it is common in American speech. The future continuous says the event is **already part of the shape of things** — not decided, not promised, just happening.

> I**'ll be seeing** Dana tomorrow — I'll ask her then.  
> We**'ll be passing** your exit anyway, so we can drop it off.

Compare *I'll see Dana tomorrow*, which sounds like a decision or a promise, with *I'll be seeing Dana tomorrow*, which reports a routine. The continuous deliberately drains the intention out of the sentence.

### The polite enquiry about someone's plans

That drained-out quality is exactly what makes the future continuous the standard way to ask what somebody intends without appearing to ask them to change it.

> **Will you be using** the car tonight? — neutral: I'm asking about your plans  
> **Will you use** the car tonight? — sounds like a request, or a demand for a decision

> **Will you be staying** for dinner?  
> **How long will you be staying**?

A waiter, a hotel clerk, or a colleague will use this form nearly every time. The plain *will* version puts you on the spot; the continuous version treats your plans as facts already in existence that the speaker is merely inquiring about.

### A guess about right now

Like plain *will*, the future continuous also does duty as a confident deduction about the present:

> She**'ll be wondering** where we are.  
> They**'ll be landing** about now.

## Future perfect

### The form

*Will have* + past participle.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *will have* + participle | *I**'ll have finished** by Friday.* |
| Negative | *won't have* + participle | *I **won't have finished** by Friday.* |
| Question | *Will* + subject + *have* + participle | ***Will** you **have finished** by Friday?* |
| Short answer | *Yes, I **will**. / No, I **won't**.* | |
| Passive | *will have been* + participle | *The road **will have been repaved** by then.* |

In speech *will have* reduces to *will've* /ˈwɪləv/ and *won't have* to /ˈwoʊntəv/. As with *would've*, the reduction produces the written error ✗ *will of*, which is not a form of English at all — *of* is never a verb.

### Finished before a future point

The future perfect needs **a stated future point**, and it says the event will be over before that point arrives. The point is almost always supplied by *by*, *by then*, *by the time*, *before*, or a span like *in three weeks*.

> **By Friday** I**'ll have finished** the report.  
> **By the time you get here**, we**'ll have eaten**.  
> She**'ll have graduated** **before the baby comes**.  
> **By then** the store **won't have opened** yet.

Look at *By the time you get here, we'll have eaten*. Both rules are operating at once: the time clause takes the present (*you get*, never ✗ *you will get*), and the main clause takes the future perfect.

### *by* and *until* are not interchangeable

***By Friday*** means at some point not later than Friday. ***Until Friday*** means continuously up to Friday. The future perfect goes with *by*:

> ✓ I**'ll have finished** it **by** Friday.  
> ✗ *I'll have finished it **until** Friday.*  
> ✓ I**'ll be working** on it **until** Friday.

### A guess about the recent past

> They**'ll have landed** by now.  
> He**'ll have forgotten** all about it.

Again not future: a confident inference about something already finished.

### How often it is really used

Honestly: less than textbooks imply. In conversation Americans reach for *I'll be done by Friday*, *We'll already be finished*, or simply *I'll finish it by Friday*, all of which are standard. The future perfect is a written and careful form, and it earns its keep when the **order of two future events** genuinely needs marking — which is why it is at home in project plans, contracts, and schedules, and rare over coffee.

## Future perfect continuous

### The form

*Will have been* + *-ing*. The rarest construction in the tense system, and mechanically the simplest, since only *will* is ever touched.

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *will have been* + *-ing* | *I**'ll have been working** here ten years.* |
| Negative | *won't have been* + *-ing* | *I **won't have been working** here long enough.* |
| Question | *Will* + subject + *have been* + *-ing* | ***Will** you **have been working** there a year by then?* |

### Duration up to a future point

It needs **two** things: a future point, and a length of time running up to it.

> **By June** I**'ll have been teaching** **for ten years**.  
> **When she retires**, she**'ll have been working** here **for thirty years**.

Drop the duration and the sentence collapses: ✗ *By June I'll have been teaching* says nothing.

### Simple or continuous?

**A stated quantity forces the simple.** The continuous measures time; it cannot measure output.

> ✓ By Friday I**'ll have written** three chapters.  
> ✗ *By Friday I'll have been writing three chapters.*  
> ✓ By Friday I**'ll have been writing** for six straight days.

**State verbs refuse the continuous**, here as everywhere:

> ✓ In March I**'ll have known** her for twenty years.  
> ✗ *In March I'll have been knowing her for twenty years.*

## The near-future and scheduled constructions

Five more constructions, each narrower than *will* and each carrying information *will* cannot.

### *be about to*

**Seconds or minutes away.** *Be about to* + base form takes no time expression, because immediacy is the whole meaning.

> The movie**'s about to** start.  
> I was just **about to** call you.

*Just* intensifies it further: *I'm just about to walk out the door.*

The negative has a second, unrelated meaning that is worth knowing, because it is not about time at all. ***I'm not about to*** means "I refuse":

> **I'm not about to** apologize for something I didn't do.

### *be on the point of*

More formal and even more immediate, and it takes an *-ing* form or a noun rather than an infinitive.

> She was **on the point of** giving up when the offer came.  
> He's **on the point of** resigning.

Mostly written. In speech, *about to* does this job.

### *be due to*

**Scheduled, and expected by other people.** *Be due to* + base form reports an arrangement made by an authority — a timetable, a contract, a doctor.

> The train **is due to** arrive at 6:40.  
> She**'s due** back Monday.  
> Your payment **was due** on the fifteenth.

Note the bare *be due* + time phrase, without *to*, in the last three. Both patterns are ordinary. *Be due to* also carries a faint sense that the schedule may slip — a train *due to arrive at 6:40* is one you are still waiting for.

### *be to*

*Be to* + base form is the **official** future: arrangements handed down, instructions, and news. It is formal, and it is everywhere in journalism and in institutional writing.

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Official arrangement | *The President **is to** visit Ottawa next month.* |
| Instruction or order | *You **are to** report to the front desk at eight.* |
| Prohibition | *No one **is to** leave the building.* |
| A plan, in narrative | *They **were to** be married in June.* |
| Purpose in an *if*-clause | *If we **are to** finish on time, we need two more people.* |

The newspaper headline version deletes *be* altogether, which is why headlines look like this:

> **President to Visit Ottawa**  
> **Fed to Hold Rates Steady**

*Be to* is not a conversational form. ✗ *I am to meet Dan at six* for an ordinary appointment sounds like a summons.

### *be set to*

Journalistic, and about likelihood rather than schedule — roughly "poised to."

> The bill **is set to** pass the Senate this week.  
> Prices **are set to** rise again in the fall.

### The near-future forms compared

| Form | How far off | Register | Who decided it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *be about to* | seconds, minutes | neutral | events themselves | *It's about to rain.* |
| *be on the point of* | seconds | formal, written | events themselves | *He was on the point of giving up.* |
| *be due to* | any stated time | neutral | a schedule or authority | *The train is due to arrive at 6:40.* |
| *be to* | any stated time | formal, official | an authority | *The board is to meet on Tuesday.* |
| *be set to* | soon, unspecified | journalistic | circumstances | *Prices are set to rise.* |

Three near relatives round it out: ***be likely to*** (probability — *Rates are likely to fall*), ***be bound to*** (inevitability — *She's bound to find out*), and ***be sure to*** (confidence — *He's sure to ask about it*).

## The future in the past

Everything above assumes you are standing in the present looking forward. Move the viewpoint into the past — in a story, in a memory, in a report — and the future has to be rebuilt from past-tense parts. **Every future construction has a past-viewpoint twin**, and they line up one for one.

| Future from now | Future from a past viewpoint | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | *would* | *He knew she **would** say no.* |
| *be going to* | *was / were going to* | *I **was going to** call you.* |
| present continuous | past continuous | *We **were having** dinner with them on Friday.* |
| present simple | past simple | *The train **left** at 6:40 that day.* |
| *be about to* | *was / were about to* | *I **was about to** leave.* |
| *be to* | *was / were to* | *They **were to** be married in June.* |
| *be due to* | *was / were due to* | *The train **was due to** arrive at six.* |
| future perfect | *would have* + participle | *By June he **would have** finished.* |
| future continuous | *would be* + *-ing* | *She **would be waiting** at the gate.* |

### *was / were going to*: the plan that did not happen

The commonest of the group, and it carries a strong implication that the plan fell through — so strong that *but* is almost always waiting at the end of the sentence.

> **I was going to** call you, but I fell asleep.  
> We **were going to** buy it, but it sold before we decided.  
> **I was going to** say something, but it didn't seem worth it.

Without a *but*, the implication is still there. *He said he was going to fix it* invites the follow-up *and he never did*, which is exactly why a reporter who wants to stay neutral writes *He said he would fix it* instead. That difference is treated in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md#be-going-to--waswere-going-to).

### *would*: the narrative future

In storytelling and biography, *would* looks forward from a point in the past — and this *would* has nothing to do with conditionals.

> He left Ohio in 1931. He **would** never return.  
> She didn't know it yet, but she **would** marry him within the year.

The reader is standing in 1931 and looking forward. Note that this *would* is fully compatible with the event having actually happened — indeed it usually did happen, which is what separates it from the unreal *would* of the [second conditional](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md#second-conditional).

### *was / were about to*

The immediate future, seen from the past. It is the standard setup for an interruption.

> **I was about to** leave when the phone rang.

### *was / were to*: arrangement and destiny

Two readings, and the context separates them cleanly.

> They **were to** be married in June. — the arrangement  
> She **was to** become the first woman to run the company. — destiny, told from the past

The second is a narrative device: the writer knows how it turned out and is telling you in advance. The first is a plain report of a plan.

***Was to have*** + participle marks an arrangement that **did not** come off, and it is the most precise tool in the group:

> The meeting **was to have been** held on Monday. — it wasn't

### The reported-speech connection

Every one of these appears automatically in reported speech, because a past reporting verb moves the viewpoint back:

> "I**'ll** call you." → He said he **would** call me.  
> "I**'m going to** quit." → She said she **was going to** quit.  
> "I**'ll have finished** by then." → He said he **would have finished** by then.

The full table is in [Backshift](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md#the-master-table).

## The master table

Every construction, what it claims, what it travels with, and one sentence.

| Form | What it means | Typical time expressions | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *will* + base | decided now; predicted from opinion; promised; a plain fact | *tomorrow*, *next week*, *in a minute*, *probably*, *I think* | *I**'ll** get it.* |
| *won't* + base | a refusal, often about right now | *(none — present time)* | *The car **won't** start.* |
| *be going to* + base | already decided; predicted from present evidence | *tonight*, *this summer*, *soon*, *look —* | *It**'s going to** rain.* |
| present continuous | a fixed arrangement, usually with someone else | *at six*, *on Friday*, *next Tuesday* | *I**'m meeting** Dan at six.* |
| present simple | a published timetable or schedule | *at 6:40*, *on May 9*, *this year* | *The train **leaves** at 6:40.* |
| present simple in a time or *if*-clause | the future, where *will* is forbidden | *when*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before*, *if* | *I'll call you **when I get** home.* |
| *will be* + *-ing* | in progress at a future moment; a plan as a matter of course; a polite enquiry | *this time tomorrow*, *at eight*, *when you arrive* | *I**'ll be driving** at nine.* |
| *will have* + participle | finished before a future point | *by Friday*, *by then*, *by the time*, *before*, *in ten years* | *I**'ll have finished** by Friday.* |
| *will have been* + *-ing* | duration running up to a future point | *by June … for ten years*, *when she retires … since 2016* | *I**'ll have been teaching** for ten years.* |
| *be about to* + base | seconds away | *(none — immediacy is the meaning)*, *just* | *It**'s about to** start.* |
| *be due to* + base | scheduled, expected by others | *at 6:40*, *on Monday*, *in April* | *The train **is due to** arrive at 6:40.* |
| *be to* + base | official arrangement, instruction, headline | *next month*, *on Tuesday*, *at eight* | *The President **is to** visit Ottawa.* |
| *was going to* + base | a past plan, usually unrealized | *…, but* | *I **was going to** call you, but…* |
| *would* + base | the future seen from a past point in a narrative | *later*, *within the year*, *never again* | *He **would** never return.* |
| *was to have* + participle | an arrangement that did not come off | *on Monday*, *in January* | *The meeting **was to have been** held Monday.* |

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **will go** to the cinema tonight.* (the tickets are booked) | *I**'m going** to the cinema tonight.* | *Will* announces a decision made now. A booked arrangement takes the present continuous. |
| ✗ *When I **will arrive**, I'll call you.* | *When I **arrive**, I'll call you.* | The future is marked once, in the main clause. Time clauses take a present form. |
| ✗ *If it **will rain**, we'll cancel.* | *If it **rains**, we'll cancel.* | Same rule, in a conditional clause. |
| ✗ *I**'m going to** have the salmon.* (deciding at the table) | *I**'ll** have the salmon.* | An instant decision takes *will*. *Be going to* reports a decision you already had. |
| ✗ *I **shall** call you tomorrow.* | *I**'ll** call you tomorrow.* | Plain-future *shall* is British and dated. American *shall* survives in legal prose and in *Shall I…?* |
| ✗ *Yes, I**'ll**.* | *Yes, I **will**.* | A contraction cannot end a clause. Short answers use the full form. |
| ✗ *Look at those clouds — it **will** rain.* | *…it**'s going to** rain.* | Visible evidence takes *be going to*. *Will* is for predictions from opinion. |
| ✗ *The car **doesn't start**.* (this morning, right now) | *The car **won't** start.* | *Won't* is a refusal in present time. The present simple states the car's general character. |
| ✗ *It**'s raining** tomorrow.* | *It**'s going to rain** tomorrow.* | The present continuous reports arrangements. Nobody arranges the weather. |
| ✗ *I **go** to the dentist tomorrow.* | *I**'m going** to the dentist tomorrow.* | The present simple future is for published timetables, not personal appointments. |
| ✗ *By the time you **will get** here, we'll have eaten.* | *By the time you **get** here…* | *By the time* is a time conjunction like any other. |
| ✗ *I**'ll have finished** it **until** Friday.* | *I**'ll have finished** it **by** Friday.* | *By* = not later than; *until* = continuously up to. |
| ✗ *By June I**'ll have been teaching**.* | *By June I**'ll have been teaching for ten years**.* | The future perfect continuous needs a duration as well as a point. |
| ✗ *We **will can** finish it.* | *We**'ll be able to** finish it.* | Two modals cannot stack. Use the semi-modal. |
| ✗ *I **will of** finished by then.* | *I**'ll have** finished by then.* | *Of* is never a verb; *will've* is what is being misheard. |
| ✗ *I **was going to have called** you.* | *I **was going to call** you.* | *Was going to* already sits in the past. The perfect infinitive belongs to *was to have called*. |
| ✗ *I am to meet Dan at six.* (an ordinary date) | *I**'m meeting** Dan at six.* | *Be to* is official and formal. Used of a dinner date, it sounds like a summons. |

## For Russian speakers

Russian has **one** future, formed two ways: the perfective present, which simply *is* the future (*я позвоню*, *я куплю*, *я приду*), and *буду* plus an imperfective infinitive (*я буду читать*, *я буду работать*). Those two forms differ in aspect, not in planning, evidence, arrangement, or schedule — the four things English future forms are actually about. **So all ten English constructions collapse into one for a Russian speaker, and nothing in the Russian sentence tells you which one to pick.**

**1. The future after *если* and *когда*. This is the commonest error, by a wide margin.**

Russian requires a future verb in both clauses. English forbids it in one of them:

| Russian | ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|---|
| «Если **будет** дождь, мы **останемся** дома.» | *If it **will rain**, we'll stay home.* | *If it **rains**, we**'ll** stay home.* |
| «Когда я **приеду**, я тебе **позвоню**.» | *When I **will arrive**, I'll call you.* | *When I **arrive**, I**'ll** call you.* |
| «Как только она **придёт**, мы **начнём**.» | *As soon as she **will come**, we'll start.* | *As soon as she **comes**, we**'ll** start.* |
| «Подожди, пока я **не вернусь**.» | *Wait until I **will come** back.* | *Wait **until I come** back.* |

There is a cruel wrinkle here. The Russian perfective present *looks* present — *приду*, *позвоню*, *придёт* — so a learner sometimes produces the correct English present by accident, and then produces *will* in the very next sentence with no felt difference between the two. The rule has to be learned as a rule: **after *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before*, *after*, *once*, *by the time*, no *will*.**

**2. *Буду* + infinitive is not *will be* + *-ing*.**

The most persistent false friend in the topic. Russian *буду делать* is simply the imperfective future; it says nothing about being in progress at a particular moment.

| Russian | ✗ Tempting | ✓ Usual |
|---|---|---|
| «Я **буду работать** завтра.» | *I **will be working** tomorrow.* | *I**'m working** tomorrow.* — an arrangement |
| «Я **буду учить** английский.» | *I **will be learning** English.* | *I**'m going to** learn English.* — an intention |
| «Что ты **будешь делать** вечером?» | *What **will** you **be doing** tonight?* | *What **are** you **doing** tonight?* — asking about plans |

*Will be working* is correct only when an action is in progress **at a stated future moment**: *At nine tomorrow I'll be working.*

**3. The perfective present maps onto *will* — and only for instant decisions.**

Here Russian helps, and it is worth noticing because so little else does:

> «Я **открою** окно.» → ***I'll open** the window.*  
> «Я **сделаю** это сам.» → ***I'll do** it myself.*  
> «Я **возьму** лосося.» → ***I'll have** the salmon.*

The trap is the reverse direction: a plan you already had is not *will* in English, even though Russian uses the same perfective. «Я **позвоню** ей сегодня вечером, я уже решил» is *I'm going to call her tonight*, not ✗ *I'll call her tonight*.

**4. *Я собираюсь* exists, and learners underuse it.**

«Я собираюсь» is a direct equivalent of *be going to*, and Russian speakers who know this still reach for *will* out of habit, because *will* was taught first as "the future tense." It is not the future tense. It is one of ten options, and for an intention it is usually the wrong one.

**5. «Машина не заводится» is *The car won't start*.**

Russian reports a machine's refusal with a present-tense reflexive; English reports it with *won't*. The result is that Russian speakers produce ✗ *The car doesn't start*, ✗ *The door doesn't open*, ✗ *The computer doesn't turn on* — all of which describe a permanent characteristic rather than what is happening in the driveway right now.

**6. «Через два часа» is *in two hours*, not *after two hours*.**

The future time preposition is ***in***, and it is not *after* and not *through*:

> «Я вернусь **через час**.» → *I'll be back **in** an hour.* — ✗ *after an hour*, ✗ *through an hour*

*After two hours* exists, but it means something else: it looks back from a later point at a completed stretch (*After two hours of waiting, we left*).

## Quick reference

**Pick a form in four questions.**

```
   Is it on a published timetable?
       YES → present simple .................... The train leaves at 6:40.
       NO  ↓

   Is it arranged — a time, a place, another person?
       YES → present continuous ................ I'm meeting Dan at six.
       NO  ↓

   Was it decided before you started speaking,
   or can you point at the evidence right now?
       YES → be going to ...................... I'm going to repaint the kitchen.
                                                Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.
       NO  ↓

   Everything else ........................... will
       decided this second, offered, promised,
       refused, predicted from opinion, or
       simply a fact about the future
```

**The four rules that fix most of the errors.**

1. **No *will* after *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *until*, *before*, *after*, *once*, or *by the time*.** The future is marked once, in the main clause.
2. **A decision you already had is *be going to*; a decision you are making now is *will*.** That single distinction accounts for most of the wrong choices.
3. **An arrangement with a time and another person is the present continuous.** Not *will*.
4. **American plain futures use *will*, not *shall*.** *Shall* means "is required to" in a contract and "should I?" in an offer.

**The seven sentences to carry.**

> **I'll get it.** — decided this second  
> **I'm going to repaint the kitchen.** — decided a while ago  
> **Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.** — evidence, right now  
> **I'm meeting Dan at six.** — arranged  
> **The train leaves at 6:40.** — timetabled  
> **I'll call you when I get home.** — no *will* in the time clause  
> **The car won't start.** — a refusal, happening now
