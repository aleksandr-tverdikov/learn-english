The passive, questions, and subordinate clauses#

The three jobs every tense has to do besides state a plain fact: turn around so that the object goes first, bend into a question or a negative, and sit quietly inside a clause that belongs to some other verb.

← Tenses · All grammar topics

The sections before this one took the twelve tense-aspect combinations one at a time and asked what each one means. This section asks what each one does when the sentence needs something other than a plain positive statement. Three operations cut straight across the whole system, and all three are mechanical once you see what they operate on.

The passive rebuilds the verb phrase as be + past participle, and the tense you started with survives inside the form of be. Twelve tenses in, twelve passives out — except that two of them are so unwieldy that English quietly refuses to write them.

Questions and negatives are built on the operator: the first auxiliary in the verb phrase. Every tense has one, or gets one issued to it. That single fact explains Is she working?, Hasn't he finished?, Will they have left?, the tag on the end of You're coming, aren't you?, the short answer Yes, I have — and the reason ✗ Did you went is wrong. Subordinate clauses, finally, do not always get to pick their own tense. A time clause is forbidden the future, a reported clause usually agrees with the verb that reports it, and a handful of frames — it's the first time, this is the best… I've ever, since — hand you a tense before you have chosen one.

Part 1 — The passive in all twelve tenses#

The shape of it#

An active clause names the doer as the subject. A passive clause promotes whatever the action landed on and leaves the doer optional.

The committee approved the plan. → The plan was approved by the committee.

The passive verb phrase is always the same two ingredients: a form of be and the past participle. The past participle never changes — approved, built, written, taken, sent — so the entire tense system is carried by be, which inflects exactly as it does anywhere else.

That is the trick to the whole table below. You are not learning twelve new forms. You are conjugating be twelve ways, which you already know how to do, and hanging one frozen participle off the end.

she writes → it is written · she is writing → it is being written she has written → it has been written · she had written → it had been written she will write → it will be written · she will have written → it will have been written

The two that English does not write#

Rows 4 and 8 are the interesting ones. Nothing in the grammar forbids them — put the passive rule and the perfect continuous rule together and you get has been being built and had been being built, which are as regularly formed as anything in the language. English simply declines to say them. Three forms of be in a row (been being plus the participle) is more than a speaker will tolerate, and rows 10 and 12 fail on the same grounds.

? The house has been being built for three years. · ? The road had been being resurfaced all that week. · ? At noon the samples will be being tested.

Here is what English writes instead. There are four escape routes, and native speakers use all of them without noticing.

Instead ofEnglish writesExample
has been being builtthe plain perfect passiveThe house has been built. (loses the duration, keeps the sense)
has been being builtan active with a vague subjectThey've been building the house for three years.
has been being builtunder + nounThe house has been under construction for three years.
had been being discussedunder + noun, past perfectThe proposal had been under discussion for months.
will be being testedthe simple future passiveAt noon the samples will be tested.

The under construction deserves a line of its own, because it is the idiomatic repair and learners rarely meet it: under construction, under review, under discussion, under investigation, under consideration, under repair, under attack, under way. Each is a noun phrase doing the work of a continuous passive, and each is available in every tense, because be is free to inflect in front of it.

The bridge is under repair. · was under repair · has been under repair · had been under repair · will be under repair

That is a complete continuous passive paradigm with no ✗ being being anywhere in it. Rows 2 and 6 — is being built, was being built — are entirely normal; it is only the perfect versions that collapse. If you can say it with one been, say it.

Turning an active sentence passive#

Three moves, in this order.

  1. The object becomes the subject. If it is a pronoun, it changes case: me → I, him → he, them → they.
  2. The verb becomes be + past participle, with be carrying the original tense and agreeing with the new subject.
  3. The old subject becomes a by-phrase — or disappears. Deleting it is the normal case, not the exception.

Worked through:

The police have been questioning him for six hours.

  1. him → he. 2. have been questioning → present perfect continuous, which has no writable passive, so the plain present perfect: has been questioned. 3. the police → by the police, or dropped.

→ He has been questioned for six hours.

The same operation across the tenses, so you can see that only be is moving:

ActivePassive
Someone cleans the office every night.The office is cleaned every night.
They are repaving the street.The street is being repaved.

A student found my wallet.My wallet was found by a student.

The last row is worth a second look. An active with a negative subject — nobody, no one, none of them — comes out as a negative passive, because the passive has nowhere to put nobody except a by-phrase, and ✗ I was told by nobody is not English.

The by-phrase, and when to drop it#

Most passives in running text have no by-phrase at all. That is the point of the construction: it lets you talk about what happened to something without committing yourself about who did it.

Drop by when the doer is:

ReasonExample
unknownMy bike was stolen last night.
obvious from the contextHe was arrested on Tuesday. (by the police — who else?)
irrelevantThe results will be mailed next week.
people in generalEnglish is spoken here.
being concealed on purposeMistakes were made.

That last row is famous for a reason. The agentless passive is the standard instrument of institutional evasion in English — the account was closed, the decision was taken, your call could not be completed — and knowing that it is doing that is part of reading English prose.

Keep by when the doer is news. English puts important and heavy material at the end of the clause, and the passive exists partly to let you get a long or newsworthy subject down there:

Hamlet was written by Shakespeare. (the whole point is who) The proposal was rejected by every member of the committee except one. (too heavy to be a subject) The fire was started by a space heater in the back room. She was raised by her grandmother.

Compare the two orders and you can hear the information structure move:

A space heater in the back room started the fire. (the heater is old news; the fire is the point) The fire was started by a space heater in the back room. (the fire is old news; the cause is the point)

Both are correct. They answer different questions, and the passive is how English chooses which one it is answering.

Two smaller notes. A with-phrase names the instrument, not the doer — He was killed by a burglar against He was killed with a hammer; both can appear at once: He was killed by a burglar with a hammer. And the by-phrase is not the only survivor: some passives keep a whole prepositional tail, as in The bill was paid by the company to the contractor in March.

Two objects, two passives#

A ditransitive verb — one that takes both an indirect and a direct object — gives you two ways in.

They gave me a book.

→ I was given a book. (the indirect object promoted — by far the commoner) → A book was given to me. (the direct object promoted; to* has to reappear)*

Note what happened to the preposition. In the active, me sits bare in front of a book. Promote a book instead, and me has to be re-attached with to — because the passive has already taken the slot that made the bare form possible. Never ✗ A book was given me, which is Victorian at best.

The verbs that work this way, with both passives shown:

ActiveIndirect-object passiveDirect-object passive

They sent me an invoice.I was sent an invoice.An invoice was sent to me.
He told us the truth.We were told the truth.The truth was told to us. (stiff)

They paid him a bonus.He was paid a bonus.A bonus was paid to him.

Also in the family: teach, lend, hand, promise, owe, grant, allow, refuse, charge, ask (I was asked a question), leave (She was left a small fortune).

And now the trap. A set of verbs that Russian treats identically refuses the indirect-object passive in English, because in the active they never take a bare indirect object either. They require to from the start, so only the direct object can be promoted:

✗ I was explained the rule. → ✓ The rule was explained to me. / ✓ Someone explained the rule to me. ✗ We were described the procedure. → ✓ The procedure was described to us. ✗ He was suggested a different route. → ✓ A different route was suggested to him. ✗ I was said that the office was closed. → ✓ I was told that the office was closed.

The members of this club: explain, describe, suggest, propose, announce, mention, report, recommend, introduce, repeat, say. Russian мне объяснили, мне предложили, мне сообщили all put the person first, which is exactly why ✗ I was explained is one of the most durable errors Russian speakers make in English. The repair is always the same — move the person into a to-phrase, or switch to a verb that does take two objects (tell, give, show).

The prepositional passive#

English can promote the object of a preposition, which Russian cannot do at all. The preposition stays behind, stranded at the end of the clause, and it looks wrong to a learner until it is pointed out.

Somebody slept in this bed. → This bed has been slept in. · They laughed at him. → He was laughed at. They sent for the doctor. → The doctor was sent for. · We deal with that in chapter 3. → That is dealt with in chapter 3. Nobody has looked at the figures. → The figures haven't been looked at. · They broke into the office. → The office was broken into. Everyone talks about her. → She is talked about constantly. · They took care of the paperwork. → The paperwork was taken care of.

Do not delete the preposition. ✗ This bed has been slept and ✗ He was laughed are not sentences; the preposition belongs to the verb, and the verb keeps it.

The passive with reporting verbs#

When the thing being reported is a general opinion rather than a particular person's words, English has two passive frames, and they are interchangeable in meaning.

Frame A — impersonal it: It + passive reporting verb + that-clause.

It is said that he owns three houses. · It is thought that the fire started in the kitchen. It was believed that the disease spread through water. · It has been reported that talks will resume Monday. · It is expected that the bill will pass.

Frame B — the subject raised
the subject of the that-clause becomes the subject of the reporting verb, and the rest turns into an infinitive.

He is said to own three houses. · The fire is thought to have started in the kitchen. The disease was believed to spread through water. · Talks are reported to be resuming Monday. · The bill is expected to pass.

The infinitive carries the time, exactly as it does elsewhere. A simple infinitive means the same time as the reporting verb; a perfect infinitive means earlier.

that-clauseRaised version
It is said that he is rich.He is said to be rich.
It is said that he was rich.He is said to have been rich.
It is said that he owns three houses.He is said to own three houses.
It is said that he has been living abroad.He is said to have been living abroad.
It was said that he had left.He was said to have left.

The verbs that take both frames: say, think, believe, know, report, consider, expect, understand, assume, presume, allege, claim, estimate, rumor, suppose, find, hold. A few are frame-A only in practice (It is rumored that… is far commoner than He is rumored to…, though both occur), and say is frame-B's most frequent member by a wide margin.

There can be raised too, which surprises people:

It is said that there are two copies. → There are said to be two copies. It is believed that there were survivors. → There are believed to have been survivors.

Two cautions:

  • Be supposed to has drifted. He is supposed to be rich can still mean "people say so," but in everyday American English be supposed to usually means obligation or expectation: You're supposed to sign both copies. We were supposed to meet at six. If you mean the reporting sense, said or thought is safer.
  • Frame B needs the passive. ✗ They say him to be rich is not English. The raising only happens under a passive reporting verb.

These frames are the impersonal cousins of ordinary reported speech; the tense machinery for reporting a named speaker is in Backshift.

Get-passives#

Spoken American English has a second passive built on get instead of be.

He got fired. · She got promoted. · My phone got stolen. · They got caught.

Three things distinguish it.

It is informal. Get belongs to speech and to casual writing: an annual report says were terminated, a person says got fired. It also leans toward events, and usually unwelcome ones. Be + participle is neutral and can describe a state; get + participle describes something that happened, at a moment, usually to the subject's cost or benefit rather than indifferently. Compare:

The window was broken. (ambiguous: a state — it is currently broken — or an event) The window got broken. (unambiguously an event, and probably somebody's fault)

It takes do-support, because get is a lexical verb, not an auxiliary. This is the part learners get wrong:

✗ Got he fired? / ✗ He got not fired. ✓ Did he get fired? · ✓ He didn't get fired. · ✓ He hasn't gotten fired yet.

Note the American participle: gotten in the perfect, not got. He has gotten promoted twice.

A short list of the fixed get-passives, several of which have no be version at all in ordinary speech: get married, get divorced, get engaged, get dressed, get lost, get hurt, get paid, get hired, get fired, get promoted, get arrested, get caught, get stuck, get started, get involved, get confused, get used to.

Two boundaries. Get is bad with stative and reporting verbs — ✗ He got known to be rich, ✗ The house got owned by a bank — and get + object + participle is a different structure entirely, a causative: I got my hair cut means I arranged it, not that it happened to me.

Verbs that refuse the passive#

Only a transitive verb — one with an object to promote — can go passive, and not even all of those.

Intransitive verbs have no object, so there is nothing to promote. This produces a small family of errors that all look alike:

✗ The accident was happened yesterday. → ✓ The accident happened yesterday. · ✗ He was arrived late. → ✓ He arrived late. ✗ The war was ended in 1945. → ✓ The war ended in 1945. (but The war was ended by the treaty — a different, transitive end) · ✗ What was occurred? → ✓ What occurred?

Also in this group: appear, disappear, die, fall, rise, remain, belong, exist, seem, come, go, sleep (in its bare use), last.

Stative and relational verbs take an object but still refuse, because nothing is transferred and so nothing can receive:

✗ Two brothers are had by her. → ✓ She has two brothers. · ✗ He is fitted by the coat. → ✓ The coat fits him. ✗ Her mother is resembled by Anna. → ✓ Anna resembles her mother. · ✗ Ten dollars was cost by the book. → ✓ The book cost ten dollars.

The standing members: have (possession), fit, suit, lack, resemble, cost, weigh (measurement), equal, contain, comprise, mean, become (in that hat becomes you). The full list with the reasoning is in the verb reference, along with the transitivity classes the rule depends on.

Note that the same verb can sit in two classes: have passivizes freely in its non-possessive senses — A good time was had by all, She was had (= swindled).

The middle voice: the -ся forms that are not passives#

English has a set of verbs that look active but describe something happening to the subject with no agent in sight. Grammarians call this the middle or ergative use, and it is exactly where Russian reaches for -ся.

The door opened. (Дверь открылась) · The glass broke. (Стакан разбился) · The book sells well. (Книга хорошо продаётся) This shirt washes easily. · The engine started on the third try. · Prices rose sharply. · The meeting ended at four.

These are not passives and must not be made into them. ✗ The door was opened by itself is wrong twice over; ✓ The door opened by itself is right. The difference against the true passive is agency:

The door opened. (no one is blamed; it just did) The door was opened. (somebody opened it) The door is open. (a state — open* here is an adjective, not a participle)*

Russian collapses all three into Дверь открыта. English keeps them apart: adjective for the state, passive for the event, middle for the event with nobody responsible.

Why English needs the passive more than Russian does#

The passive is roughly twice as common in English prose as its counterparts are in Russian, and the reason is structural rather than stylistic.

Russian marks grammatical roles with case endings, so word order is free. Книгу написал Петров and Петров написал книгу mean the same thing and differ only in what is being foregrounded; the -у on книгу tells you it is the object no matter where it stands. A Russian writer who wants the object first simply puts it first.

English has no case on nouns, so word order is the marking. Move the object to the front of an English clause and it stops being the object — The book wrote Petrov is a sentence about a literate book. The only way English can put the affected thing first is to change the voice. The passive is English's word-order tool.

So Russian solves with word order what English has to solve with grammar, and Russian's own passive-like devices are correspondingly narrower. There are three:

  1. The reflexive -ся passive — Дом строится, Вопрос обсуждается. Mostly imperfective, mostly written.
  2. The impersonal plural — a third-person plural verb with no subject at all: Мне сказали, Его уволили, Здесь не курят. This is the everyday Russian equivalent of an agentless English passive.
  3. The short past passive participle — Дом был построен в 1920 году, Работа сделана. Perfective, and closest in feel to the English was built.

Two habits to carry across, and one to leave behind.

  • Carry across: the impersonal plural is your best guide to when English wants a passive. If the Russian sentence has a bare third-person plural verb with nobody named, English almost always wants be + participle. Мне сказали → I was told, not ✗ They said me.
  • Carry across: English keeps they say, people think, you never know as informal actives alongside the passives, and they are perfectly good: They say he's rich is the spoken version of He is said to be rich.
  • Leave behind: the -ся form does not always mean a passive. Дверь открылась is The door opened; only Дверь открывают / Дверь была открыта is The door was opened. Mapping every -ся to a passive produces ✗ The door was opened by itself and ✗ The book is sold well.

Part 2 — Questions, negatives, and short forms in every tense#

The auxiliary rule#

Every English verb phrase has an operator: the first auxiliary in it. Is in is working. Has in has been working. Will in will have been working. The operator is the only word in the phrase that does grammatical work, and it does five jobs:

JobExample
Questions — it moves in front of the subjectShe is working. → Is she working?
Negatives — not / n't attaches to itShe isn't working.
Tags — it is copied onto the endShe's working, isn't she?
Short answers — it stands for the whole phraseYes, she is.
Emphasis and ellipsisShe is working. / So is he. / She works harder than he does.

A tense that has no auxiliary of its own — the present simple and the past simple — is issued one: do. That is all do-support is.

Do-support, and why ✗ Did you went happens#

The simple tenses have no auxiliary, so there is nothing for a question or a negative to operate on. English inserts do purely to have something to operate on, and do then carries everything the lexical verb was carrying: the tense, and the third-person -s.

she works → does she work · she doesn't work she worked → did she work · she didn't work

The tense moves onto do, and the lexical verb returns to the bare base form. This is the whole of it, and the error is what happens when the second half of the sentence is forgotten:

✗ Did you went to the meeting? → ✓ Did you go to the meeting? · ✗ She doesn't works here. → ✓ She doesn't work here. ✗ Does he has a license? → ✓ Does he have a license? · ✗ Didn't he came back? → ✓ Didn't he come back? · ✗ Did she was at home? → ✓ Was she at home?

Every one of them marks the tense twice. English marks tense exactly once per verb phrase, at the front. Did has already said "past"; went saying it again is like writing the the.

Two further points about do. Never with another auxiliary: if the phrase already has an operator, do has no job and cannot appear — ✗ Do you can swim? → ✓ Can you swim?; ✗ Did you have finished? → ✓ Had you finished?; ✗ Do you are ready? → ✓ Are you ready?

And emphatic do is the third use, and it is the one that shows what do is really for. With no question and no negative in sight, do gives the sentence an operator so that the operator can be stressed:

I do like it. (contradicting someone who said otherwise) · He did call — I saw the number. She does know, she just isn't saying. · Do sit down. (warm imperative)

Russian marks this with particles — ведь, же, таки — and with intonation alone. English builds a word for it.

Subject questions against object questions#

This is the one place where an English question has no inversion and no do, and learners over-apply the rule they have just learned.

When the wh-word is the subject, the question is already in statement order. There is nothing to invert, and nothing for do to do:

Who broke the window? (not ✗ Who did break the window?) · What happened? Which train goes to Boston? · How many people came? · Whose car is blocking the driveway?

When the wh-word is the object — or the complement of a preposition — the ordinary machinery applies:

What did you break? · Who(m) did you see? · Which train did you take? How many people did you invite? · Whose car did you borrow?

Side by side:

Subject question — no doObject question — do
Who called?Who did you call?

What fell off the shelf?What did you knock off the shelf?

The auxiliaries behave the same way in subject questions: they simply stay where they were, with no inversion.

Who is calling? · Who has been using my computer? · Who will pay for this? · Who had already left?

Three notes.

  • Subject questions take the singular by default. Who wants coffee? is right even when six people do. ✗ Who are coming?
  • A subject question can take do — for emphasis only. Who did break the window? is a contradiction of something just said, not a neutral question.
  • Whom is optional in American English, except immediately after a preposition. Who did you see? is the normal spoken and written form; Whom did you see? is formal; To whom did you speak? is formal but obligatory in that shape, and everyone else says Who did you speak to?

Russian speakers have a running start and a small trap. Russian marks the difference with case on the word itself — кто against кого — so the distinction is familiar. The trap is that Russian keeps the same word order either way (Кто разбил окно? / Кого ты видел?), while English changes the whole sentence structure.

Negative questions and what they imply#

A negative question contracts n't onto the operator and moves the whole thing forward:

Isn't she coming? · Didn't you call her? · Haven't they finished? · Won't he mind?

The uncontracted version separates not and puts it after the subject. It is formal, emphatic, and slightly cold:

Did you not call her? · Have they not finished? · Is she not coming?

What is never possible is ✗ Did not you call her? — the uncontracted not stays behind the subject.

Negative questions almost never ask for information. They carry an attitude, and which one depends on context:

UseExampleWhat it means
SurpriseAren't you coming?I assumed you were
Seeking agreementIsn't it beautiful?say yes
ReproachWhy didn't you tell me?you should have
Polite offer or invitationWouldn't you like to sit down?softer than Would you
Recalling a factDidn't he used to work here?I'm fairly sure he did

And now the answer problem. English answers the fact, not the question's polarity. Yes always goes with a positive verb and no always with a negative one, no matter how the question was framed:

Didn't you call her? — No (= I didn't call her). — Yes (= I did call her).

Russian works the other way: нет can mean "no, what you suggested is wrong — I did call." A bare yes or no in answer to an English negative question is therefore a reliable way to be misunderstood in both directions. Always answer with the verb attached: No, I didn't. / Yes, I did. / Yes, it is. / No, he hasn't.

Question tags for every tense#

A tag is a miniature question welded to the end of a statement. It copies the operator, uses a pronoun subject, and flips the polarity.

She's working, isn't she? · She isn't working, is she?

Positive statement → negative tag. Negative statement → positive tag. Across the twelve tenses, the tag column of the table above is the complete answer, but here it is with the negatives as well:

The special cases, all of which are fixed and have to be memorized:

Two more facts about tags that grammar books often omit.

  • Intonation decides what the tag means. Falling intonation makes it a request for confirmation from someone who already agrees — Nice day, isn't it? Rising intonation makes it a genuine question — You locked the door, didn't you? asked with real doubt.
  • A same-polarity tag exists, and it does not ask anything. Positive statement plus positive tag signals interest, suspicion, or sarcasm: So you're the new manager, are you? · He thinks he's clever, does he? Do not produce these by accident; native speakers hear them as loaded.

Russian gets all of this with one invariable tag — правда?, да?, не так ли?, ведь так? — which is why English tags feel disproportionately hard. An English tag agrees in three ways at once: with the operator, with the subject, and against the polarity. Get the operator right and the other two follow.

Short answers#

A short answer repeats the operator and nothing else. Repeating the lexical verb is the error.

Do you like it? — Yes, I do. (not ✗ Yes, I like.) · Has she left? — No, she hasn't. Are they coming? — Yes, they are. (not ✗ Yes, they're.) · Will he sign? — No, he won't. Had you met before? — Yes, we had. · Were you waiting long? — No, I wasn't. · Is this yours? — Yes, it is.

Two rules inside that list.

  • Never contract an affirmative short answer. ✗ Yes, I'm. ✗ Yes, he's. ✗ Yes, they've. The contraction needs something to lean on, and at the end of a clause there is nothing. Negative short answers contract freely — No, I'm not. / No, he isn't. The rule and its relatives are in when not to contract.
  • A bare yes or no is abrupt in English in a way it is not in Russian. Do you have a minute? — Yes. reads as curt; Yes, I do. is the neutral form.

The operator also carries the agreement and disagreement formulas, and they are worth learning as a set:

FormulaExample
Agreeing with a positiveI'm tired. — So am I. / Me too. / I am too.
Agreeing with a negativeI don't drive. — Neither do I. / Nor do I. / Me neither.
Disagreeing with a positiveHe's late. — No, he isn't. / Actually, he isn't.
Echo question (interest)I passed. — Did you? · She's leaving. — Is she?
Substituting a whole clauseWill it rain? — I hope not. / I think so. / I'm afraid so.

Note the inversion in So am I and Neither do I. It is obligatory: ✗ So I am means something else entirely (agreement with a fact just pointed out — You've got a flat tire. — So I have.).

Part 3 — Tense in subordinate clauses#

A main clause picks its tense by meaning. A subordinate clause sometimes does, and sometimes does not — it may be constrained by the conjunction that introduces it, by the tense of the clause it hangs on, or by a frame that dictates a tense before you have made any choices. This part sorts out which is which.

Time clauses: the future is forbidden#

After a time conjunction, a future meaning takes a present form. Will is not permitted.

I'll call you when I get home. (not ✗ when I will get) As soon as she arrives, let me know. Wait here until I come back. Check the oil before you leave. After the movie ends, we'll get something to eat. By the time you read this, I'll be in Denver. Once the paperwork clears, the money is yours. The moment he walks in, tell him I need him. Whenever you're ready, we'll start. I'll be grading papers while you're at the game. You can stay as long as you pay rent.

The full list of conjunctions this applies to: when, as soon as, until, till, before, after, by the time, once, the moment, the minute, whenever, while, as, as long as, every time, next time, the next time, now that.

The logic is not arbitrary. The main clause is the one making the claim about the future, and it carries will to prove it. The subordinate clause is only fixing the point on the timeline that the claim is measured against, and the conjunction has already said the point is in the future. Marking it twice adds nothing, so English forbids the second marking — in almost exactly the way it forbids ✗ Did you went.

This is the single most persistent error Russian speakers make in English, because Russian marks the future in both clauses and hears nothing odd about it:

Когда я приду, я позвоню тебе. → ✗ When I will come, I will call you. → ✓ When I come, I'll call you. Как только она закончит, мы начнём. → ✓ As soon as she finishes, we'll start. Подожди, пока я не вернусь. → ✓ Wait until I get back.

Note also that the Russian пока не carries a negative English does not want: ✗ Wait until I don't come back. Until already means "up to the point when."

The present perfect option#

A time clause can use the present perfect instead of the present simple, and the difference is completion. The perfect insists that the first event will be finished before the second begins.

I'll call you when I have finished. (finished first, then the call) · As soon as you have signed both copies, we'll process the claim. After I have spoken to her, I'll let you know. · Once the check has cleared, the car is yours. When you've read it, pass it on. · I won't decide until I've seen the figures.

Which to use:

ConjunctionPresent simplePresent perfect
whenwhen I finish — normal, neutralwhen I have finished — completion stressed
as soon asas soon as I finish — normalas soon as I have finished — slightly more emphatic
afterafter I finishafter I have finished — the commoner form
onceonce I finishonce I have finished — the commoner form
beforebefore I finish — required✗ before I have finished — nonsensical
untiluntil I finish — normaluntil I have finished — also fine

The honest summary: the present simple is always safe and is commoner in speech; the present perfect is available whenever completion is the point, and is preferred after after and once, where completion is built into the meaning.

The same option exists in the past. After a past main clause, a time clause takes the past simple or the past perfect, and would is banned in just the way will is:

He said he'd call when he got home. (not ✗ when he would get) · As soon as she had signed, they filed the paperwork. · I waited until the rain stopped.

The when that is allowed to take will#

When does two different jobs, and only one of them bans the future. If when introduces a time clause — telling you when the main event happens — no will. If it introduces an indirect question — a noun clause, the object of a verb like know, ask, tell, wonder, find out — will is perfectly normal, because the clause is now a question about the future rather than a marker of it.

Time clause — no willIndirect question — will is fine
I'll tell him when he arrives.I don't know when he will arrive.
Call me when you're ready.Let me know when you'll be ready.
We'll start when everyone gets here.Do you know when everyone will get here?
She'll sign it when she sees the terms.I wonder when she'll see the terms.

Read the first row both ways and the difference is audible. I'll tell him when he arrives = I will do the telling at the moment of his arrival. I'll tell him when he will arrive = I will inform him of his arrival time. Two completely different sentences with one word changed.

If splits the same way. A conditional if-clause refuses will; an indirect-question if (= whether) takes it freely:

✓ If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel. (condition) · ✓ I don't know if it will rain tomorrow. (question — = whether)

One further will gets into if-clauses legitimately, the volitional one meaning "be willing to" — If you will just sign here — and it is dealt with in Conditionals.

Sequence of tenses in reported speech#

A that-clause under a past reporting verb agrees with that verb rather than with the present moment. The verb moves one step further back, and one step only.

DirectReported after said
"I work downtown."He said he worked downtown.
"I'm waiting."He said he was waiting.
"I've finished."She said she had finished.
"I saw it."She said she had seen it. (or, commonly, she saw it)
"I'll call."She said she would call.
"I can drive."He said he could drive.
"I had left."He said he had left. (already at the floor)

The full mechanism — the one-step ladder, why the past perfect is the bottom of it, which modals cannot move, and the cases where American speakers leave the tense alone — is Backshift. Three things belong here only as reminders:

  • A present-tense reporting verb triggers nothing. She says she is tired.
  • Only the finite verb moves. has been waiting → had been waiting, never ✗ had been waited.
  • A time clause inside a report shifts too. "I'll call when I get home" → He said he would call when he got home.

Conditional clauses#

Conditional clauses have their own rules and their own topic, and the two facts that matter for tense are short enough to state here:

  • No will in the if-clause of a condition. If it rains, we'll cancel.
  • No would in an unreal if-clause. If I had the money, I'd buy it. — never ✗ If I would have the money.

Both follow from the same logic as the time clauses above: the subordinate clause is the setup, and the main clause is where the future or the hypothetical gets marked. Everything else — the four types, the mixed ones, were to, inversion, unless, and the бы mapping — is in Conditionals, with the tense-as-distance idea worked out in Real and unreal.

Since-clauses#

Since meaning "from a point in time" forces a perfect tense in the main clause, and a past simple in its own clause.

I've lived here since I was a child. · She's been much happier since she changed jobs. Nothing has gone right since the merger was announced. · It's been three years since I saw her. · How long has it been since you had a vacation?

The pattern in one line: main clause perfect, since-clause past simple.

The main-clause requirement is not optional, and this is where a very common error lives, because Russian uses the plain present:

Я живу здесь с 2019 года. → ✗ I live here since 2019. → ✓ I've lived here since 2019. Я знаю его с детства. → ✗ I know him since childhood. → ✓ I've known him since childhood. Она работает там с марта. → ✗ She works there since March. → ✓ She's been working there since March.

Two refinements.

  • A since-clause can take a perfect of its own, when the state in it is still running: Since she has been in charge, things have improved describes an ongoing tenure, where Since she took over is the ordinary past-simple version and is commoner.
  • It's been X since… allows both: It's been three years since I saw her (standard American) and …since I've seen her (also heard). Use the past simple in writing.

And the other since — the one meaning because — has no tense constraint whatever: Since you're here, let's get started. Since he had already paid, we let him in. If since can be replaced by as or because, none of the rules above apply.

It's the first time…#

A frame that hands you a tense. The pattern is perfect in the relative clause, matched to the tense of the frame.

This is the first time I've driven a stick shift. · That's the third time he's called today. · It's the only time she's ever complained. It was the first time she'd flown alone. · It was the last time anyone had seen him.

Present frame → present perfect. Past frame → past perfect. The frames: the first / second / third / last / only time, and it's the first time, this is the first time, that's the first time alike.

Russian uses a plain present here, and the transfer error is predictable:

Я первый раз веду машину с ручной коробкой. → ✗ It's the first time I drive a stick shift. → ✓ It's the first time I've driven a stick shift. Он уже третий раз звонит. → ✓ That's the third time he's called.

This is the best… I have ever…#

The same machinery under a superlative. A relative clause after a superlative — or after the only, the first, the last — takes a perfect, usually with ever.

That's the best meal I've ever eaten. · This is the worst traffic I've seen all year. · She's the smartest person I've ever worked with. It was the strangest thing I'd ever heard. (past frame → past perfect) · That's the only thing he's said all morning. · This is the first house we've looked at that I actually like.

Ever is optional but idiomatic, and it is the word that makes the perfect feel inevitable: the best meal I've ever eaten covers a lifetime up to now, which is exactly what the present perfect is for. Without a perfect, the sentence changes meaning: That's the best meal I ate would have to be about some particular closed period, and needs a time expression to survive — the best meal I ate in Italy.

Relative clauses and tense agreement#

Here is the clause type that is free. A relative clause is not governed by the main verb; it takes whatever tense its own content requires, and a past main clause does not drag it backward.

I met a man who works at the embassy. (he still does) · I met a man who worked at the embassy. (then, or he has since left) She was wearing the dress she bought in Paris. · He mentioned a book that will be published in the fall. · The house that was built in 1920 is still standing. Yesterday I ran into the woman who is buying our car. · They fired the manager who has been running the place since March.

Read the first two rows against each other. Both are correct English, and the tense carries real information about whether the job is current — information the reader will take seriously, so choose deliberately. Contrast this with a that-clause under a reporting verb, where agreement is expected:

Clause typeGoverned by the main verb?Example
Reported that-clauseyes, by defaultHe said he was tired.
Time clauseyes — and the future is bannedHe said he'd call when he got home.
Relative clausenoHe mentioned a man who works upstairs.
Clause of reason or resultnoHe left because he has an early flight.
Comparison clausenoShe's taller than her brother was at that age.

And a subordinate clause stating a general truth keeps the present under any main verb at all.

He explained that light travels faster than sound. · The teacher said water boils at 212 degrees. · I didn't know that Finland has two official languages.

The "still true" exception#

Backshift is a default, not a law, and the place it lapses most visibly is a fact that has not stopped being true between the speaking and the reporting.

"I'm allergic to shellfish," she said. → She said she is allergic to shellfish. · "I live in Denver," he said. → He said he lives in Denver. "I'll be there at six," he said. → He said he'll be there at six. (if six has not arrived yet) · "The meeting starts at nine," she said. → She said the meeting starts at nine.

The shifted versions — she said she was allergic, he said he lived in Denver — are equally correct and imply nothing about the fact having lapsed. American English shifts by default even for still-true facts, so the unshifted form is a choice with a job rather than a relaxation.

What the job is
the unshifted form puts the reporter's own weight behind the claim and asserts that it holds right now — worth using when the currency of the fact matters, as when warning a caterer about an allergy.
And what it costs
it commits you. If the fact has stopped being true, the unshifted version is not merely awkward but false. He said he works at Boeing is wrong if he was laid off last month; He said he worked at Boeing stays silent about the present and is always safe. The full sorting of the cases — the impossible, the untriggered, and the genuinely optional — is in When not to backshift.

Three more kinds of clause refuse backshift outright, because they contain no shiftable tense to begin with: the unreal past (He said if he had more time he would help), the subjunctive (She insisted that he be present), and any non-finite form (He said he wanted to leave, that he hated waiting).

Quick reference#

The passive, in one line. be + past participle, with be carrying the tense. Twelve tenses in, ten writable passives out — has been being and had been being are replaced by under construction, has been built, or an active.

The passive transformation, in three moves. Object becomes subject (and changes case). Verb becomes be + participle, with be in the original tense and agreeing with the new subject. Old subject becomes by + phrase, or vanishes — and vanishing is the normal case. Two objects give two passives: I was given a book (usual) / A book was given to me (the to comes back) — but never with explain, describe, suggest, announce, mention, say: ✓ The rule was explained to me.

The operator rule. Find the first auxiliary. That is the word that inverts for a question, takes n't for a negative, is copied into the tag, and stands alone in a short answer. If the phrase has no auxiliary — present simple, past simple — insert do and put the lexical verb back in its base form.

SituationWhat happens
she is workingis does everything
she will have been workingwill does everything
she worksno auxiliary → does she work / she doesn't work
she workedno auxiliary → did she work / she didn't work
she has a carmain-verb have takes do in American English

Subject questions have no do. Who called? against Who did you call? If the wh-word is the subject, the sentence is already in order. Negative questions get answered with the verb: Didn't you call? — No, I didn't / Yes, I did, never a bare no.

The three clause types that lose control of their tense.

Time clause — no future, ever. I'll call when I get home. Reported clause — agrees with the reporting verb. He said he was tired. Conditional clause — no will, no would. If it rains, we'll cancel.

The three frames that hand you a tense.

since + a starting point → perfect in the main clause. I've lived here since 2019. it's the first time → present perfect. It's the first time I've driven one. superlative + relative clause → present perfect. The best meal I've ever eaten.

The clause type that keeps control. Relative clauses. I met a man who works at the embassy and …who worked at the embassy are both correct and say different things.
