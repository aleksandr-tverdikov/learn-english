Tenses: reference and practice#

All twelve tense-aspect combinations in one table — active, passive, negative, interrogative — plus the future constructions, the timelines, the spelling and sound of every ending, the time expressions that choose the tense for you, and ninety-seven exercises with worked answers.

← Tenses · All grammar topics

The other sections of this topic take the tenses one at a time — present simple, present continuous, present perfect, present perfect continuous, past simple, past continuous, past perfect, past perfect continuous, the future — and then step back to ask which one a given sentence wants and what happens to all of them in the passive and inside subordinate clauses. This section puts the whole system side by side, because side by side is how the choice actually presents itself. You already know what you mean; the only open question is which of twelve shapes says it. Everything in the first half is a lookup table. Everything in the second half is graded against those tables, with a reason attached to every answer rather than a bare key.

The master table#

Three times — past, present, future — multiplied by four aspects — simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous — give twelve combinations, and English uses all twelve in ordinary speech. One verb runs down the whole table so the machinery stays visible: work, a plain regular verb, with she as the subject.

#TenseStructureWhat it meansSignal expressionExample (work)
1Present simplebase (+ -s for he/she/it)habits, facts, permanent states, timetablesevery day, usually, on Mondays, alwaysShe works downtown.
2Present continuousam / is / are + -ingin progress now, temporary, a fixed arrangement aheadright now, at the moment, this week, currentlyShe's working from home this week.
3Present perfecthave / has + past participlea past event whose result is present; unfinished timejust, already, yet, ever, never, since, so farShe's worked here since April.
4Present perfect continuoushave / has been + -inga stretch of activity running up to nowfor two hours, since noon, all morning, latelyShe's been working since seven.
5Past simplepast form (V2)a finished event inside finished timeyesterday, last week, in 2019, two days agoShe worked there in 2019.
6Past continuouswas / were + -ingin progress at a past moment; the backgroundat eight last night, while, when … came inShe was working when I called.
7Past perfecthad + past participlethe earlier of two past eventsby the time, before, already (by then), never … beforeShe had worked there before she moved.
8Past perfect continuoushad been + -inga stretch of activity up to a past pointfor six hours when …, since morning, all dayShe had been working for nine hours.
9Future simplewill + baseprediction, a decision made as you speak, a promisetomorrow, next week, in a minute, probably, I thinkShe'll work late tonight.
10Future continuouswill be + -ingin progress at a future momentthis time tomorrow, at noon on Friday, all day SaturdayShe'll be working at nine.
11Future perfectwill have + past participlefinished before a future deadlineby June, by then, by the time you get hereShe'll have worked here a year by June.
12Future perfect continuouswill have been + -inga stretch measured up to a future pointby August … for ten years, by then … for monthsBy August she'll have been working on it for six months.

Read the table down the aspect rather than across the time and it stops looking like twelve unrelated items. Rows 1, 5 and 9 are the simple: an event treated as a whole, with no interest in its edges. Rows 2, 6 and 10 are the continuous: an event treated as a line, unfinished, with a middle you can stand in. Rows 3, 7 and 11 are the perfect: an event seen from a later vantage point, which is what have actually contributes. Rows 4, 8 and 12 are the two ideas stacked — a line, seen from a vantage point after it.

The same twelve with an irregular verb#

Nothing in the structure column changes for an irregular verb. What changes is which word fills the slot. Here is take — take, took, taken, taking — with he as the subject.

#TenseExample (take)Where the irregularity shows
1Present simpleHe takes the bus.nowhere — the -s is regular
2Present continuousHe's taking the bus.nowhere — the -ing drops a silent e, which is a spelling rule, not irregularity
3Present perfectHe's taken the bus.the participle taken, not ✗ took
4Present perfect continuousHe's been taking the bus.nowhere — the participle here is been
5Past simpleHe took the bus.the past took
6Past continuousHe was taking the bus.nowhere
7Past perfectHe had taken the bus.the participle taken
8Past perfect continuousHe had been taking the bus.nowhere
9Future simpleHe'll take the bus.nowhere — after a modal the verb is bare
10Future continuousHe'll be taking the bus.nowhere
11Future perfectHe'll have taken the bus.the participle taken
12Future perfect continuousHe'll have been taking the bus.nowhere

An irregular verb is irregular in exactly two cells of your memory, the past and the participle, and they surface in exactly four of the twelve rows: the past simple uses V2, and rows 3, 7 and 11 use V3. Everywhere else the verb appears as the bare base or as the -ing form, both of which are fully predictable. That is why ✗ he has took and ✗ she has went are the errors that survive longest — they are the only places the choice is live.

The twelve in the passive#

The passive is be + past participle, and the tense is carried by be. Whatever the active does to the main verb, the passive does to be instead, and the main verb freezes as a participle. The verb below is write, the subject the report.

#TensePassive structureExample
1Present simpleam / is / are + V3The report is written every Monday.
2Present continuousam / is / are being + V3The report is being written right now.
3Present perfecthave / has been + V3The report has been written.
4Present perfect continuoushave / has been being + V3not used — rewrite as active, or use the present perfect
5Past simplewas / were + V3The report was written last week.
6Past continuouswas / were being + V3The report was being written when the power went out.
7Past perfecthad been + V3The report had been written before the meeting.
8Past perfect continuoushad been being + V3not used — rewrite as active, or use the past perfect
9Future simplewill be + V3The report will be written by Friday.
10Future continuouswill be being + V3avoided — say They'll be writing the report
11Future perfectwill have been + V3The report will have been written by Friday.
12Future perfect continuouswill have been being + V3not used — rewrite as active

Four of the twelve are grammatically derivable and nobody writes them. The rule is worth stating as a rule: the passive does not stack two forms of being. Has been being written is not wrong in the way ✗ has wrote is wrong; it is simply a sentence no American writes, and an editor would replace it. When you need the meaning, use the active voice, which has no such problem: They've been writing the report all week.

Three further passive facts, worked out at length in Passive, questions, and clauses:

  • Only a transitive verb has a passive. Arrive, happen, sleep, belong, seem and exist take no object, so there is nothing to promote: ✗ The accident was happened.
  • Get replaces be in informal American English, and it adds a suggestion that the subject was affected: The window got broken. It is normal in speech, restricted in edited prose.
  • A two-object verb has two passives, and American English prefers the one that promotes the person: I was given this ring is more natural than This ring was given to me, unless the ring is what the sentence is about.

Questions and negatives, all twelve#

English asks a question by moving an auxiliary in front of the subject, and negates by putting not after it. Eleven of the twelve tenses already contain an auxiliary, so both operations are free. Only the present simple and the past simple have none, and those two borrow do.

#TenseStatementYes-or-no questionNegative
1Present simpleShe works here.Does she work here?She doesn't work here.
2Present continuousShe's working.Is she working?She isn't working.
3Present perfectShe has worked here.Has she worked here?She hasn't worked here.
4Present perfect continuousShe's been working.Has she been working?She hasn't been working.
5Past simpleShe worked here.Did she work here?She didn't work here.
6Past continuousShe was working.Was she working?She wasn't working.
7Past perfectShe had worked here.Had she worked here?She hadn't worked here.
8Past perfect continuousShe'd been working.Had she been working?She hadn't been working.
9Future simpleShe'll work late.Will she work late?She won't work late.
10Future continuousShe'll be working.Will she be working?She won't be working.
11Future perfectShe'll have worked a year.Will she have worked a year?She won't have worked a year.
12Future perfect continuousShe'll have been working.Will she have been working?She won't have been working.

Four rules cover every cell above, and between them they account for most of what goes wrong.

RuleWhat it meansThe error it prevents
The first auxiliary moves, and only the firstWill she have been working? — will moves, have and been stay put✗ Have will she been working?
Only one tense marker per verb phrasedo carries the tense, so the main verb goes back to base✗ Did you saw him? · ✗ She doesn't works here.
Add do only when there is no auxiliaryrows 1 and 5 only✗ Did you have finished? · ✗ Does she is working?
A question about the subject does not invertwh-word replaces the subject and the word order stays flatWho broke the window? — not ✗ Who did break

Wh-questions are the same operation with a question word in front: Where does she work? · How long has she been working? · What were you doing? The exception in the last row is worth a second look, because it is invisible until you compare two sentences: What happened? asks about the subject and stays flat, while What did you see? asks about the object and inverts. The full treatment, including negative questions and tag questions, is in Passive, questions, and clauses.

The future constructions#

English has no future tense — no ending on the verb marks future time, the way -ed marks past time. What it has instead is about ten constructions that point forward, each carrying a different attitude toward the event. Choosing among them is a matter of what you know and when you knew it, not of grammar.

ConstructionStructureWhat it meansChoose it whenExample
willwill + basea neutral prediction, a decision made as you speak, a promise, an offerthere was no plan a second agoThat's the phone — I'll get it.
be going to (intention)am / is / are going to + basean intention formed before you started speakingthe plan already existsWe're going to repaint the kitchen.
be going to (prediction)am / is / are going to + basea prediction resting on evidence you can see right nowthe evidence is in front of youLook at those clouds — it's going to rain.
present continuousam / is / are + -inga fixed arrangement, normally with a time and another personit is in your calendarI'm seeing the dentist at four.
present simplebase (+ -s)a timetable or schedule nobody in the conversation controlstrains, flights, programs, opening hoursThe flight leaves at 6:05.
future continuouswill be + -ingin progress at a future moment; also a neutral inquiry with no pressureyou want the middle of the event, or you want to sound uncommittedThis time tomorrow I'll be flying over the Atlantic. · Will you be using the car?
future perfectwill have + V3finished before a stated future pointthe deadline matters more than the eventBy Friday I'll have sent the draft.
future perfect continuouswill have been + -inga duration measured up to a future pointyou are counting hours, months, or yearsIn May he'll have been living here for a decade.

Two things to fix in place, because they produce errors that nothing else in the system produces.

Will and going to are not interchangeable, and the difference is about when the decision was made. I'll have the salad is a decision made at the table; I'm going to have the salad means you decided in the car. With predictions, going to points at visible present evidence and will points at your opinion: She's going to be sick (she has gone gray) versus She'll be fine (I think so).

No will in a future time clause. After when, as soon as, once, until, before, after, by the time and if, English uses the present simple or the present perfect for future time, exactly as it does in a conditional: Call me when you get in, never ✗ when you will get in. The clause is already future by position. The one case that looks like an exception is a wh-clause that is a question in disguise: I don't know when he will arrive — there when he will arrive is the object of know, not a time clause.

Timelines#

Six pictures cover the tenses that a definition alone does not settle. Each one is drawn with now as the vertical bar.

Present perfect vs. past simple#

PAST SIMPLE  —  a point inside finished time

   past                                          now
   ----------X------------------------------------|------>  future
             |                                    |
        "at two o'clock"                     I lost my keys at two.

   The time is over. It is named, known, or askable ("When did you?").


PRESENT PERFECT  —  a past event whose result is still here

   past                                          now
   ----------X====================================|------>  future
             |            the result              |
        (time NOT named)                     I've lost my keys.

   The event is past; what the sentence is about is the state it left.
   Naming a finished time breaks it:  X I've lost them yesterday.

Past perfect#

PAST PERFECT  —  the earlier of two past events

   past                                          now
   ------X1---------------X2----------------------|------>
         |                |
   the train had left   we got there
      (earlier)         (later — past simple)

   "By the time we got there, the train had left."

   X2 is the vantage point. The past perfect is whatever is already
   over when you stand on it. With no second past point in sight,
   the past perfect has nothing to be earlier THAN, and the past
   simple is the right tense.

Future perfect#

FUTURE PERFECT  —  finished before a future deadline

   now                                          future
   ----|----------------X--------|---------------------->
       |                |        |
     today        we finish   BY JUNE

   "By June we'll have finished the bridge."

   The tense points at the deadline, not at the event. That is why
   "by" is almost always present, and why "in June we'll finish"
   is a different sentence: it puts the finishing inside June.

Past continuous with past simple#

PAST CONTINUOUS  —  a line interrupted by a point

   past                                          now
   ------[==============X==============]-----------|----->
          I was cooking |  (still cooking after)
                        |
                the power went out

   "I was cooking when the power went out."

   The continuous is the longer background; the simple is the shorter
   event that lands inside it. Swap them and you change the story:
   "When the power went out, I cooked" = I started after the blackout.

The perfect continuous pair#

PRESENT PERFECT CONTINUOUS  —  a stretch running up to now

   past                                          now
   ------[========================================]|----->
          since noon                               |
   "I've been waiting since noon."

   Still waiting, or just stopped — either way the stretch touches NOW,
   and the sentence is about its length, not about a result.


PAST PERFECT CONTINUOUS  —  a stretch running up to a past point

   past                                          now
   ---[==========================X]----------------|----->
       for nine hours             |
                            she stopped (the past vantage point)
   "She had been driving for nine hours when she stopped."

Spelling: the three endings in one table#

Three endings attach to an English verb — third-person -s, the -ing form, and the regular past -ed — and the same handful of stem shapes governs all three. Reading them together is faster than learning three separate lists, because the stem is the thing doing the deciding.

Three notes that the table cannot carry.

The doubling rule is a stress rule, not a letter rule. Begin doubles because the stress is on -gin; open does not, because the stress is on o-. Say the word out loud and the answer appears. This is also the single clearest British–American spelling split in the verb system: American English writes traveled, traveling, canceled, canceling, labeled, modeling, quarreled, and British English doubles the l in all of them. Both are correct in their own dialect; be consistent within a document.

Three verbs spell the third person normally and pronounce it oddly: say → says /sɛz/, do → does /dʌz/, and have → has /hæz/, where the stem vowel changes and the spelling does not warn you.

Lie has two verbs hiding in it. Lie, lay, lain means recline and takes no object; lie, lied, lied means tell an untruth; lay, laid, laid means put something down and takes an object. All three share the -ing rule above — lying, lying, laying — which is where the confusion usually starts.

Pronunciation of the -s and -ed endings#

Both endings have three pronunciations, both are chosen by the sound immediately before the ending, and in both cases the spelling tells you nothing. The rule is the same rule twice: the ending assimilates to the voicing of the sound before it, unless that sound is too similar to the ending, in which case a vowel is inserted to keep them apart.

The -s ending — /s/, /z/, or /ɪz/#

/s/a voiceless sound: /p, t, k, f, θ/works, stops, writes, hits, eats, sleeps, wants, cooks, meets, thinks, walks, picks, helps, keeps, laughs, hopes, sits, puts, lets, drops
/z/a voiced sound: /b, d, ɡ, v, ð, m, n, ŋ, l, r/ and every vowelruns, reads, plays, comes, lives, calls, sees, goes, knows, loves, opens, brings, wears, feels, drives, says, gives, stays, tells, means

The third row is the only one worth memorizing, because it is the only one that adds a syllable: watch is one syllable and watches is two. The first two rows are automatic once you stop thinking about them — your mouth cannot voice a /z/ straight after a /k/ without effort. The identical rule governs plural nouns (cats /s/, dogs /z/, buses /ɪz/) and the possessive -'s, which is why one rule learned here pays three times.

Two American details. First, a handful of -ed words are adjectives with their own /ɪd/ pronunciation, against the rule: a learned professor /ˈlɜrnɪd/, blessed /ˈblɛsɪd/, beloved, naked, wicked, crooked, rugged, jagged, dogged. As verbs they follow the table — he learned /lɜrnd/, she blessed /blɛst/. Second, used to is pronounced /ˈjust tə/ with a /t/, not /juzd/, and the same goes for supposed to /səˈpoʊst tə/ — which is why both are so often misspelled ✗ use to and ✗ suppose to. The full sound inventory, with audio, is in the pronunciation guide.

Time expressions and the tenses they allow#

The fastest way to pick a tense is to read the time words first. They are rarely ambiguous, and several of them permit exactly one tense — which means that getting the adverbial right and the verb wrong produces a sentence that is audibly broken rather than merely odd.

The two rows that account for the most errors are ago and since. Both mean a fixed distance in time, and they belong to opposite tenses: ago counts backward from now to a closed point, so it takes the past simple, while since names the open edge of a stretch that is still running, so it takes a perfect. Two years ago and for two years and since 2023 can describe the same period and still take three different verb forms. Russian has one past tense here, which is why ✗ I work here since 2019 survives so long — the reasoning is laid out in Present perfect and Choosing the right tense.

State verbs and the continuous#

The continuous aspect says "this is a line with a middle." A verb that names a state rather than an activity has no middle to stand in: you do not gradually know something. Those verbs normally refuse the continuous, and the refusal is not stylistic — ✗ I am knowing her is ungrammatical, not merely unusual.

Two currents pull against the rule and both are worth knowing. Informal American English has started using the continuous with a few emotion verbs for a deliberately temporary, engaged effect — I'm loving this album, I'm not hating the new layout — and the construction is ordinary in speech and advertising while still being out of place in formal writing. And a stative verb in the present perfect continuous is usually a mistake for the present perfect: write I've known her for ten years, not ✗ I've been knowing her. The full argument is in Present continuous and Present perfect continuous.

lie / laylay / laidlain / laid✗ I was laying downI lay down · I laid the book down

The sing–sang–sung family works exactly like begin and drink, and once one of them is secure the rest come free: ring–rang–rung, swim–swam–swum, sink–sank–sunk, shrink–shrank–shrunk, spring–sprang–sprung. Two more traps that are spelling rather than morphology: choose /tʃuz/ becomes chose /tʃoʊz/ and chosen, with one o in the past; and lead /lid/ becomes led /lɛd/, which is why ✗ he lead the team last year is so common in writing and invisible in speech.

Quick reference#

Choose a tense in three questions.

  1. When? Past, present, or future — and if the answer is "a past point I can name," you are almost certainly in the past simple.
  2. Finished, in progress, or looking back? Finished and whole → simple. In progress, temporary, with a middle → continuous. Seen from a later point, with the result still mattering → perfect.
  3. Does anything measure the length? For two hours, since noon, all day, how long → add the continuous to the perfect.

The four shapes, in one block.

  SIMPLE              the event as a whole      she worked
  CONTINUOUS          the event as a line       she was working
  PERFECT             the event from later      she had worked
  PERFECT CONTINUOUS  the line, from later      she had been working

  put PAST / PRESENT / WILL in front of the auxiliary  ->  12 forms

The five rules most worth carrying.

RuleExample
A named finished time forces the past simpleI saw him yesterday, not ✗ I've seen him yesterday
Since and for + still true → a perfectI've lived here since March
No will in a time clause or an if-clausewhen you get in, not ✗ when you will get in
State verbs take the simpleI know, I want, I have (own) — not the continuous
Two past events, unequal ages → past perfect on the older oneThe train had left before we got there

Answers#

Each answer gives the form and the reason it is that form. Where two answers are both standard American English, the alternative is given too.

A. Put the verb in the right tense#

  1. boils — Present simple for a permanent fact. Nothing about a physical law is in progress.
  2. 'm trying — Present continuous: an activity happening at this second, which is exactly why the speaker wants quiet.
  3. have known — Present perfect: since marks an open stretch reaching now. Know is a state verb, so the continuous is blocked — ✗ I've been knowing.
  4. 've been fixing — Present perfect continuous: the filthy hands are the visible residue of a stretch of activity, and all afternoon measures it.
  5. moved — Past simple: in 2019 is a closed time, and a named closed time always wins.
  6. was cooking — Past continuous: the longer background action that the blackout interrupted. While is the giveaway.
  7. had already left — Past perfect: two past events, and the leaving is the older one. Already sits between had and the participle.
  8. had been driving — Past perfect continuous: nine hours measured up to a past point, and the exhaustion is the result.
  9. 'll carry — Future will: an offer made at the moment of speaking. I'm going to carry would claim you had planned it.
  10. 'll be flying — Future continuous: this time on Friday asks for the middle of an event, not its start or end.
  11. will have finished — Future perfect: by December is a deadline, and by + a future point is its standard signal.
  12. will have been teaching — Future perfect continuous: a span of forty years counted up to a future moment.
  13. leaves — Present simple for a timetable. Trains and flights take the present simple even for tomorrow.
  14. 's snowing — Present continuous: visible, in progress, temporary.
  15. Have … eaten — Present perfect for an experience at no stated time. Ever permits nothing else.
  16. have … been waiting — Present perfect continuous: how long asks for the length of a stretch touching now.
  17. wrote — Past simple. Shakespeare's life is over, so no stretch can reach the present. Compare Margaret Atwood has written more than fifty books.
  18. were … doing — Past continuous: at eight o'clock last night is a point inside the activity.
  19. had never seen — Past perfect: before that trip supplies the later past vantage point.
  20. had been working — Past perfect continuous: a decade measured up to the moment she went back to school.
  21. won't tell — Will for a promise. I'm not going to tell is also possible and sounds more like a standing intention.
  22. 'll be eating — Future continuous: you are declining in advance because seven falls inside the meal. I'll eat would mean you start at seven.
  23. 've lived / 've been living — Both are standard: since March takes a perfect, and with a long-term situation like living somewhere the two aspects are near-synonyms. The continuous leans slightly more temporary.
  24. arrived — Past simple: yesterday afternoon is finished time.
  25. takes — Present simple: a habit, marked by every morning.
  26. is knocking — Present continuous: audible right now.
  27. haven't finished — Present perfect: yet attaches to a perfect in negatives and questions.
  28. broke — Past simple: last winter is closed. The limp is present but the sentence names the time of the break, and a named time is decisive.
  29. will have left — Future perfect: the leaving is over before the reading, which is itself in the future.
  30. had been arguing — Past perfect continuous: an hour of activity up to the past moment of walking in.

B. Choose the form that fits#

  1. have seen — No time is named, and the experience counts toward the present. This is the classic experiential perfect.
  2. saw — Last Tuesday is a closed time, which forces the past simple whatever the relevance.
  3. lived — From 2010 to 2015 closes the stretch at both ends, so it is finished and the perfect is impossible.
  4. has lived — Since 2010 leaves the stretch open at the present end.
  5. Have you ever been — Ever requires a perfect. Been rather than gone, because the question is about the experience, not about where the person is now.
  6. won — Her life is over, so nothing can reach the present. Tense choice here is a biographical fact, not a grammatical one.
  7. think — Stative think means "hold the opinion" and takes no continuous.
  8. 'm thinking — Dynamic think means "turn it over," which is an activity with a middle.
  9. tastes — Stative: a property of the soup.
  10. is tasting — Dynamic: a deliberate act the chef is performing.
  11. are staying — Present continuous for a temporary situation with an end date. Stay would make it their permanent address.
  12. has — Possession is a state, and family relations are the least dynamic possession there is.
  13. had started — The concert began before the arrival, so the older event takes the past perfect.
  14. ate … left — Two past events told in the order they happened and joined by and then, so the sequence is already clear and the past simple is enough. The past perfect would be redundant.
  15. hadn't visited — Thirty years of not visiting, measured up to the past moment of not recognizing the place.
  16. had stolen — The theft came before the arrest, and the past perfect keeps the order straight across the relative clause.
  17. will get — A decision made as the doorbell rings. Going to would claim you planned to answer it.
  18. is going to — A prediction resting on evidence visible right now, which is precisely what going to is for.
  19. are going to — The paint was bought yesterday, so the intention predates the sentence.
  20. will — An opinion with no present evidence behind it. Going to is also acceptable here; will is the neutral choice.

C. Make it negative or make it a question#

  1. She doesn't work downtown. — The present simple has no auxiliary, so do is added and carries the tense; the main verb loses its -s.
  2. They haven't finished the audit. — The perfect already contains have, so not simply follows it.
  3. He wasn't sleeping when I called. — Not after was; nothing else moves.
  4. The kids hadn't eaten yet. — Already becomes yet in the negative. ✗ hadn't already eaten is not how English says it.
  5. I won't be home by six. — Will not contracts irregularly to won't.
  6. The bus doesn't leave at noon. — Present simple again, so do again.
  7. Have you seen the report? — Have moves in front of the subject. No do — there is already an auxiliary.
  8. How long has she been waiting? — The wh-phrase comes first, then the inverted auxiliary, then the rest of the verb phrase untouched.
  9. Did they go to Boston last weekend? — The past simple borrows do, and went returns to the base go, because only one form can carry the tense.
  10. Will he have finished by Friday? — Only the first auxiliary moves: will inverts, have stays.
  11. Who broke the window? — A question about the subject does not invert and takes no do. ✗ Who did break the window? is grammatical only as an emphatic retort.
  12. What was she reading? — A question about the object does invert, which is exactly the contrast with item 11.

D. Turn the active into the passive#

  1. The plan was approved by the committee. — Past simple passive: was + participle. The agent is worth keeping because the committee is informative.
  2. Ninth Street is being repaved. — Present continuous passive: is being + participle. The agent (they) is vague and gets dropped.
  3. My bike has been stolen. — Present perfect passive. Someone is exactly the kind of agent the passive exists to delete.
  4. The results will be announced on Monday. — Future passive: will be + participle, never ✗ will been.
  5. These houses are built by a local firm. — Present simple passive; the agent carries the news, so it stays.
  6. The tickets had already been sold. — Past perfect passive: had been + participle, with already before been.
  7. The brakes were being fixed when I arrived. — Past continuous passive: were being + participle.
  8. By June the survey will have been completed. — Future perfect passive: will have been + participle, the longest chain the passive uses in practice.
  9. I was given this ring by my grandmother. — A two-object verb has two passives, and American English prefers the one that promotes the person. This ring was given to me by my grandmother is correct too and puts the focus on the ring.
  10. He is said to live abroad. — The reporting passive. It is said that he lives abroad is the other standard version; both delete people, which is the point of the construction.

E. Spelling and the sound of the endings#

  1. stopping, writing, traveling, dying, panicking — One-syllable C-V-C doubles; silent e drops; the unstressed second syllable does not double in American English; -ie becomes -ying; a final -c takes a k.
  2. beginning, seeing, carrying, preferring, opening — Begin and prefer are stressed on the last syllable, so they double; see keeps its e; consonant + -y keeps the y before -ing; open is stressed on the first syllable and does not double.
  3. watches, studies, plays, goes, fixes — -ch and -x take -es; consonant + -y becomes -ies; vowel + -y just adds -s; final -o takes -es.
  4. passes, tries, buys, does, says — -s takes -es; the last two are the irregular pronunciations, does /dʌz/ and says /sɛz/, with perfectly regular spelling.
  5. carried, stopped, preferred, traveled, hoped — Consonant + -y becomes -ied; C-V-C doubles; final stress doubles; first-syllable stress does not (AmE); silent e adds only -d.
  6. planned, enjoyed, worried, visited, offered — Plan doubles; enjoy is vowel + y and does not change; worry is consonant + y; visit and offer are stressed on the first syllable, so no doubling.
  7. works /s/, plays /z/, watches /ɪz/, stops /s/, uses /ɪz/ — /k/ and /p/ are voiceless; /eɪ/ is a vowel and therefore voiced; /tʃ/ and /z/ are sibilants and add a syllable.
  8. runs /z/, laughs /s/, changes /ɪz/, writes /s/, opens /z/ — Laughs ends in the sound /f/ despite the gh spelling, which is the whole point: the sound decides, not the letters.
  9. worked /t/, played /d/, wanted /ɪd/, washed /t/, needed /ɪd/ — /k/ and /ʃ/ are voiceless; /eɪ/ is voiced; /t/ and /d/ force the extra syllable.
  10. decided /ɪd/, cleaned /d/, stopped /t/, visited /ɪd/, laughed /t/ — Only the /ɪd/ words gain a syllable: stopped and laughed are one syllable each, visited is three.

F. Find and fix the error#

  1. I have known her since 2015. — Two errors in one: know is a state verb and takes no continuous, and since requires a perfect rather than a present.
  2. I have lived here for three years. — Since names a point (since 2022); a length of time takes for. And an open stretch takes the present perfect, not the present simple — the direct import of Russian я живу здесь три года.
  3. Yesterday I went to the dentist. — A named finished time blocks the present perfect absolutely.
  4. When I was a child, I played the violin every day. — A repeated past habit is the past simple. The past continuous would describe one stretch of playing, not a routine.
  5. She has been working here for ten years. — For + a stretch reaching now requires a perfect; the present continuous can only mean "at this moment."
  6. Have you finished the report? — The perfect already has an auxiliary, so do has no work to do. ✗ Did you have finished stacks two tense markers.
  7. I didn't see him at the party. — Did carries the tense, so the main verb goes back to the base form.
  8. He doesn't work on Fridays. — Third-person -s moves onto the auxiliary: does not, not do not.
  9. I'll tell you when I know something. — No will in a future time clause. The main clause keeps its will; the when-clause takes the present simple.
  10. By the time we arrived, the movie had already started. — By the time + a past event puts the other verb in the past perfect, because the starting is the older of the two events.
  11. The house was built in 1920. — The passive is be + past participle, and build is irregular: built. The date also fixes the tense as past.
  12. I have gone to the store already. — The perfect takes the participle gone, not the past form went.
  13. She has taken the last bus. — Same error with a different verb: the participle is taken, never took.
  14. He has two brothers. — Have meaning "possess" is stative and refuses the continuous. He's having would mean he is experiencing something.
  15. What are you doing right now? — A wh-question about the object inverts the subject and the auxiliary. Only a question about the subject keeps statement order.
