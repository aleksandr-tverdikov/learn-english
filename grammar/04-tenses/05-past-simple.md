# The past simple

> The form and the one rule that generates most of its errors, the spelling and the sound of *-ed*, the irregular verbs and their traps, everything the tense is actually used for, and the places a Russian speaker's instincts push it wrong.

[← Tenses](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

The past simple is what English uses for something that is **over**.

> I **called** her last night.  
> We **moved** to Denver in 2019.  
> He **didn't say** anything.  
> **Did** you **see** the email?

That is the whole meaning, and it is worth stating plainly, because the past simple is almost always taught against the present perfect — which leaves learners thinking it means "finished, with no connection to now." It does not. The past simple is the **default** past tense of English: the one that carries narrative, that reports what happened, that a speaker reaches for unless something specific pushes them elsewhere. The present perfect is the marked choice; the past simple is the unmarked one, and in doubt it is the right guess.

Two properties fall out of "it is over." **The event is complete** — it began and ended, and nothing is left running. And **the event is located** — at a point or across a stretch of past time, either stated (*last night*, *in 2019*) or recoverable from context. The second does most of the work: **a stated finished time and the past simple go together and cannot be separated**, and that one fact settles most past simple versus present perfect questions a learner will ever face.

One warning before the form. The past-tense *form* and past *time* are not the same thing. English borrows the past simple to mark distance of other kinds — unreality (*If I had more time*) and social distance (*I wondered if you could…*) — and neither is in the past. Both get sections at the end, because a learner who has not been told about them reads them as tense errors.

## The form

### Affirmative

**One form for every person.** Apart from the modals, the past simple is the only tense in English where the verb does not change for the subject at all: no third-person *-s*, no agreement of any kind. *I / you / he / she / it / we / they* — ***worked***, ***went***, ***put***. The single exception is *be*, which keeps *was* and *were* and has [its own section](#be-in-the-past).

> She **worked** there for six years.  
> They **went** home early.  
> I **put** the keys on the counter.

### Negative: *didn't* + the base form

The negative is *did* + *not* plus — this is the rule the whole tense turns on — **the main verb back in its base form**.

> I **didn't call** her. — not ✗ *I didn't called her*  
> He **didn't go**. — not ✗ *He didn't went*  
> We **didn't know**. — not ✗ *We didn't knew*

**Why:** English marks tense **once per verb phrase**, on the first word. *Did* is the past tense of *do*, so the past is already marked; putting it on the lexical verb as well marks it twice, and English rejects double marking absolutely. Same constraint as *He doesn't work*, never ✗ *He doesn't works*.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| ✗ *I didn't **went**.* | *I didn't **go**.* |
| ✗ *She didn't **saw** it.* | *She didn't **see** it.* |
| ✗ *He didn't **had** time.* | *He didn't **have** time.* |
| ✗ *I not **saw** him.* | *I **didn't see** him.* |

The full *did not* exists for emphasis and formal writing — *I did not authorize that* — and nowhere else.

### Questions: *did* + subject + the base form

Same rule, same reason. The auxiliary carries the tense; the verb is bare.

> **Did** you **see** the email?  
> **What did** he **say**?  
> **Where did** they **go**?  
> **How long did** it **take**?

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| ✗ *Did you **saw** it?* | *Did you **see** it?* |
| ✗ *What he **said**?* | *What **did** he **say**?* |
| ✗ *Where **you went**?* | *Where **did you go**?* |
| ✗ *When **have** you **arrived**?* | *When **did** you **arrive**?* |

### The questions that take no *did*

One systematic exception, and it catches everybody. **When the *wh*-word is the subject, there is no auxiliary and no inversion.** The word order is a statement's, and the past tense sits on the lexical verb.

> **Who called?** — not ✗ *Who did call?*  
> **What happened?** — not ✗ *What did happen?*  
> **How many people came?**

| The *wh*-word is… | Question | The answer it expects |
|---|---|---|
| the **subject** | *Who **called** Dana?* | *Marcus called her.* |
| the **object** | *Who **did** Dana **call**?* | *She called Marcus.* |
| the **subject** | *What **broke** the window?* | *A branch did.* |
| the **object** | *What **did** he **break**?* | *He broke a window.* |

The test is to answer the question. If the answer replaces the subject, drop *did*; if it replaces something after the verb, keep it. *Who did call?* is not ungrammatical — it is the **emphatic** form, insisting that somebody did: *"Nobody called." "Somebody did call — I have the voicemail."*

### Negative questions and short answers

Negative questions are formed on the auxiliary and contracted by default; the uncontracted *Did you **not** receive it?* is markedly formal and slightly cold.

> **Didn't** you **get** my message?  
> **Why didn't** she **say** something?

A past-simple negative question carries a **strong expectation** or surprise. *Didn't you lock the door?* is not neutral; it means the speaker assumed you did. And the answer reports the **fact**, not the form of the question — English has nothing like the Russian «нет» that agrees with a negative:

> "**Didn't** you call him?" — "**No**, I didn't." (= I didn't call)  
> "**Didn't** you call him?" — "**Yes**, I did." (= I did call)

In short answers *did* stands in for the whole verb phrase, and after a *be* question the answer uses *was* or *were*, because *be* never borrows *do*.

| Question | Affirmative | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| *Did you see it?* &middot; *Did she call?* | *Yes, I **did**. / Yes, she **did**.* | *No, I **didn't**. / No, she **didn't**.* |
| *Was he there?* &middot; *Were they late?* | *Yes, he **was**. / Yes, they **were**.* | *No, he **wasn't**. / No, they **weren't**.* |

✗ *Yes, I did saw it.* ✗ *Yes, I saw.* ✗ *Yes, I was see it.*

### Emphatic *did*, and the contractions

*Did* also appears in a positive statement, stressed, to contradict or insist — and the main verb still stays bare: *I **did** call you, twice.* *She **did** apologize, actually.* In writing, italics do the same job.

The tense contracts less than the present, because *did* has no reduced positive form: there is no ✗ *I'd see* meaning *I did see*.

| Full | Contracted |
|---|---|
| *did not* | ***didn't*** — used everywhere but formal writing and emphasis |
| *was not* / *were not* | ***wasn't*** / ***weren't*** |
| *there was not* / *there were not* | ***there wasn't*** / ***there weren't*** |
| *could not* / *would not* | ***couldn't*** / ***wouldn't*** — the past modals, for completeness |

In fast speech *did you* is /ˈdɪdʒə/ ("Dih-juh eat?") and *didn't you* is /ˈdɪdn̩tʃə/. Neither is written; both are catalogued in [Contractions](../02-contractions/README.md).

### The paradigm in one table

| | Regular — *work* | Irregular — *see* |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *I **worked** late.* | *I **saw** the email.* |
| Negative | *I **didn't work** late.* | *I **didn't see** the email.* |
| Question &middot; negative question | ***Did** / **Didn't** I **work** late?* | ***Did** / **Didn't** I **see** the email?* |
| Emphatic | *I **did** work late.* | *I **did** see the email.* |
| Passive | *It **was finished** on time.* | *It **was seen** by everyone.* |

The pattern to carry away: **the past tense lives on exactly one word.** If *did* is there, that word is *did*. If not, it is the verb.

## Regular verbs: spelling the *-ed*

A regular verb builds its past simple and its past participle the same way, and the rules below cover both, plus the *-ed* adjective.

| # | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Silent *-e* → add **-d** | *hope → hoped*, *live → lived*, *close → closed*, *decide → decided*, *move → moved*, *use → used* |
| 2 | *-ee*, *-ye*, *-oe* → add **-d** | *agree → agreed*, *free → freed*, *dye → dyed*, *tiptoe → tiptoed* |
| 3 | Consonant + *-y* → **-ied** | *carry → carried*, *study → studied*, *try → tried*, *worry → worried*, *apply → applied*, *reply → replied*, *deny → denied* |
| 4 | Vowel + *-y* → just **-ed** | *play → played*, *stay → stayed*, *enjoy → enjoyed*, *destroy → destroyed*, *obey → obeyed* |
| 5 | Stressed consonant–vowel–consonant → **double the consonant** | *stop → stopped*, *plan → planned*, *beg → begged*, *prefer → preferred*, *admit → admitted* |
| 6 | Ends in *-c* → add **-k** | *panic → panicked*, *picnic → picnicked*, *mimic → mimicked*, *traffic → trafficked* |
| 7 | Everything else → **-ed** | *walk → walked*, *open → opened*, *want → wanted*, *watch → watched* |

Rule 4 has three famous exceptions, all of them irregular verbs rather than spelling variants: *pay → **paid***, *say → **said***, *lay → **laid***. Never ✗ *payed*, ✗ *sayed*, ✗ *layed*.

### Doubling: the full statement

Rule 5 is really a rule about **stress**; the one-syllable case is just its trivial version, since a one-syllable word is always stressed. **Double the final consonant when the last syllable is stressed and ends in a single vowel + a single consonant.**

| Verb | Stress | Past |
|---|---|---|
| *stop*, *plan*, *chat*, *beg* | one syllable | *stopped*, *planned*, *chatted*, *begged* |
| *prefer* | pre-**FER** | *preferred* |
| *refer*, *occur*, *admit*, *permit* | re-**FER**, oc-**CUR**, ad-**MIT**… | *referred*, *occurred*, *admitted*, *permitted* |
| *regret*, *control*, *commit* | re-**GRET**, con-**TROL**, com-**MIT** | *regretted*, *controlled*, *committed* |
| *offer* | **OF**-fer | *offered* — no doubling |
| *answer*, *visit*, *happen*, *listen* | first syllable | *answered*, *visited*, *happened*, *listened* |
| *remember*, *develop*, *focus*, *benefit* | stress not final | *remembered*, *developed*, *focused*, *benefited* (British also *focussed*, *benefitted*) |

***Prefer → preferred*** against ***offer → offered*** is the pair to memorize. The letters at the end are identical; only the stress differs, and only the stress matters.

Three conditions block doubling wherever the stress falls: **two vowels** before the final consonant (*rain → rained*, *need → needed*, *look → looked*, *seem → seemed*), **two consonants** at the end (*help → helped*, *work → worked*, *start → started*, *pretend → pretended*), and a final ***w*, *x*, or *y***, which never double (*snow → snowed*, *allow → allowed*, *fix → fixed*, *play → played*).

### *-l*: American *traveled*, British *travelled*

The doubling rule splits by dialect on exactly one letter. **American English applies the stress rule to *-l* like any other consonant; British English doubles the *-l* regardless of stress.**

| Verb | Stress | American | British |
|---|---|---|---|
| *travel*, *cancel*, *label* | first syllable | ***traveled***, ***canceled***, ***labeled*** | *travelled*, *cancelled*, *labelled* |
| *model*, *signal*, *total*, *quarrel*, *fuel* | first syllable | ***modeled***, ***signaled***, ***totaled***, ***quarreled***, ***fueled*** | *modelled*, *signalled*, *totalled*, *quarrelled*, *fuelled* |
| *compel*, *rebel*, *patrol*, *expel* | **final** syllable | ***compelled***, ***rebelled***, ***patrolled***, ***expelled*** | identical — stress is final, so both double |

Two honest footnotes. **First**, *cancelled* appears in American writing often enough that it no longer looks foreign, but *canceled* is what American dictionaries and the AP stylebook give, and it is the form to write. **Second**, the nouns do not follow the verb: it is ***cancellation*** with two *l*'s in both dialects, and ***traveler*** / ***traveling*** with one in American English against British *traveller* / *travelling*.

### *-ed* or *-t*: the other dialect split

A handful of verbs have both a regular *-ed* past and an older *-t* past. American English prefers ***-ed*** in nearly every case; British English prefers *-t*.

> **American:** *learned, burned, dreamed, spelled, spilled, spoiled, smelled, leaped, leaned*  
> **British:** *learnt, burnt, dreamt, spelt, spilt, spoilt, smelt, leapt, leant*

*Kneel* is the one where both American forms are alive: *kneeled* and *knelt*. The *-t* forms are not wrong in American English — *dreamt* and *leapt* survive in poetry — but *-ed* is the default. Some *-t* forms have split off as adjectives and are standard in that role only: *burnt toast*, *spilt milk* in the proverb.

## Regular verbs: pronouncing the *-ed*

The spelling is one letter sequence. The sound is three, and the choice is automatic: it depends on nothing but the sound immediately before the ending. Not the letter — the **sound**.

| *-ed* is said | After | Syllable added? |
|---|---|---|
| **/ɪd/** | /t/ or /d/ — and nothing else | **Yes** |
| **/t/** | any other **voiceless** sound: /p, k, f, s, ʃ, tʃ, θ/ | No |
| **/d/** | any other **voiced** sound, including every vowel | No |

Check it with two fingers on your throat. If the sound before *-ed* buzzes, the ending buzzes too — /d/; if not, /t/. And if that sound is already /t/ or /d/, you cannot say another without a vowel in between, so English inserts one. The ending chooses nothing; it copies the voicing of its neighbor, and /ɪd/ exists only because /t/+/t/ and /d/+/d/ are unpronounceable as clusters.

| Sound | Word list |
|---|---|
| **/ɪd/** — one syllable longer | *wanted, needed, started, decided, visited, waited, ended, added, landed, invited, painted, counted, hated, rented, expected, attended, suggested, collected, recorded, avoided, insisted, reported* |
| **/t/** | /p/ *stopped, helped, jumped, hoped, dropped* &middot; /k/ *looked, worked, walked, talked, asked, liked* &middot; /f/ *laughed, coughed, sniffed* &middot; /s/ *kissed, passed, missed, danced, promised, crossed* &middot; /ʃ/ *washed, finished, pushed, published* &middot; /tʃ/ *watched, reached, searched, touched* |
| **/d/** | /b/ *robbed, grabbed* &middot; /g/ *begged, hugged* &middot; /v/ *moved, lived, arrived, loved, believed* &middot; /z/ *closed, used, raised, surprised, refused* &middot; /dʒ/ *changed, managed, judged* &middot; /m/ *seemed, claimed, named* &middot; /n/ *opened, cleaned, listened, happened, rained, joined* &middot; /l/ *called, filled, traveled, smiled* &middot; /r/ *offered, answered, remembered, covered* &middot; vowels *played, stayed, enjoyed, agreed, tried, studied, followed, allowed* |

### The traps

**The silent *-e* is still silent.** *Hoped* is /hoʊpt/, one syllable — not ✗ /ˈhoʊpɪd/. The *e* in the spelling belongs to *hope*, not to the ending. Same for *liked* /laɪkt/, *closed* /kloʊzd/, *used* /juzd/.

**Spelling lies about voicing.** The letters do not decide; the sound does.

| Word | Looks like | Actually |
|---|---|---|
| *laughed*, *danced*, *fixed* | *-gh-*, *-c-*, *-x-* | /læft/, /dænst/, /fɪkst/ — *gh* is /f/, *c* is /s/, *x* is /ks/, so all take /t/ |
| *used* | *-s-* | /juzd/ — the *s* is /z/, so /d/ |
| *watched* | *-tch-* | /wɑtʃt/ — /tʃ/, not /t/, so **no** extra syllable |
| *judged* | *-dged* | /dʒʌdʒd/ — /dʒ/, not /d/, so **no** extra syllable |

The last two rows are the ones to look at twice: /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ are single sounds, not /t/ and /d/, so they do **not** trigger /ɪd/. ✗ /ˈwɑtʃɪd/ is a common and very audible error.

**The two *used*.** The verb meaning "employed" is /juzd/: *I used your pen.* The habitual *used to* is /ˈjustə/ — voiceless /s/, voiceless /t/ — rhyming with *just a*: *I used to smoke* is "I YOOS-tuh smoke."

### The adjectives that keep /ɪd/

A short list of words ending in *-ed* take a full /ɪd/ syllable **when they are adjectives**, breaking the rule above. Most are leftovers from an older stage of the language, and several are not verbs at all.

| Word | Said | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ***naked*** | /ˈneɪkɪd/ | There is no verb *to nake*. Always /ɪd/. |
| ***wicked*** | /ˈwɪkɪd/ | No verb either. Always /ɪd/. |
| ***rugged***, ***ragged***, ***jagged***, ***wretched*** | /ˈrʌgɪd/, /ˈrægɪd/, /ˈdʒægɪd/, /ˈrɛtʃɪd/ | Always /ɪd/. |
| ***aged*** | /ˈeɪdʒɪd/ **or** /eɪdʒd/ | *An **aged** man* /ˈeɪdʒɪd/; but *He **aged** ten years* and *a boy **aged** nine* are /eɪdʒd/. |
| ***beloved*** | /bɪˈlʌvɪd/ **or** /bɪˈlʌvd/ | *Her **beloved** dog* /bɪˈlʌvɪd/; *beloved by all* is often /bɪˈlʌvd/. |
| ***blessed*** | /ˈblɛsɪd/ **or** /blɛst/ | *The **blessed** saints* /ˈblɛsɪd/; *The priest **blessed** them* /blɛst/. |
| ***learned*** | /ˈlɝnɪd/ **or** /lɝnd/ | *A **learned** professor* /ˈlɝnɪd/; *I **learned** it yesterday* /lɝnd/. |
| ***dogged***, ***cursed***, ***crooked*** | /ˈdɔgɪd/, /ˈkɝsɪd/, /ˈkrʊkɪd/ | Adjective /ɪd/; the verbs are /dɔgd/, /kɝst/, /krʊkt/. |
| ***supposed***, ***alleged***, ***-legged*** | /səˈpoʊzɪd/, /əˈlɛdʒɪd/, /ˈlɛgɪd/ | *A **supposed** expert* /səˈpoʊzɪd/ against *I **supposed** so* /səˈpoʊzd/ — and *supposed to* is /səˈpoʊstə/. *Four-**legged***, *long-**legged***. |

The middle rows deserve a name: **where one spelling is both a past-tense verb and an adjective, the verb follows the rule and the adjective takes /ɪd/** — *He blessed them* /blɛst/ against *the blessed saints* /ˈblɛsɪd/. A past-simple verb never takes the adjective pronunciation. The endings in full are in [Pronunciation](../../10-pronunciation/README.md#the--ed-and--s-endings).

## Irregular verbs

An irregular verb has a past simple that is not made with *-ed*. That is the only difference; everything in [The form](#the-form) applies unchanged.

> He **went** home. → He **didn't go** home. → **Did** he **go** home?  
> I **saw** it. → I **didn't see** it. → **Did** you **see** it?

This is the practical payoff of *do*-support: **an irregular verb is only irregular in the affirmative.** The moment a negative or a question appears, *did* takes over and the irregular form vanishes. A learner unsure whether it is *brought* or ✗ *brang* can sidestep the problem entirely in a question — *Did you bring it?*

### The second form, not the third

Every irregular verb has three principal parts, and the past simple takes the **second**. The past simple stands alone; the past participle needs an auxiliary (*have*, *has*, *had*, or a form of *be*).

```
   base      past simple     past participle
   go        went            gone
   see       saw             seen
   write     wrote           written
   drink     drank           drunk
```

Swapping them is one of the most recognizable errors in English, and it runs both ways:

| Wrong | Right | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **seen** him yesterday.* | *I **saw** him yesterday.* | participle used as a past simple |
| ✗ *She **done** it.* ✗ *He **drunk** all of it.* | *She **did** it.* *He **drank** all of it.* | same |
| ✗ *I have **went** home.* | *I have **gone** home.* | past simple used as a participle |
| ✗ *It was **wrote**.* ✗ *He had **took** it.* | *It was **written**.* *He had **taken** it.* | same, in a passive and a perfect |

The first three are widespread in some registers of spoken American English. They are still not written, and they are heard.

Group 1 is the one to look at first, because its members have **no visible past tense at all**: *cut, put, hit, let, set, cost, shut, hurt, quit, spread, cast, burst, bet, split*. In *Yesterday I put it on your desk*, nothing in the verb says "past" — the time expression carries the whole load, and learners repair the apparent gap with ✗ *putted*, ✗ *costed*, ✗ *hitted*.

### *read* and *read*

The spelling never changes; the pronunciation does. Base *read* /rid/, like *reed*. Past simple and participle *read* /rɛd/, like the color *red*.

> I **read** /rid/ the news every morning.  
> I **read** /rɛd/ it yesterday.  
> I **have read** /rɛd/ it twice.

Only context or the speaker's voice separates them; there is no written signal at all, and every prefixed form does the same — *reread*, *misread*, *proofread*. Two consequences: in **reading aloud**, the tense must be decided before the word is spoken, and getting it wrong is audible; in **listening**, /rɛd/ is often the only past marker in the clause. Full entry: [read](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/01-all-three-same.md#read).

### *lie*, *lay*, and the other *lie*

Three verbs, two of which share a form. This is the most-failed distinction in English, and native speakers fail it constantly.

| Verb | Meaning | Forms | Object? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***lie*** | recline, be situated | *lie — **lay** — lain* | **no** |
| ***lay*** | put something down | *lay — **laid** — laid* | **yes** |
| ***lie*** | tell an untruth | *lie — **lied** — lied* — regular | no |

The collision: **the past simple of *lie* is spelled *lay***, which is also the base form of the other verb.

> I **lay** down for a nap. — past of *lie*; correct  
> ✗ *I **laid** down for a nap.* — everywhere, still wrong  
> He **laid** his keys on the counter. — past of *lay*, with an object  
> She **lied** about the mileage. — the regular verb

**The test is the object.** If something is being put somewhere, it is *lay / laid*; if a subject is reclining or a thing is situated, it is *lie / lay / lain*. ✗ *Layed* is not a word in any of the three. Entries: [lie](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#lie), [lay](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/07-plus-d.md#lay).

### *hung* or *hanged*

One verb, two past forms, and the difference is not stylistic. ***Hung*** covers everything — objects, pictures, clothes, phone calls, smoke. ***Hanged*** is for execution by hanging, and nothing else.

> We **hung** the wreath. She **hung** up before I finished.  
> The state **hanged** three men in 1892.  
> ✗ *The painting **was hanged** over the fireplace.* — this puts a rope around a canvas.

See [hang](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#hang).

### A few more collisions

| Pair | Forms | The difference |
|---|---|---|
| *rise* / *raise* &middot; *sit* / *set* | *rose, risen* / *raised* &middot; *sat, sat* / *set, set* | The first of each pair takes no object (*the sun rose*), the second takes one (*they raised the rent*). *Set* never changes form. |
| *fall* / *feel* | *fell, fallen* / *felt, felt* | Two verbs learners merge. *I fell* ≠ *I felt*. |
| *find* / *found* | *found, found* / *founded, founded* | *Find → found*; but the verb *found* ("establish") is regular: *founded in 1908*. |
| *wind* / *lead* | *wound, wound* / *led, led* | *He wound the clock* /waʊnd/, not *wounded*; and *lead → led*, never ✗ *lead*, though the metal *lead* is /lɛd/. |

American English also has past forms British English lacks: ***dove*** alongside *dived*, ***snuck*** alongside *sneaked*, and ***fit*** as the past of *fit* (*the jacket fit me*, British *fitted*). All are standard in the United States.

## *Be* in the past

*Be* is the one verb that does not behave like the rest of this section. It has **two past forms**, it makes its own negatives and questions without *do*, and it is the most frequent verb in the language.

| Subject | Form | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| I &middot; he / she / it | *was* | *wasn't* |
| you &middot; we &middot; they | *were* | *weren't* |

> I **was** at home all evening.  
> They **were** late again.  
> She **wasn't** happy about it.

Because *be* is an auxiliary itself, it inverts and negates directly. ✗ *Did you be there?* and ✗ *I didn't be ready* are not English.

> **Was** she there? **Weren't** they supposed to call?  
> **Why was** he so angry? **How many people were** there?

### *there was* and *there were*

The dummy subject *there* agrees with the noun **that follows it**, not with *there*.

> **There was** a problem with the invoice. **There were** three messages waiting.  
> **There wasn't** enough time. **Were there** any questions?

With an uncountable noun, *was*: *There was a lot of traffic.* With a list, the first item usually decides in speech: *There was a couch, two chairs, and a lamp.* And the honest note: ***There was three of us*** and ***there's four people waiting*** are extremely common in American speech, including from educated speakers. They are not written, and an editor will change them.

### Why ✗ *I was go* happens

This error — *was* or *were* in front of a base form — comes from treating *be* as the general past auxiliary the way *did* is. It is not one. **In the past simple, *was* and *were* are exactly one of two things:** a **main verb** (*I was tired*, *she was a nurse*), or the **auxiliary of a continuous or a passive** (*I was working*, *it was written*). Never a base form after it.

| Wrong | Right | What it should have been |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **was go** to the store.* | *I **went** to the store.* | a plain past simple |
| ✗ *I **was go** when you called.* | *I **was going** when you called.* | a past continuous — needs *-ing* |
| ✗ *The house **was build** in 1912.* | *The house **was built** in 1912.* | a passive — needs the participle |
| ✗ *Did you **were** there?* | ***Were** you there?* | *be* inverts by itself |
| ✗ *I **didn't was** ready.* | *I **wasn't** ready.* | *be* negates by itself |

## What the past simple is used for

### 1. A completed action at a stated past time

The core use. Something happened, it finished, and the sentence says or implies when.

> I **called** the doctor's office this morning. She **graduated** in 2018.  
> They **bought** the house two years ago. He **lost** his wallet at the game.

The time need not appear in the sentence; it has to be **recoverable** — from an earlier sentence, from the situation, or from shared knowledge. *Who broke this?* has no time expression and is unmistakably about a specific past occasion, because the broken thing is standing there.

### 2. A sequence of finished events

Put past simples end to end and English reads them **in the order they are written**. This is the engine of every narrative in the language, and it needs no time expressions after the first.

> I **got** up, **made** coffee, **checked** my email, and **left** for the office.  
> The alarm **went** off. He **grabbed** his coat, **ran** down three flights, and **hailed** a cab.

This is why the past simple is the tense of news reports, stories, biographies and police statements — anything answering "what happened?" Change one verb to a past continuous and it stops being a step in the sequence and becomes the **background** the other events happen against: *I **was making** coffee when the phone **rang**.* The division of labor is the subject of [the past continuous](06-past-continuous.md).

### 3. Past habits and repeated actions

The past simple covers repeated past actions as readily as single ones. Usually a frequency word makes the repetition explicit, because without one the sentence reads as a single occasion.

> We **went** to the lake **every summer**.  
> He **called** his mother **every Sunday**.  
> I **never ate** breakfast in those days.

Two other forms compete for this job, and each adds something the past simple does not.

#### *used to*

***Used to* + base form** says the habit or state **is over**, and sets up a contrast with the present whether or not the contrast is stated: *I **used to smoke***; *We **used to go** to the lake every summer*; *There **used to be** a diner on this corner.* The form is defective, and its negative and question are where the errors live:

| | Form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | *I **used to** go.* | With *-d*. |
| Negative | *I **didn't use to** go.* &middot; *I **never used to** go.* | **No *-d*** after *did* — ✗ *didn't used to*. The *never* version is more common and avoids the trap. |
| Question | ***Did** you **use to** go?* | No *-d*. Stiff; most speakers ask *Did you go… back then?* |
| Present | — | **There is none.** ✗ *I use to go to the gym* meaning "I go regularly." |

The spelling ✗ *I use to go* for the affirmative is caused by the pronunciation — /ˈjustə/, with the *-d* inaudible — and it is an error, not a variant. And ***used to*** + base is a different construction from ***be used to*** + *-ing* ("be accustomed"): *I used to drive to work* against *I'm used to driving to work*. *Get used to* is the third member.

#### *would*

***Would* + base form** also marks a repeated past action, adding a **reminiscing, narrative** quality. It is the form of memoir and anecdote.

> Every August, we **would drive** up to the cabin.  
> My grandfather **would sit** on the porch and **wave** at every car that passed.

And here is where *would* cannot go — three restrictions, all real:

- **Not with state verbs.** *Would* needs a repeatable action. ✗ *I would have a motorcycle.* ✗ *He would live in Boston.* ✗ *She would know him well.* ✗ *They would own a farm.* Use the past simple or *used to*: *I used to have a motorcycle.*
- **Not for a single event.** ✗ *Last Tuesday I would call her.* *Would* means "repeatedly," so a one-off is contradictory.
- **Not without an established past frame.** A *would* opening a text with nothing before it reads as a conditional or a future-in-the-past, not a habit: ✗ *My grandfather would take me fishing* as a first sentence leaves the reader waiting for the *if*. Set the frame: *When I was small, my grandfather would take me fishing.*

That last restriction is why *used to* is the safer of the two — it carries its own time frame, where *would* borrows one.

#### The three side by side

| | Past simple | *used to* | *would* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated action | *We **went** every summer.* | *We **used to go** every summer.* | *We **would go** every summer.* |
| Past state | *He **had** a motorcycle.* | *He **used to have** a motorcycle.* | ✗ *He **would have** a motorcycle.* |
| Single finished event | *I **met** her in 2015.* | ✗ *I **used to meet** her in 2015.* | ✗ *I **would meet** her in 2015.* |
| What it adds | nothing — the neutral form | it is over; a contrast with now | reminiscence, narrative texture |
| Needs a frequency word? | usually, to read as a habit | no | no |
| Can it open a text? &middot; take a state verb? | yes &middot; yes | yes &middot; yes | **no** — needs an anchor &middot; **no** |

Use the **past simple** unless you want one of the two extra flavors; ***used to*** when the point is that things have changed; ***would*** only inside a narrative whose past frame is already standing, and never with a state.

### 4. Past states, with state verbs

State verbs — *know, have, believe, want, like, love, hate, seem, own, mean, understand, belong, need, remember, cost, contain* — describe situations rather than actions. They take the past simple and do **not** take the past continuous.

> I **knew** him well in college. She **had** two brothers. He **seemed** nervous.  
> They **owned** the building until 2015. It **cost** more than we expected.

✗ *I was knowing him.* ✗ *She was having two brothers.* ✗ *We were wanting to help.*

Several have a second, dynamic sense that does take the continuous, and the meaning shifts with it: *have* = "experience" (*we were having dinner*), *think* = "consider", *see* = "meet" (*she was seeing someone*). The splits are in [the verbs reference](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md#54-stative-verbs-and-the-continuous).

### 5. A finished stretch of time

A past simple with a duration says the stretch **is over**. This is the contrast that carries the most weight against the present perfect.

> I **lived** in Denver **for six years**. — and I don't now  
> I**'ve lived** in Denver for six years. — and I still do

> He **worked** there **from 2010 to 2018**. The meeting **lasted** twenty minutes.

The duration is not what chooses the tense. Whether the period is **closed** is. *For six years* fits both tenses; the past simple closes it and the present perfect leaves it open.

### 6. In time clauses

After *when*, *after*, *before*, *as soon as*, *until*, *once*, *by the time*, and *while*, a past simple main clause takes a past simple in the subordinate clause too.

> **When** the bell **rang**, everyone **stood** up.  
> **As soon as** she **saw** me, she **turned** around.  
> **By the time** we **got** there, it **was** over.

**The conjunction can do the ordering, so the past perfect is often optional.** *After I finished, I left* and *After I had finished, I left* say the same thing, because *after* already orders the events. Where nothing supplies the order, the past perfect earns its keep: *When I arrived, she left* (she left after I got there) against *When I arrived, she **had** left* (she was already gone) — a contrast belonging to [the past perfect](07-past-perfect.md).

And note that a past *when*-clause is a real past, not the tenseless present of a future time clause. *I'll call you when I **get** home* uses a present form for future time; *I called you when I **got** home* uses a genuine past. Nothing transfers between the two patterns.

### 7. In reported speech

A present tense inside someone's words becomes a past simple when the report is framed in the past. That past is **agreement with the reporting verb**, not a claim about past time.

> "I **work** downtown," he said. → He said he **worked** downtown.  
> "I**'m** tired," she said. → She said she **was** tired.

He has not stopped working downtown. The whole mechanism, including where the shift does not happen, is [Backshift: the tense changes](../01-reported-speech/02-backshift.md).

### 8. Polite distancing

The past simple is borrowed to make a request, offer or question **less direct**, and therefore more polite. **Nothing about these sentences is in the past.**

> I **wondered** if you could take a look at this.  
> I **was hoping** you might have a minute.  
> **Did** you **want** to sit down? I just **wanted** to check on the invoice.

Compare *I want to ask you something* with *I wanted to ask you something*: the second is softer, for exactly the reason a second conditional is softer — a step back in tense is a step back from the listener. The scale runs in three stages: *I **wonder** if you could…* → *I **wondered** if you could…* → *I **was wondering** if you could…*, the last being the most polite formula in ordinary American English. Answering one in the past tense — *"Did you want coffee?" "Yes, I wanted coffee"* — misreads it. The answer is *Yes, please.*

### 9. The unreal past

The past simple form is also how English marks a situation as **not real** — in the second conditional, after *wish* and *if only*, after *it's time*, *would rather* and *as if*.

> If I **had** more time, I'd learn Spanish. I wish I **knew** the answer.  
> It's time we **left**. He talks as if he **owned** the place.

None of those is about past time. The tense is measuring **distance from reality**, not distance in time, and it is a separate system that happens to reuse the same forms. It is not explained here: it is [Conditionals](../03-conditionals/README.md) — [the four types](../03-conditionals/01-the-four-types.md) for the second conditional, [wishes and regrets](../03-conditionals/05-wishes-and-regrets.md) for the *wish* family. The one thing to carry across: **a past-tense form does not guarantee past time**, and a learner who assumes it does will misread every *wish* sentence in English.

## Time expressions

| Type | Expressions |
|---|---|
| **Yesterday and its parts** | *yesterday*, *yesterday morning / afternoon / evening*, *the day before yesterday* |
| **Last, ago** | *last night*, *last week / month / year*, *last Tuesday*, *two days ago*, *an hour ago*, *long ago* |
| **In / on / at** | *in 2019*, *in March*, *in the nineties*, *in college*, *on Monday*, *at six o'clock*, *at the time* |
| **Clauses and vague** | *when I was a child*, *before the merger*, *the other day*, *back then*, *in those days*, *once*, *just now* |
| **Spans** | *from 2010 to 2018*, *for six years* (closed), *all day yesterday*, *during the war* |

> I saw her **the other day**. He left **two days ago**.  
> **Back then** nobody locked their doors.  
> **When I was a child**, we spent every August on the coast.

### A stated finished time forces the past simple

This is the rule, and it has no exceptions worth listing: **if the sentence names a past time that is over, the verb is the past simple, and the present perfect is not available.**

> ✗ *I **have seen** him **yesterday**.* → *I **saw** him yesterday.*  
> ✗ *She **has called** me **last night**.* → *She **called** me last night.*  
> ✗ *They **have moved** here **in 2019**.* → *They **moved** here in 2019.*  
> ✗ *I **have finished** it **two hours ago**.* → *I **finished** it two hours ago.*

The reason is a genuine incompatibility, not an arbitrary rule. The present perfect connects an event to **now** and refuses to locate it anywhere else; *yesterday* does nothing but locate it. The two cancel out — which is why the present perfect works with *today*, *this week* and *recently*, periods that include now, and fails with *yesterday*, which does not.

Read backwards: **a *when* question about a past event is always past simple**, because *when* asks for exactly the location the present perfect refuses to give. ✗ *When **have** you **arrived**?* → *When **did** you **arrive**?* The contrast in full, from the present perfect's side, is [section 3](03-present-perfect.md) and [section 10](10-choosing-the-right-tense.md).

### The expressions that can go either way

Some periods contain the present moment or not, depending on when you are speaking, and the tense follows.

| Expression | Past simple | Present perfect |
|---|---|---|
| *this morning* | *I called him this morning.* — said in the afternoon | *I've called him this morning.* — said at 10 a.m. |
| *today* | *Did you eat today?* — said at night | *Have you eaten today?* — said midday |
| *this week* | *We didn't hit the target this week.* — Friday evening | *We haven't hit the target this week.* — Wednesday |
| *just*, *already*, *yet* | *I just saw her.* *Did you eat yet?* *I already ate.* | *I've just seen her.* *Have you eaten yet?* |

The last row is an American–British split rather than a difference of meaning. **American English uses the past simple with *just*, *already* and *yet* freely**, where British English wants the present perfect, and both are standard in the United States — the one place where a learner taught from British materials gets corrected into an error. *Just now*, though, is fixed to the past simple: *I saw her just now*, never ✗ *I've seen her just now*.

## The passive

The past simple passive is ***was* / *were* + the past participle***.

| | Form |
|---|---|
| Affirmative | *The house **was built** in 1912.* &middot; *It **was written** by Dana.* |
| Negative | *The windows **weren't cleaned**.* |
| Question | ***Was** the report **filed**?* &middot; ***Wasn't** she **told**?* |
| Continuous | *The room **was being painted**.* |

> Two hundred workers **were laid off** in March.  
> The meeting **was canceled** at the last minute. **Were** you **given** a receipt?

**The one error to watch is the participle.** The passive needs the **third** form, never the second and never the base: ✗ *was wrote* → *was **written***; ✗ *was took* → *was **taken***; ✗ *was broke* → *was **broken***; ✗ *was build* → *was **built***.

American English also uses a **get-passive** — informal, common, and usually carrying a note of something happening *to* the subject: *He **got fired** last week.* *My car **got towed**.* Its negative and question take *did*, because *get* is an ordinary verb: *He didn't get fired.* *Did she get promoted?* The passive of all twelve tenses is in [Passive, questions and clauses](11-passive-questions-and-clauses.md).

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I didn't **went**.* ✗ *Did you **saw** it?* | *I didn't **go**. Did you **see** it?* | *Did* carries the tense; the main verb is bare. |
| ✗ *I **have seen** him **yesterday**.* | *I **saw** him yesterday.* | A stated finished time forbids the present perfect. |
| ✗ *When **have** you **arrived**?* | *When **did** you **arrive**?* | *When* asks for a location in past time; the present perfect refuses to give one. |
| ✗ *I **was go** to the store.* | *I **went** to the store.* | *Was* is a main verb or the auxiliary of a continuous or a passive. It never precedes a base form. |
| ✗ *The house was **build** in 1912.* | *The house was **built** in 1912.* | The passive takes the past participle. |
| ✗ *I **seen** him.* ✗ *I have **went** home.* | *I **saw** him. I have **gone** home.* | The past simple is the second form; the perfect takes the third. |
| ✗ *stopped* /ˈstɑpɪd/, ✗ *watched* /ˈwɑtʃɪd/ | /stɑpt/, /wɑtʃt/ | /ɪd/ only after /t/ or /d/. /tʃ/ is not /t/, and a silent *-e* stays silent. |
| ✗ *Who **did call**?* (neutral question) | *Who **called**?* | No *did* when the *wh*-word is the subject. |
| ✗ *He **didn't used to** smoke.* ✗ *I **use to** go.* | *He **didn't use to** smoke. I **used to** go.* | No *-d* after *did*; the *-d* stays in the affirmative, however it sounds. |
| ✗ *I **would have** a motorcycle back then.* | *I **used to have** a motorcycle.* | *Would* does not take state verbs. |
| ✗ *I **laid** down for a nap.* ✗ *The painting was **hanged**.* | *I **lay** down.* *The painting was **hung**.* | *Lay* needs an object; *lie* does not, and its past is *lay*. *Hanged* is for executions only. |
| ✗ *I **travelled**.* ✗ *He **prefered** tea.* | *I **traveled**. He **preferred** tea.* | American English does not double an unstressed *-l*; a stressed final syllable does double. |
| ✗ *She **studyed**.* ✗ *They **payed** me.* | *She **studied**.* *They **paid** me.* | Consonant + *y* → *-ied*; *pay* is irregular. |
| ✗ *There **was** three of us.* (in writing) | *There **were** three of us.* | *There* agrees with the noun that follows it. |
| ✗ *Yes, I **did saw** it.* ✗ *It **costed** $20.* | *Yes, I **did**. It **cost** $20.* | The short answer is the auxiliary alone; and some irregular verbs have no visible past. |

## For Russian speakers

Four interference points, in order of how much damage they do.

### 1. Russian aspect does not map onto the English past tenses

This is the big one, and it is a difference of **system**, not of vocabulary. Russian has **one past tense** and marks the rest with **aspect**: «читал» is imperfective, «прочитал» is perfective, and the pair says whether the action was completed. English has **four** past-referring forms — past simple, past continuous, present perfect, past perfect — and none of them is an aspect in the Russian sense.

The substitution learners make is wrong in both directions: perfective «прочитал» is **not** the present perfect, and imperfective «читал» is **not** the past continuous.

**The English question is not "was the action completed?" It is "is a past time stated, and is the period closed?"** Russian asks about the action; English asks about the time. Make that swap and most of the errors disappear.

| Russian | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| «Вчера я **читал** книгу.» | *Yesterday I **read** a book.* | Imperfective, but the day is over → past simple. *I was reading* is also possible and means something else — it makes the reading a background. |
| «Я **прочитал** эту книгу.» | *I**'ve read** that book.* | Perfective with **no time stated** → the present perfect is available. |
| «Я **прочитал** её вчера.» | *I **read** it yesterday.* | Perfective **with** a stated time → past simple only. |
| «Я **купил** машину вчера.» | *I **bought** a car yesterday.* | ✗ *I have bought a car yesterday* — the classic error, produced straight from the perfective. |
| «Я **работал** там три года.» | *I **worked** there for three years.* | Imperfective, closed period → past simple. Not ✗ *I was working there for three years*. |
| «Я **работаю** там три года.» | *I**'ve worked** there for three years.* | Russian uses the **present** for a stretch still running; English uses the present perfect. Never ✗ *I work there for three years*. |
| «Он **жил** в Бостоне.» | *He **lived*** / ***used to live*** / ***has lived** in Boston.* | One Russian form, three English sentences making three different claims about now. |
| «Он **писал** письмо, когда я **вошёл**.» | *He **was writing** a letter when I **came in**.* | Here the imperfective does match a continuous — one action interrupts another. And «Ты уже **поел**?» is both *Did you eat yet?* and *Have you eaten yet?* in American English. |

The rule of thumb that survives contact with real sentences: **look for a past time expression first.** If one is there and the period is over, stop — it is the past simple, whatever the Russian aspect was.

### 2. The missing auxiliary

Russian negates by putting «не» in front of the verb and asks yes/no questions by intonation alone, with no extra word and no change of order. English inserts an auxiliary that was not in the statement, moves it in front of the subject for a question, and strips the tense off the main verb — **three operations where Russian has zero or one**.

```
   «Ты видел его.»   →   «Ты не видел его.»   →   «Ты видел его?»
   I saw him.        →   I didn't see him.    →   Did you see him?
                         insert did + not,        invert did,
                         verb → base              verb → base
```

| Wrong | Right | What went missing |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **not saw** him.* ✗ *I **no went**.* | *I **didn't see** him. I **didn't go**.* | The auxiliary. «не» was translated directly. |
| ✗ *You **saw** him?* | ***Did** you **see** him?* | The auxiliary and the inversion. |
| ✗ *Where **you went**?* ✗ *Why she **didn't called**?* | *Where **did you go**? Why **didn't** she **call**?* | Both, plus the tense marked twice in the second. |

*You saw him?* is a real English sentence, but it means "I can't believe it — you saw him?" As a neutral question it sounds startled.

### 3. The tense is marked once, and *did* takes it

Russian has nothing that behaves like *do*-support, so there is no instinct for the base form after *did*: the learner's verb stays in the past because in Russian it must. State it as a mechanical rule and it becomes automatic — **the moment *did* appears, the next verb is the dictionary form.** No *-ed*, no irregular past, nothing. ✗ *I didn't **saw** him.* ✗ *Did you **went**?*

There is one Russian-shaped trap in the other direction. «был» looks like an auxiliary — «Я был дома», «Я был занят» — and learners generalize it into ✗ *I was go*, ✗ *I was see*. Russian «был» combines with adjectives and nouns, never with another past verb; English *was* combines with *-ing* forms and participles, never with a base form. See [Why ✗ *I was go* happens](#why--i-was-go-happens).

### 4. The *-ed* ending is a sound problem, not a spelling problem

Two Russian habits collide with the *-ed* rule. **Final devoicing**: Russian devoices consonants at the end of a word — «год» is said /got/ — so *played* comes out as /pleɪt/ and *lived* as /lɪft/. English keeps the voicing, and the whole /t/ versus /d/ rule depends on it. **Inserting a vowel**: because the spelling has an *e*, learners say a syllable that is not there — ✗ /ˈstɑpɪd/ for *stopped*, ✗ /ˈwɔkɪd/ for *walked*. This is the most recognizable pronunciation error in learner English and it is entirely mechanical to fix: **the extra syllable exists only after /t/ and /d/.**

> *wanted* /ˈwɑntɪd/ — two syllables &middot; *walked* /wɔkt/ — one &middot; *called* /kɔld/ — one

There is also no Russian equivalent of the present perfect at all, which is why point 1 is hard: the learner is not choosing between two forms they both understand, but being asked to make a distinction their first language never requires. The remedy is the time-expression test, not translation.

## Quick reference

```
   Where does the past tense go?

   no auxiliary ..... on the verb ............... I saw him.
   did / didn't ..... on the auxiliary, and the   I didn't see him.
                      verb in its BASE form       Did you work late?
   was / were ....... be needs no did, ever ..... Was he there?

   Past simple or present perfect?

   a finished past time is stated → past simple, no choice
   none stated → either; present perfect if it bears on now
```

**The *-ed* sound.** /ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ only — the one case with an extra syllable. Otherwise /t/ after a voiceless sound, /d/ after a voiced one.

**The *-ed* spelling.** Silent *-e* → *-d*; consonant + *y* → *-ied*; a stressed final CVC → double the consonant; everything else → *-ed*. American English does not double an unstressed *-l*.

**The five sentences to remember.**

> **I saw him yesterday.** — a stated past time, so nothing else is possible  
> **I didn't see him.** — *did* has the tense; the verb is bare  
> **Did you see him?** — same, inverted  
> **Who saw him?** — no *did*, because *who* is the subject  
> **I was tired.** — *be* does all of this by itself

## Practice

**A. Form.** Put the verb into the past simple, keeping the sentence type.

1. She (not call) me back.
2. (you / see) the email I sent?
3. What time the meeting (start)?
4. Who (break) this?
5. They (not be) at home.
6. He (not can) find it. *(Careful — this one is not a past simple.)*
7. Where (you / go) after the game?

**B. Spelling.** Write the past simple of: *stop, travel, prefer, offer, carry, play, panic, hope, admit, cancel*.

**C. Pronunciation.** Sort into /t/, /d/, /ɪd/: *watched, wanted, played, laughed, decided, closed, danced, needed, opened, fixed*.

**D. Irregular.**

8. Yesterday I (read) the whole report.
9. She (lie) down for twenty minutes after lunch.
10. He (lay) the baby in the crib.
11. They (hang) the new blinds last week.
12. The convicted man (hang) in 1892.

**E. Use.**

13. I (live) in Denver for six years, but I (move) to Austin in 2019.
14. Every summer we (go) to the lake. *(Rewrite twice: once with* used to*, once with* would*.)*
15. He (have) a motorcycle back then. *(Can you use* would*? Why not?)*
16. ✗ *I have finished it two hours ago.* — fix it.
17. ✗ *When have you arrived?* — fix it.
18. ✗ *I didn't went to the meeting.* — fix it.
19. The house (build) in 1912. *(Passive.)*

### Answers

| # | Answer | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | She **didn't call** me back. | *Did* takes the tense; *call* is bare. |
| 2 | **Did you see** the email I sent? | Not ✗ *Did you saw*. |
| 3 | What time **did** the meeting **start**? | *What time* asks about the time, not the subject, so *did* is required. |
| 4 | Who **broke** this? | No *did* — *who* is the subject. |
| 5 | They **weren't** at home. | *Be* negates by itself: ✗ *didn't be*. |
| 6 | He **couldn't** find it. | A modal, not a past simple; *can* has its own past. ✗ *didn't can*. |
| 7 | Where **did you go** after the game? | |
| B | **stopped, traveled, preferred, offered, carried, played, panicked, hoped, admitted, canceled** | One *l* in *traveled* and *canceled* — American. *Preferred* doubles because pre-**FER** is stressed; *offered* does not. *Panicked* adds the *k*. |
| C | **/t/** watched, laughed, danced, fixed &middot; **/d/** played, closed, opened &middot; **/ɪd/** wanted, decided, needed | Only *want*, *decide*, *need* end in /t/ or /d/. *Watched* is /tʃ/ — one syllable. |
| 8 | Yesterday I **read** /rɛd/ the whole report. | Spelling unchanged; pronunciation changed. |
| 9 &middot; 10 | She **lay** down for twenty minutes. &middot; He **laid** the baby in the crib. | *Lie* takes no object and its past is *lay*; *lay* takes one. ✗ *laid down*. |
| 11 &middot; 12 | They **hung** the new blinds. &middot; The convicted man **was hanged** in 1892. | Objects get *hung*; executions get *hanged* — and the second is a passive. |
| 13 | I **lived** in Denver for six years, but I **moved** to Austin in 2019. | Both periods are closed. |
| 14 | We **went** to the lake every summer. / We **used to go**… / Every summer we **would go**… | *Used to* adds "and we don't now"; *would* adds reminiscence and needs the past frame that *every summer* supplies. |
| 15 | He **had** a motorcycle back then. / He **used to have** a motorcycle. | Not ✗ *He would have a motorcycle* — *would* does not take state verbs. |
| 16 | I **finished** it two hours ago. | *Two hours ago* is a stated finished time. |
| 17 | When **did** you **arrive**? | *When* asks for a past location; the present perfect cannot give one. |
| 18 | I **didn't go** to the meeting. | Base form after *did*. |
| 19 | The house **was built** in 1912. | *Was* + past participle. ✗ *was build*. |
