Tenses#

All twelve tense–aspect combinations, every way English talks about the future, and the one question that decides between them.

← All grammar topics · Library home

English does not have twelve tenses. It has three times — past, present, future — and four aspects — simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous. Twelve is what you get when you multiply them.

              simple          continuous        perfect            perfect continuous
present       I work          I am working      I have worked      I have been working
past          I worked        I was working     I had worked       I had been working
future        I will work     I will be working I will have worked I will have been working

The time says when. The aspect says how you are looking at it: as a whole (simple), as something in progress (continuous), as something finished with a result that matters now (perfect), or as duration leading up to a point (perfect continuous). Every choice in this topic is a choice of viewpoint, not of when something happened.

For a Russian speaker the difficulty is not learning twelve forms. It is that Russian divides the same ground differently: two aspects, perfective and imperfective, marked on the verb itself and running through past, present and future alike. So one Russian past covers I did, I was doing, I have done and I had done, and one Russian future covers all ten English ways of talking about what comes next. Nothing in a Russian sentence tells you which English form to reach for; that information has to come from the situation, and this topic is about how to read it.

Note also that English has no future tense at all — no ending, no inflection. What it has is a set of constructions (will, be going to, the present continuous, the present simple, be about to, be to) that each say something slightly different about how settled the future is. Section 9 covers them all.
