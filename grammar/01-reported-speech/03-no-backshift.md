# When not to backshift

> Every case where the verb in the reported clause does not move back a tense — the exceptions, the free choices, and the places where the textbook rule is routinely broken by Americans who speak perfectly good English.

[← Reported speech](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

[Backshift](02-backshift.md) is the rule most learners meet first and then over-apply. Taught as an automatic mechanical operation — *present becomes past, past becomes past perfect, will becomes would* — it produces sentences that are grammatically defensible and conversationally wrong, and it produces outright errors in the several structures that have no backshifted form at all. This file is the other half of the rule.

Three separate things get lumped together under "exceptions," and keeping them apart is most of the work. Some cases are **impossible**: the past perfect has nowhere further back to go, *would* and *should* have no past forms, and the unreal past in *if I were you* and *I wish I knew* is not a real tense that can be moved. Some cases are **not triggered**: backshift only ever happens under a past-tense reporting verb, so *she says*, *she has said*, and *she'll say* never cause it in the first place. And some cases are a genuine **choice** — both forms are correct, they mean slightly different things, and Americans pick between them constantly without thinking about it.

The one generalization worth memorizing before anything else: **backshift requires a past-tense reporting verb, and even then it is obligatory far less often than textbooks imply.** Everything below is an elaboration of that sentence.

## 1. The short version

| # | Trigger | Example | Backshift |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reporting verb is present, present perfect, or future | She **says** she's tired. | Never |
| 2 | The statement is still true | He said he **lives** / **lived** in Denver. | Optional |
| 3 | Permanent or general truth | She said light **travels** / **traveled** faster than sound. | Optional |
| 4 | Original is already past perfect | He said he **had left** before we called. | Impossible |
| 5 | Simple past with a clear time frame | He said he **went** to Boston the week before. | Optional, usually skipped |
| 6 | Second conditional | She said if she **had** more time she'd learn Spanish. | Impossible |
| 7 | Third conditional | He said if he **had known**, he **would have** called. | Impossible |
| 8 | *wish*, *would rather*, *it's time*, *as if* | She said she wished she **knew**. | Impossible (inner clause) |
| 9 | Subjunctive | He said he insisted she **be** there. | Impossible |
| 10 | Modal with no past form | She said he **might** come. | Impossible |
| 11 | Report follows the words immediately | She just said she'**s** on her way. | Normally skipped |
| 12 | The event is still scheduled | He said the train **leaves** at six. | Optional, usually skipped |

Rows 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 are rules — break them and you produce either a different meaning or an ungrammatical sentence. Rows 2, 3, 5, 11, and 12 are choices, and the rest of this file is mostly about how Americans make them.

One category is missing from that table because it is not about grammar at all: a number of the "rules" a learner is likely to have been taught come out of British coursebooks and do not describe American practice. Those are collected in §12, and the mandative subjunctive among them is the difference most likely to give an American reader pause.

## 2. The reporting verb is not in the past

This is the largest category by a wide margin, and it is the one learners most often miss, because the rule they were taught was stated as *reported speech backshifts* rather than *a past reporting verb backshifts*. Backshift is a copy of the reporting verb's tense pushed onto the reported clause. If the reporting verb never went into the past, nothing gets pushed anywhere.

### Present-tense reporting verbs

Under *says*, *tells me*, *thinks*, *claims*, *insists*, *writes*, *reports*, and every other present-tense reporting verb, the reported clause keeps the exact tense of the original words. Only the pronouns move.

> "I'm exhausted," she says.  →  She says **she's** exhausted.

> "I have already eaten," he says.  →  He says **he has** already eaten.

> "I'll be there by six," Marcus says.  →  Marcus says **he'll** be there by six.

> "I didn't sign anything," the witness says.  →  The witness says **he** didn't sign anything.

> "We were living in Phoenix at the time," they say.  →  They say **they** were living in Phoenix at the time.

Note what does *not* happen in the last two. A past-tense original under a present-tense reporting verb stays exactly where it is — it does not become a past perfect. There is no tense movement of any kind.

The present reporting verb is standard American usage in four situations, and in all four the no-backshift rule is absolute:

**Reporting a text that still says what it says.** A document does not stop speaking, so the present is normal.

> "Pool closes at nine," the sign says.  →  The sign says the pool **closes** at nine. *(not* closed*)*

> "Your refund is being processed," the email says.  →  The email says **my** refund **is being** processed. *(not* was being*)*

**Reporting live, in the moment.** On the phone, across a room, relaying for someone who is standing right there.

> "I need ten more minutes," Dana calls from the kitchen.  →  Dana **says she needs** ten more minutes.

**Reporting a standing position someone holds.** Habitual or characteristic claims.

> "The whole system is broken."  →  My father **says** the whole system **is** broken.

**The historical present, in casual narrative.** Americans switch to the present to tell a story with more life in it, and the reported clauses follow the reporting verb into the present.

> So this guy walks up and **says he needs** a ride to the airport, and I **tell him I'm** going the other way.

### Present perfect reporting verbs

*Has said*, *have told me*, *has claimed*, *has admitted* — the present perfect is a present tense in English, whatever its name suggests, and it triggers no backshift.

> "I'm not running again," the senator has said.  →  The senator **has said she's** not running again.

> "I will veto the bill."  →  The governor **has** repeatedly **said he will** veto the bill.

> "We can't meet that deadline," they've told us.  →  They'**ve told us they can't** meet that deadline.

That last move is worth knowing, because switching *said* to *has said* is the cheapest way to keep a claim in its original tense without sounding like you are quoting.

### Future reporting verbs

> "I'll be late."  →  She'**ll say she'll** be late.

> "The numbers don't add up," the auditor will report.  →  The auditor **will report** that the numbers **don't** add up.

> "I want a lawyer."  →  He'**s going to say he wants** a lawyer.

### Modal and conditional reporting verbs

Any reporting verb that is not itself past-tense behaves the same way, including modal ones.

> "I'm fine."  →  She **would probably say she's** fine.

> "I can't afford it."  →  He **might tell you he can't** afford it.

> "It's a mistake."  →  They **may well argue it's** a mistake.

The one that catches people is *would say*. *Would* looks like a past form, but here it is the conditional modal, not the past of *will*, and it triggers nothing.

## 3. Optional backshift: the statement is still true

Here the reporting verb *is* past, so backshift is available — but the fact reported has not stopped being true between the moment of speaking and the moment of reporting. English allows either form. This is the single most common decision point in real reported speech, and both answers are correct.

> "I live in Denver," he said.  →  He said he **lived** in Denver. *or* He said he **lives** in Denver.

> "I'm a vegetarian," she told me.  →  She told me she **was** a vegetarian. *or* She told me she **is** a vegetarian.

> "The store closes at nine," the clerk said.  →  The clerk said the store **closed** at nine. *or* The clerk said the store **closes** at nine.

### Which one Americans actually say

Textbooks in the British tradition often present the unshifted version as the natural choice for a still-true fact. That is not what American English does. **Backshift is the American default even when the fact is still true**, and *He said he lived in Denver* carries no implication whatever that he has moved. Sequence of tenses is a strong habit in American speech and an outright convention in American editing.

The unshifted present is nonetheless very common in conversation, and it is not sloppy. It does a specific job: it foregrounds that the fact holds *now*, at the moment of reporting, and it quietly puts the reporter's own weight behind it.

| | Backshifted — *He said he lived in Denver* | Unshifted — *He said he lives in Denver* |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency in American speech | The default, and the clear majority of cases | Common, especially with fresh news |
| Frequency in American editing | Strongly preferred; house style at most publications | Used when the currency of the fact is the point |
| What it says about the fact now | Neutral — no claim either way | Asserts it is still true |
| Who is vouching for it | The original speaker only | The reporter too, at least tacitly |
| Best with | Any statement, any age | Recent statements, current arrangements, live relays |
| Risk | Can read as skeptical under contrast (see §11) | Sounds wrong if the fact has since changed |

The practical rule: **backshift unless you have a reason not to.** The reasons not to are all in this file.

### The constraint on the unshifted form

The unshifted present is not free — it commits you. If the fact has since stopped being true, the unshifted version is not merely unidiomatic but false.

> "I'm pregnant," she told me in March. *(The baby was born in July; it is now December.)*
> Correct: She told me in March that she **was** pregnant.
> Wrong: ~~She told me in March that she **is** pregnant.~~

> "I work at Boeing," he said. *(He was laid off last month.)*
> Correct: He said he **worked** at Boeing.
> Wrong: ~~He said he **works** at Boeing.~~

Backshift has no such constraint. It is silent about the present, which is exactly why it is the safe default.

## 4. Permanent facts and general truths

The traditional rule says that a statement of permanent or general truth does not backshift. The rule is real, but it is much weaker than it is usually taught.

> "Water boils at 212 degrees," the teacher said.  →  The teacher said water **boils** at 212 degrees.

> "Light travels faster than sound," he said.  →  He said light **travels** faster than sound.

> "Denver is a mile above sea level," she told us.  →  She told us Denver **is** a mile above sea level.

> "The Missouri flows into the Mississippi."  →  Our guide explained that the Missouri **flows** into the Mississippi.

### What the textbooks get wrong here

Backshifting a general truth is **not an error**. *The teacher said water boiled at 212 degrees* is a perfectly normal American sentence, and it is what most speakers produce when they are simply relaying what was said rather than teaching the fact themselves. Many grammar books mark this as incorrect. It is not, and a learner who has been told otherwise should unlearn it.

The choice is about focus, not correctness:

| | *He said water boils at 212 degrees* | *He said water boiled at 212 degrees* |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The fact | The act of saying it |
| Reads as | The reporter also asserts the fact | Neutral relay of someone's words |
| Typical context | Explaining, teaching, settling an argument | Recounting a conversation, a lecture, a class |
| With a fact the reporter rejects | Impossible — you would be endorsing it | Normal, and the usual choice |

That last row matters. When the "general truth" is one the reporter does not accept, backshift is the natural and almost obligatory choice, and the effect is audible distance:

> "The earth is flat," he insisted.  →  He insisted the earth **was** flat.

> "Vaccines cause autism," she claimed.  →  She claimed vaccines **caused** autism.

Leaving those unshifted — *He insisted the earth is flat* — is grammatical but sounds like the reporter is going along with it. This is the pragmatic effect worked out in full in §11.

## 5. The past perfect has nowhere left to go

Backshift moves a verb one step further into the past. The past perfect is already the last step, so nothing happens. This is not a stylistic exception but a structural dead end: English has no form behind *had gone*. The reasoning is laid out in [Backshift](02-backshift.md#why-the-past-perfect-is-the-floor).

> "I had already left when she called," he said.  →  He said he **had** already left when she called.

> "We had been driving for six hours," they told me.  →  They told me they **had been** driving for six hours.

> "I hadn't finished the report," she admitted.  →  She admitted she **hadn't finished** the report.

> "By the time we got there, the fire had spread to the roof."  →  The chief said that by the time **they** got there, the fire **had spread** to the roof.

Nothing changes but the pronouns. There is no *had had gone*, no double perfect, no further step. The same applies to the past perfect continuous and to perfect modals (*could have gone*, *should have known*, *might have been*), which are covered in §9.

One consequence worth naming: because the past perfect is both a backshift target and a backshift dead end, *He said he had gone to Boston* is ambiguous between two originals — "I went to Boston" and "I had gone to Boston." English does not distinguish them, and no amount of care in reporting will recover the difference. If the distinction matters, quote directly.

## 6. Past simple often stays past simple

The textbook rule says the simple past backshifts to the past perfect. In actual American speech it usually does not, and the past perfect version can sound stiff or even overwrought in conversation. Along with the still-true present in §3, this is one of the two places where the rule as taught and the rule as spoken part company most often — and the spoken version is not an error. The structural reason is in [Backshift](02-backshift.md#the-floor-is-also-why-past-simple-often-stays-put): once a verb is past, a listener already knows the event is past, and the extra step buys nothing unless it is buying order.

> "I went to Boston last week," he said.  →  He said he **went** to Boston **the week before**.
> Also correct, more formal: He said he **had gone** to Boston the week before.

> "I saw her at the airport," Tom told me.  →  Tom told me he **saw** her at the airport.

> "We didn't get the contract," she said.  →  She said they **didn't get** the contract.

> "I was watching the game when the power went out."  →  He said he **was watching** the game when the power went out.

The past continuous behaves the same way: it can shift to the past perfect continuous, but in speech it usually stays put.

### When the past perfect is genuinely needed

The past perfect earns its keep when it disambiguates the order of two past events. If the original past event happened *before* the moment being narrated, and the sentence would otherwise be read as simultaneous, shift it.

| Situation | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The time is stated explicitly | Simple past is fine | He said he **arrived** on Tuesday. |
| The order is obvious from meaning | Simple past is fine | She said she **broke** her leg skiing. |
| Casual speech, any clear context | Simple past strongly preferred | I heard she **quit**. |
| Two past events, order not obvious | Past perfect | She told the inspector she **had turned** off the stove. |
| The reported event precedes another reported event | Past perfect | He explained that the company **had filed** before the deadline passed. |
| Formal writing, legal or academic prose | Past perfect preferred | The report stated that the valve **had failed** during the test. |
| The reporting verb is far from the original event | Past perfect helps | Years later he admitted he **had lied**. |

The honest summary: in conversation, keep the simple past unless the sentence gets confusing without the past perfect. In edited writing, use the past perfect. Neither is wrong in the other's territory.

## 7. Unreal past: conditionals, *wish*, and their relatives

A whole family of structures uses a past-tense form to signal unreality rather than past time. Because that past tense is not carrying time information, it cannot be moved further back — there is no "more unreal." These are hard rules, and violating them changes the meaning.

### Second conditional

The hypothetical present. Nothing in either clause moves.

> "If I had more time, I'd learn Spanish," she said.  →  She said that if **she had** more time, **she'd** learn Spanish.

> "If I were you, I'd wait," he told me.  →  He told me that if **he were me**, **he'd** wait.

> "If they offered me the job, I'd take it."  →  He said that if they **offered** him the job, he **would take** it.

Pushing the *if* clause back into the past perfect does not make the sentence more properly reported — it turns a second conditional into a third one and changes what the person said. *She said that if she had had more time she would have learned Spanish* reports a regret about the past, not a wish about the present. That is a different statement.

### Third conditional

The hypothetical past. Already at the back wall; nothing moves.

> "If I had known, I would have called," he said.  →  He said that if **he had known**, **he would have** called.

> "We would have made the flight if the shuttle hadn't broken down."  →  They said they **would have made** the flight if the shuttle **hadn't broken** down.

> "If she hadn't spoken up, nobody would have noticed."  →  He said that if **she hadn't spoken** up, nobody **would have noticed**.

The full inventory of conditional types and the conjunctions that build them is in [subordinators of condition](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md); how each type behaves when it is reported, including the mixed conditionals, is worked through in [Special cases](08-special-cases.md#reporting-conditionals).

### *wish* and *if only*

This one has a moving part, so read it carefully. **The verb *wish* itself backshifts. The clause after it does not.**

> "I wish I knew the answer," she said.  →  She said she **wished** she **knew** the answer.

> "I wish I had a bigger car," he told me.  →  He told me he **wished** he **had** a bigger car.

> "I wish I hadn't said that," she admitted.  →  She admitted she **wished** she **hadn't said** that.

> "I wish they would stop calling."  →  He said he **wished** they **would** stop calling.

> "If only we'd left earlier."  →  She said that **if only** they **had left** earlier.

That last one is worth a caution. *If only* is an exclamation more than a proposition, and it embeds under *that* awkwardly at best; Americans normally either quote it — *She said, "If only we'd left earlier"* — or rebuild it around *wish*: *She said she wished they had left earlier.* The tense facts are as shown either way, but the embedded version is the least natural of the three. The same problem, and the full inventory of ways around it, is in [Special cases](08-special-cases.md#if-only).

Compare the two verbs in each pair: *wish* → *wished* is ordinary backshift on a real present-tense verb; *knew*, *had*, *hadn't said*, and *would stop* are unreal forms and stay exactly as they were. A learner who backshifts both produces *She said she had wished she had known the answer*, which reports a past wish about a past fact — again, not what was said. [Special cases](08-special-cases.md#reporting-wish-and-if-only) tabulates the whole family.

### *would rather*, *it's time*, *as if*, *would sooner*

The same split shows up in every structure that takes an unreal past. The outer verb shifts if it is real; the unreal past inside never does.

> "I'd rather you drove," she said.  →  She said she'**d rather I drove**.

> "It's time we left," he said.  →  He said it **was** time they **left**.

> "It's about time somebody said something."  →  She said it **was** about time somebody **said** something.

> "I'd rather we didn't talk about it," she said.  →  She said she'**d rather they didn't talk** about it.

> "He acts as if he owned the place."  →  She said he **acted as if he owned** the place.

> "I'd sooner walk than take that bus."  →  He said he'**d sooner walk** than take that bus.

*Suppose* and *imagine* are the ragged edge of this group. As bare imperatives they resist embedding — *She said, suppose he doesn't show up* is stiff at best — and American speakers normally quote them directly or rebuild the sentence: *She wondered what would happen if he didn't show up.*

## 8. The subjunctive does not shift

American English keeps a live subjunctive where British English reaches for *should* or the plain indicative, and the American form is invariant. It has no tense to move, so reporting it changes nothing.

### The mandative subjunctive

After verbs and adjectives of demanding, requiring, and recommending, the verb in the *that* clause is a bare base form — no *-s*, no *to*, no agreement — in both the direct and the reported version.

> "I insist that he be present," she said.  →  She said she **insisted** that he **be** present.

> "We demand that the fee be waived," they wrote.  →  They demanded that the fee **be** waived.

> "I recommend that she not sign it," the lawyer said.  →  The lawyer recommended that she **not sign** it.

> "It's essential that every member vote," he said.  →  He said it **was** essential that every member **vote**.

> "The board asks that the report be filed by Friday."  →  He said the board **asked** that the report **be** filed by Friday.

Note the pattern once more: the reporting verb and any surrounding real-tense verb shift (*insist* → *insisted*, *is* → *was*), while the subjunctive verb — *be*, *vote*, *not sign* — sits still. Negation is a bare *not* in front of the verb, with no *do*: *recommended that she not sign*, never ~~*that she doesn't sign*~~.

#### The American form and the British one

This is the single most visible grammatical difference between American and British reported speech, and it is the one most likely to be miscopied out of a British coursebook.

| | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | "I insist that he **be** present." | "I insist that he **should be** present." |
| Reported | She insisted that he **be** present. | She insisted that he **should be** present. |
| Negative | …recommended that she **not sign** it. | …recommended that she **should not sign** it. |
| Third-person singular | …demanded that he **pay** the fee. | …demanded that he **should pay** the fee. |
| Plain indicative | Only when *insist* reports a **claim**: *She insisted that he **was** present.* | Also available for the **demand** sense |

The bare subjunctive is the American default in speech and the near-universal choice in American editing, and it is not formal or archaic — *the doctor recommended he stop smoking* is ordinary conversation. The *should* version is not wrong in American English, but it reads as British, and in the reported clause it can be misread as advice (*should* = "ought to") rather than as the demand that was actually made. Prefer the bare form.

Two traps follow from this. First, the subjunctive is invisible except in the third-person singular and with *be*, so *they demanded that we leave* looks identical to the indicative and gives no clue; the form only surfaces in *that he leave*, *that she not sign*, *that it be*. Second, the British indicative version does have a real American use — a *that*-clause after *insist* that reports a **claim** rather than a demand takes an ordinary tensed verb and backshifts normally:

> "He was present, I'm telling you," she insisted.  →  She insisted that he **had been** present. *(a claim about a fact — ordinary backshift; in speech,* was *is just as good, per §6)*

> "He must be present," she insisted.  →  She insisted that he **be** present. *(a demand — subjunctive, no shift)*

*Insist*, *suggest*, and *urge* all straddle that line. Decide which act the speaker performed before you pick the form; §12 collects the rest of the American-versus-British points.

For the verb that most often straddles the demand/claim line, see [insist](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#insist) in the reporting-verb dictionary. The demanding and recommending verbs as speech acts are handled in [Reported commands, requests, and advice](06-commands-requests.md), and the subjunctive form itself is treated under [verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md).

### The *were* subjunctive

> "If I were taller, I'd play center," he joked.  →  He joked that if **he were** taller, **he'd** play center.

> "I'd move to Maine if it weren't so cold," she said.  →  She said she'**d move** to Maine if it **weren't** so cold.

> "She talks about it as though it were her idea."  →  He said she **talked** about it as though it **were** her idea.

Americans also use *was* in these frames in casual speech (*if I was taller*), and reporting does not change that choice either — whichever form the original used survives intact.

### Formulaic subjunctives

Fixed expressions are frozen and simply carry over.

> "God bless you," she said.  →  She said **God bless** him.

> "So be it," he said.  →  He said **so be it**.

> "Long may she reign."  →  He said **long may she reign**.

These sit right at the boundary where reported speech stops working and direct quotation takes over. If a formulaic subjunctive sounds odd embedded in a *that* clause, quote it instead.

## 9. Modals with no past form

Nine modals and modal-like forms have no past form to shift into. They are the same in reported speech as in direct speech, always. This section is the short version; [Modal verbs in reported speech](07-modals.md) is the long one.

### The invariant modals

| Modal | Direct | Reported | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **would** | "I'd help if I could." | He said he **would** help if he could. | Already the past of *will*; no further form |
| **could** | "I could be wrong." | She said she **could** be wrong. | Already the past of *can* |
| **might** | "It might rain." | He said it **might** rain. | Already the past of *may* |
| **should** | "You should call her." | She said I **should** call her. | No past form; *should have* is a different meaning |
| **ought to** | "You ought to see a doctor." | He said I **ought to** see a doctor. | Americans often swap in *should*, which is more natural |
| **used to** | "I used to smoke." | He said he **used to** smoke. | Never ~~*had used to*~~ |
| **had better** | "You'd better call her." | She said I'**d better** call her. | Never ~~*had had better*~~ |
| **must** (deduction) | "He must be lost." | She said he **must** be lost. | *Had to be lost* would mean obligation |
| **need** (modal) | "You needn't wait." | He said I **needn't** wait. | The bare modal *needn't* is chiefly British. Americans say *You don't need to wait*, and that is a lexical verb, so it **does** shift: *He said I **didn't need to** wait.* |

The last row is the one to watch, because two different verbs hide behind the same word. Modal *need* — no *to*, no *-s*, negative *needn't* — is invariant and rare in American English. Lexical *need* — *needs to*, *doesn't need to* — is an ordinary verb and backshifts like any other. Do not carry the British *needn't* into an American report just because a coursebook listed it under invariant modals.

> "You needn't wait," he said.  →  He said I **needn't** wait. *(British-sounding but grammatical)*

> "You don't need to wait," he said.  →  He said I **didn't need to** wait. *(the ordinary American report)*

Full transformations, with pronouns moved so the shape is visible:

> "I'd help you if I could," he said.  →  He said he **would** help **me** if **he could**.

> "It might snow tonight," she said.  →  She said it **might** snow **that night**.

> "You should have told me," she said.  →  She said I **should have** told **her**.

> "We'd better leave now," he said.  →  He said **we'd better** leave **right then**.

> "I used to work weekends," she said.  →  She said she **used to** work weekends.

> "That must be the wrong address," he said.  →  He said that **must** be the wrong address.

### Perfect modals never shift

*Could have*, *should have*, *would have*, *might have*, *must have*, and *ought to have* are already perfect. They stay — a modal plus a perfect is the end of the line for the same reason the past perfect is, and there is no ~~*had could have*~~ to shift into. See [Modal + perfect](07-modals.md#modal--perfect-the-end-of-the-line).

> "She must have missed the exit," he said.  →  He said she **must have** missed the exit.

> "I could have handled it better," she admitted.  →  She admitted she **could have** handled it better.

> "They might have already left," he said.  →  He said they **might have** already left.

### The modals that do shift, for contrast

These belong to [Backshift](02-backshift.md) and [Modal verbs in reported speech](07-modals.md#modals-that-change), and are listed here only so the two sets are easy to tell apart.

| Direct | Reported | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **will** | would | The core case |
| **can** | could | *Can't* → *couldn't* |
| **may** (possibility) | might | Many Americans leave *may* unchanged |
| **may** (permission) | could | *He said I could go* |
| **shall** (future, rare in American English) | would | Outside contracts and statutes Americans rarely produce it, but a British original reports normally |
| **shall** (offers and suggestions) | should | "Shall I drive?" → He asked if he **should** drive. |
| **must** (obligation) | had to *or* must | Both standard; *had to* is more common in American speech |

The *must* row is the only genuinely split one. Obligation *must* may shift to *had to* or stay put; deductive *must* never shifts, because *had to* would be heard as obligation and change the meaning outright.

> "You must fill out the form," she said.  →  She said I **had to** fill out the form. *or* She said I **must** fill out the form.

> "There must be a mistake," he said.  →  He said there **must** be a mistake. *(Never* had to be *— that would mean something forced it.)*

### The ambiguity this creates

Because *could*, *might*, and *would* are both the past forms of other modals and modals in their own right, a reported sentence can trace back to two different originals, and English does not distinguish them.

| Reported | Possible original 1 | Possible original 2 |
|---|---|---|
| He said he **could** swim. | "I can swim." | "I could swim." (as a child) |
| She said it **might** rain. | "It may rain." | "It might rain." |
| He said he **would** call. | "I will call." | "I would call (if you asked)." |

If the difference matters, quote directly or paraphrase — *He said he was able to swim back then* — rather than trusting the reader to guess.

## 10. Time, immediacy, and things still on the calendar

Backshift assumes distance between the moment of speaking and the moment of reporting. When there is no distance, there is nothing to signal, and the tenses stay.

### The just-reported quote

Reporting words spoken seconds ago, with a past reporting verb, normally takes no backshift at all. The clock has not moved.

> "I'm on my way."  →  She just said she'**s** on her way.

> "I don't want to go."  →  He said he **doesn't** want to go — didn't you hear him?

> "What time is it?"  →  I asked what time it **is**.

> "I'm hungry."  →  What? I **said I'm** hungry.

That last pattern — repeating yourself when you weren't heard — is essentially always unshifted in American speech. *I said I was hungry* is grammatical, but it sounds like a report of an earlier conversation rather than a repetition of the sentence you just uttered.

Note also the time adverbs: none of them shift here either. *Tomorrow* stays *tomorrow*, *here* stays *here*, *this* stays *this*, because the deictic center has not moved.

> "I'll finish it tomorrow."  →  He just said he'**ll** finish it **tomorrow**.

Under a genuinely distant report the same sentence becomes *He said he would finish it the next day*. The full table of time and place shifts, and the deictic center that decides whether any of them move, is in [Pronouns, time, and place](04-pronouns-time-place.md).

### Events still scheduled

If the event has not happened yet at the moment of reporting, the present simple of a timetable and the present continuous of an arrangement both stay put.

> "The train leaves at six," he said.  →  He said the train **leaves** at six.

> "The meeting is at three," she told me.  →  She told me the meeting **is** at three.

> "We're moving in June," they said.  →  They said they'**re moving** in June.

> "Registration opens Monday," the email said.  →  The email said registration **opens** Monday.

> "I'm seeing the doctor on Thursday."  →  He said he'**s seeing** the doctor on Thursday.

Once the event is in the past relative to the report, the unshifted form is no longer available and backshift becomes obligatory:

> *(Said on Monday, reported on Wednesday, about a Tuesday train.)*
> "The train leaves at six," he said.  →  He said the train **was leaving** at six. *or* He said the train **left** at six.

### Standing arrangements and open commitments

The same logic covers promises, offers, and refusals that have not yet been discharged. The reporting-verb entries — see [promise](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise) — carry the details, but the tense principle is the one above: if the commitment is still live, the present survives.

> "I'll pay you back Friday."  →  He said he'**ll** pay me back Friday. *(Friday hasn't come.)*

> "I'll pay you back Friday."  →  He said he'**d** pay me back Friday. *(It's Saturday and he hasn't.)*

The second version, with backshift and no follow-through, is the one that carries a note of complaint. That is the effect the next section is about.

## 11. What the choice signals

Where backshift is optional, the two forms are not interchangeable in tone. English exploits the choice, and American speakers read it without thinking about it.

The core effect: **the unshifted form ties the statement to the present and puts the reporter's own weight behind it; the backshifted form leaves the statement inside the past conversation and says nothing about now.** Because the backshifted form is silent about the present, it can be *heard* as a refusal to vouch — especially when the unshifted option was obviously available and the speaker passed it up.

Both columns below use the same past-tense reporting verb — *said*, *told me* — so that the only thing changing is the tense inside the reported clause. That is the choice this section is about. Switching the reporting verb itself to the present is a third option, and a stronger one; it comes at the end of the section.

| The reported claim | Unshifted — *said* + present | Backshifted — *said* + past |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm a doctor" | He said he **is** a doctor. — Neutral to endorsing; no doubt raised. | He said he **was** a doctor. — Neutral in most contexts, but readable as *and I have my doubts*. |
| "The check is in the mail" | She said the check **is** in the mail. — Passed along as current fact. | She said the check **was** in the mail. — The classic skeptical relay. |
| "I'm handling it" | He said he'**s** handling it. — The reporter accepts this. | He said he **was** handling it. — Often implies it isn't being handled. |
| "The road is open" | They said the road **is** open. — Usable as current information. | They said the road **was** open. — Was true then; check again. |

Three cautions, because this effect is easy to overstate:

**Backshift is not itself a signal of doubt.** It is the default. *She said she was tired* means she said she was tired. The skeptical reading appears only in contrast — when the fact is obviously still current, the unshifted form would have been natural, and the speaker chose otherwise. Tone of voice does most of the work.

**American journalism backshifts as a matter of house style, with no implication at all.** "The mayor said she was reviewing the proposal" is the standard newspaper construction and carries no editorial skepticism. Publications that use the unshifted form do so to mark a claim as still-operative, not to endorse it.

**Legal, technical, and academic writing often refuses to backshift on purpose**, in order to preserve the claim exactly as made. "The witness testified that the vehicle is registered in her name" keeps the assertion intact and auditable, and an academic writer's *Chomsky argued that language is innate* marks a claim that still stands rather than one that expired with its author. These are deliberate conventions, not errors; [Special cases](08-special-cases.md#no-backshift-for-claims-that-still-stand) sets out the register rules for each field.

### Using the effect deliberately

> "I'm the one who found it," he said.  →  He said he **was** the one who found it. *(Distance. The reporter is not confirming.)*

> "I'm the one who found it," he said.  →  He said he **is** the one who found it. *(The reporter is passing the claim along as fact.)*

> "Everything is under control."  →  Management said everything **was** under control. *(Then. Draw your own conclusion.)*

> "Everything is under control."  →  Management says everything **is** under control. *(Current status, reported straight.)*

Switching the reporting verb to the present — *he says*, *management says* — is the cleanest way to get the unshifted version without anyone wondering whether you forgot the rule, which is why it is so common in [headlines and summaries](08-special-cases.md#headlines-present-tense-no-backshift).

## 12. American practice, and where the British books differ

Most grammar material a learner meets in English was written for a British classroom, and reported speech is one of the places where the two standards genuinely diverge. Everything in this file describes American English. This section collects the specific points where following a British book would make an American report read wrong.

### The summary table

| Point | American | British | Where it is treated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandative subjunctive | insisted that he **be** present | insisted that he **should be** present | §8 |
| Subjunctive negation | recommended that she **not sign** | recommended that she **should not sign** | §8 |
| Still-true statements | backshift is the default | leaving the present is presented as the natural choice | §3 |
| Bare modal *need* | *didn't need to* (shifts) | *needn't* (invariant) | §9 |
| *shall* in the original | rare outside legal drafting; reports as *should* or *would* | still current in questions and offers; same reports | §9 |
| *have got* / *gotten* | *had gotten* a raise | *had got* a raise | below |
| Simple past vs. present perfect | "I already ate" is ordinary | "I've already eaten" is required | below |
| Reporting-verb spelling | apologize, criticize, analyze, canceled | apologise, criticise, analyse, cancelled | below |
| *inquire* / *enquire* | *inquire* for both senses | *enquire* to ask, *inquire* to investigate | below |
| Collective subjects | the team **has** agreed | the team **have** agreed | below |
| Commas and periods at a quote's end | inside the closing quote | outside, unless part of the quotation | [Basics](01-basics.md#27-periods-and-commas-go-inside-the-closing-quote) |

### Spelling the reporting verbs

Reporting verbs are where the *-ize* / *-ise* split shows up most, because so many of them are Greek-suffix verbs. American English writes **-ize** across the board:

> "I'm sorry I missed the meeting."  →  He **apologized** for missing the meeting. *(not* apologised*)*

> "The estimate is far too low."  →  She **criticized** the estimate as far too low. *(not* criticised*)*

> "This is the central problem."  →  He **emphasized** that it **was** the central problem. *(not* emphasised*)*

The same goes for *recognize*, *summarize*, *characterize*, *minimize*, *theorize*, *apologize*, and *acknowledge* → *acknowledgment* (no *e* before the *-ment* in American spelling, and *judgment* likewise).

Two traps sit inside this rule.

**Not every *-ise* is a disguised *-ize*.** A handful of verbs end in *-ise* in both standards, because the *-ise* is part of the stem rather than the Greek suffix. Several of them are reporting verbs, and Americans who "correct" them produce nonsense words:

| Always *-ise*, in both standards | Never |
|---|---|
| **promise** | ~~promize~~ |
| **advise** | ~~advize~~ |
| **surmise** | ~~surmize~~ |
| **apprise** | ~~apprize~~ |
| **compromise** | ~~compromize~~ |
| **supervise**, **revise**, **devise**, **improvise**, **exercise**, **advertise** | ~~-ize~~ |

> "I'll pay you back Friday."  →  He **promised** to pay me back Friday. *(never* promized*)*

> "You should get a lawyer."  →  She **advised** me to get a lawyer. *(never* advized*)*

**The *-yse* verbs go to *-yze*.** American *analyze*, *paralyze*, *catalyze*; British *analyse*, *paralyse*, *catalyse*.

> "The data show a clear trend."  →  The team **analyzed** the data and reported that **they showed** a clear trend.

And the *-l-* doubling runs the other way from what a British book will show. When the stress is not on the final syllable, American English keeps a single *l* before a suffix: *counseled*, *canceled*, *labeled*, *marveled*, *signaled*, *quarreled*, *traveled*. British doubles all of these.

> "Don't sign it."  →  The attorney **counseled** her not to sign it. *(British:* counselled*)*

> "That's extraordinary."  →  He **marveled** at it. *(British:* marvelled*)*

### *inquire* and *enquire*

British English splits the pair by meaning: *enquire* is to ask an ordinary question, *inquire* is to conduct a formal investigation. **American English does not make the split.** *Inquire* covers both senses and *enquire* is simply the British spelling; use *inquire* everywhere, and *inquiry* for the noun.

> "Has the position been filled?" she asked.  →  She **inquired whether** the position **had been** filled.

> "Where were you that night?"  →  The detective **inquired** where I **had been** that night.

Note the complement pattern while you are here: *inquire* takes no personal object. *She inquired whether…* and *She inquired of the registrar whether…* are both fine; ~~*She inquired the registrar whether…*~~ and ~~*She inquired me…*~~ are not. The full pattern is in [Reported questions](05-questions.md).

*Inquire* is also markedly more formal than *ask* in American English. In ordinary speech Americans ask; *inquire* belongs to police reports, journalism, and business correspondence.

### *got* and *gotten*

American English keeps a distinct past participle *gotten* for acquiring and becoming, and it survives backshift intact:

> "I've gotten three offers," she said.  →  She said she **had gotten** three offers. *(British:* had got*)*

> "The weather has gotten worse."  →  He said the weather **had gotten** worse.

The exception is *have got* meaning *possess* or *must*, where American English uses *got*, not *gotten*, and where the whole thing usually collapses to *have* or *had to* under backshift:

> "I've got a meeting at four."  →  She said she **had** a meeting at four. *(not* had gotten *— that would mean she acquired one)*

> "You've got to see this."  →  He said I **had to** see it.

### Simple past where British English wants the present perfect

Americans use the simple past with *already*, *just*, and *yet* where British English requires the present perfect. This matters here because the two originals report differently under the loose rule and identically under the strict one:

> "I **already ate**," he said.  →  He said he **already ate**. *(ordinary American)* *or* He said he **had already eaten**. *(formal)*

> "I'**ve** already **eaten**," he said.  →  He said he **had already eaten**.

Both land on *had already eaten* when you shift, which is why the distinction disappears in edited prose and survives only in speech. A British-trained learner reporting an American speaker should not "fix" the original into a present perfect before reporting it — report what was said.

### Collective subjects

British English treats collective nouns as plural when the members are in view: *the team have agreed*, *the government are considering it*. American English keeps them singular. When you are **quoting**, leave the original alone. When you are **reporting**, American practice re-agrees the verb:

> "The committee have reached a decision," the British chair said.
> Quoted: The chair said, "The committee **have** reached a decision."
> Reported: The chair said the committee **had** reached a decision.

The past tense hides the difference here, which is convenient; it surfaces under a present-tense reporting verb, where an American writer would produce *The chair says the committee **has** reached a decision.*

### What is the same in both

Do not over-correct. The core machinery of this file is shared: backshift itself, the past-perfect floor, the unreal past in conditionals and *wish*, the invariant modals, and the pragmatics of §11 all work identically on both sides of the Atlantic. The divergences worth acting on are the eleven rows in the table above, and none of the rest of this file needs a dialect label.

## 13. Errors this section is meant to prevent

The two tables below are different in kind, and the difference is worth keeping straight. The first collects **hard errors** — sentences that are ungrammatical, that report something other than what was said, that assert something false, or that misspell the reporting verb. The second collects **mismatches**: sentences that are perfectly grammatical but that an American speaker would not produce in the situation described, usually because a mechanical rule was applied where a choice was available. Most grammar books put both kinds in one list and mark them all "wrong," which is where a lot of learner anxiety comes from.

### Hard errors

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ~~She says she **was** tired.~~ | She says she **is** tired. | Present reporting verb — no backshift is triggered |
| ~~He has said he **would** come.~~ | He has said he **will** come. | Present perfect is a present tense |
| ~~He said he **had had** left already.~~ | He said he **had** left already. | Past perfect has no further form |
| ~~She said if she **had had** more time she **would have** learned Spanish.~~ | She said if she **had** more time she **would** learn Spanish. | Second conditional does not shift; shifting makes it a third conditional |
| ~~He said if he **had had** known, he would have called.~~ | He said if he **had** known, he **would have** called. | Third conditional is already at the back wall |
| ~~She said she **had wished** she **had known**.~~ | She said she **wished** she **knew**. | *Wish* shifts; the unreal clause after it does not |
| ~~He insisted that she **was** present.~~ (he was demanding it) | He insisted that she **be** present. | Mandative subjunctive is invariant |
| ~~The lawyer recommended that she **didn't** sign.~~ | The lawyer recommended that she **not sign**. | Subjunctive negation is a bare *not*, with no *do* |
| ~~She said it **would** rain.~~ (original: "It might rain.") | She said it **might** rain. | *Might* has no past form and needs none |
| ~~He said he **had used to** smoke.~~ | He said he **used to** smoke. | *Used to* is invariant |
| ~~She said I **had had better** hurry.~~ | She said I'**d better** hurry. | *Had better* is invariant |
| ~~He said there **had to** be a mistake.~~ (original: "There must be a mistake.") | He said there **must** be a mistake. | Deductive *must* does not shift; *had to* would mean something forced it |
| ~~She said she **had gotten** a meeting at four.~~ (original: "I've got a meeting at four.") | She said she **had** a meeting at four. | *Have got* = possess; *gotten* would mean she acquired one |
| ~~He said he **works** at Boeing.~~ (he was laid off last month) | He said he **worked** at Boeing. | The unshifted present asserts the fact is still true, and it isn't |
| ~~She promized to call.~~ | She **promised** to call. | *Promise* is *-ise* in both standards |

### Grammatical, but not what an American would say

| Off | Better | Why |
|---|---|---|
| She just said she **was** on her way. *(relaying it seconds later)* | She just said she'**s** on her way. | No time has passed; the shift signals a distance that isn't there |
| He said the train **left** at six. *(the train hasn't left)* | He said the train **leaves** at six. | Still-scheduled events keep the present |
| I asked what time it **was**. *(asking right now)* | I asked what time it **is**. | Immediate report, no distance |
| He said he **had gone** to Boston the week before. *(casual conversation)* | He said he **went** to Boston the week before. | The past perfect is correct but stiff in speech (§6) |
| She insisted that he **should be** present. | She insisted that he **be** present. | The *should* form is British; in American English it can be misread as advice (§8, §12) |
| He apologised for being late. | He **apologized** for being late. | American spelling (§12) |
| She enquired whether the room was ready. | She **inquired** whether the room was ready. | American English does not use *enquire* (§12) |

Nothing in the second table is an error of grammar. Each row is a case where the sentence is well formed and simply reports the wrong thing about the speaker, the moment, or the dialect.

## 14. How to decide, in order

1. **Is the reporting verb in the past?** If not — *says*, *has said*, *will say*, *would say* — stop. Nothing shifts. (§2)
2. **Is the reported verb already past perfect, or a perfect modal?** If so, stop. There is nowhere to go. (§5, §9)
3. **Is it a modal with no past form** — *would, could, might, should, ought to, used to, had better*, deductive *must*? If so, stop. (§9)
4. **Is it an unreal past** — second or third conditional, *wish*, *if only*, *would rather*, *it's time*, *as if*, *were*? If so, stop; only the outer real-tense verb moves. (§7)
5. **Is it a subjunctive?** If so, stop. (§8)
6. **Are you repeating something said moments ago, or reporting an event still on the calendar?** Leave it in the present. (§10)
7. **Is the statement a simple past with a clear time frame?** Leave it in the simple past unless you are writing formally or the order of events would be unclear. (§6)
8. **Otherwise you have a free choice.** Backshift by default. Skip the backshift when you want the claim read as still true and are willing to stand behind it. (§3, §4, §11)

Then one last pass over the spelling of the reporting verb itself — *apologized*, *criticized*, *analyzed*, *counseled*, *inquired* — because that is the American marker a British-trained learner misses most often. (§12)
