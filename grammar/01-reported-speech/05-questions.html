Reported questions#

How a direct question turns into a subordinate clause inside a statement — the word order that changes back, the do that disappears, and the choice between if and whether.

← Reported speech · All grammar topics

A reported question is not a question. It is a noun clause sitting inside a statement, and every difference between the direct and the reported form follows from that one fact. Are you coming? is a question and behaves like one: the auxiliary jumps in front of the subject, and the sentence ends with a question mark. She asked whether I was coming. is a statement whose object happens to be a question in meaning only, so the inversion unwinds, the question mark goes, and the clause looks exactly like the statement I was coming with a conjunction bolted to the front.

"Are you coming?" she asked. → She asked whether I was coming.

Everything below is an elaboration of that. Two changes account for nearly every mistake learners make with reported questions — word order and do-support — and both of them are places where the textbook rule really is the rule, followed by essentially every native speaker of American English in essentially every register. That is worth saying up front, because other corners of English grammar (whom, shall, split infinitives) are taught as rules and ignored in practice. This one is not. She asked where was I is not informal English; it is simply not English.

The rest of the reported-speech machinery applies here unchanged: tenses usually move one step back (Backshift), except when they don't (When not to backshift), and pronouns, times, and places are recalculated from the reporter's position (Pronouns, time, and place shifts). This section covers only what is special about questions.

The three changes that define a reported question#

1. Word order returns to statement order#

In a direct question the tensed auxiliary sits in front of the subject. In a reported question it goes back behind the subject, exactly where it would be in an ordinary statement.

"Where are you going?" he asked. → He asked where I was going.

"Is she still at the office?" I asked. → I asked whether she was still at the office.

"What have they decided?" she asked. → She asked what they had decided.

"Can you hear me?" he asked. → He asked whether I could hear him.

The test is simple: strike out the reporting clause and the conjunction, and what is left must be a grammatical statement. He asked where I was going → I was going ✓. He asked where was I going → was I going ✗.

The apparent exception. A few clauses with be keep what looks like an inverted shape: She asked what was wrong, He asked what was the matter, Nobody asked who was next, I wondered how much was left. Nothing has been inverted in any of them. The wh-word is the subject of its own clause, so what was wrong already is statement order — subject, verb, complement — exactly like the printer was broken. Learners spot these, decide the rule has been violated, and "repair" them to She asked what wrong was, which is not English. The strike-out test settles it: remove the frame and what was wrong stands on its own as a sentence, while where was I going does not.

What real speech does. In some varieties of American English — notably African American English, much Southern speech, and Irish-influenced dialects — embedded inversion after ask and wonder is entirely normal: He asked me was I okay, I wonder is it too late. This is a genuine feature of those varieties, not an error in them, but it does not appear in standard written American English, and a learner who produces it in an essay or an email will be read as making a mistake rather than as speaking a dialect.

2. Do-support disappears#

The do, does, and did of a direct question exist only to carry tense so the main verb does not have to invert. Once the inversion is gone, that job is gone with it, and the tense goes back onto the main verb.

"Do you know him?" she asked. → She asked whether I knew him.

"Does it hurt?" the doctor asked. → The doctor asked whether it hurt.

"What did you want?" he asked. → He asked what I had wanted.

"Why did she leave so early?" they asked. → They asked why she had left so early.

Two kinds of do survive, and neither one is do-support. The first is do as a full lexical verb, which is a main verb like any other and stays put:

"Did you do the dishes?" she asked. → She asked whether I had done the dishes.

The second is the do of negation. English needs an auxiliary to attach not to, so a negative reported clause keeps its do for the same reason the plain statement She doesn't answer keeps it — nothing to do with questions at all.

"Why doesn't she answer?" he asked. → He asked why she didn't answer.

"Don't you want the job?" she asked. → She asked whether I didn't want the job.

That last one is correct and slightly stilted, which is a fact about negative questions rather than about do — see negative questions below.

Stressed do in a question is still do-support. Leaning on the do of a direct question — "DO you actually want this?" — adds insistence without changing the syntax. That do is still there only to carry tense off the main verb, so it disappears from the report along with every other interrogative do, and the insistence has to be re-expressed with a word, because a subordinate clause has no stress pattern to inherit.

"DO you actually want this?" she asked. → She asked whether I actually wanted it. → She asked pointedly whether I wanted it.

Genuine emphatic do does survive — in reported statements. Emphatic do lives in declaratives (I do want it), where it is a real auxiliary and not a prop for inversion. Nothing dislodges it, and it backshifts like anything else:

"I do want it," he said. → He said he did want it. "She does know," he insisted. → He insisted that she did know.

So the generalization is not "emphatic do is dropped." It is narrower and more useful: the do that dies is the do that existed only to support inversion. In a reported answer or comment, which is a statement, an emphatic do is perfectly safe — see Reported short answers.

3. The question mark goes away#

The sentence is a statement, so it ends with a period. This holds no matter how long the reported question runs or how urgently it was originally asked.

"Where on earth have you been?!" she asked. → She asked where I had been.

A comma before the clause is wrong too. She asked, if I was coming punctuates the clause as though it were a quotation; the comma belongs only with a genuine direct quote.

All three at once#

Each change is small; together they can transform a sentence past the point of recognition. Trace one all the way through:

"Why didn't you call me back yesterday?" she asked. → She asked why I hadn't called her back the day before.

Inversion undone (didn't you → I hadn't), do-support absorbed into the past perfect, question mark replaced by a period, pronouns flipped to the reporter's point of view, and yesterday shifted to the day before.

Yes/no questions: if and whether#

The basic transformation#

A yes/no question has no wh-word to introduce its clause, so English supplies one: if or whether. Both mean the same thing, and in the ordinary case either is correct.

"Do you have a minute?" he asked. → He asked if I had a minute. "Do you have a minute?" he asked. → He asked whether I had a minute.

"Has the check cleared?" she asked. → She asked whether the check had cleared.

"Are you two related?" the clerk asked. → The clerk asked if we were related.

Nothing else may fill this slot. That introduces reported statements, not questions, so He asked that I had a minute is not an awkward version of the sentence — it is broken. (Ask that does have a life of its own, but it makes a request, and in American English it takes the bare subjunctive: He asked that I be given a minute. See ask that is a request, not a question.) The sequence if or not does not exist in any position. And the direct question's own inverted word order never survives: He asked whether had I a minute is as wrong with a conjunction in front of it as it was without one.

The five places where only whether works#

If is the narrower word. In each of the following environments it is ungrammatical, and whether is the only option.

1. Immediately before or not. The two words whether or not form a unit; if or not does not exist. Note that the split version, with or not trailing at the end of the clause, is fine with if.

"Are you coming or not?" she asked. → She asked whether or not I was coming. ✗ She asked if or not I was coming. ✓ She asked if I was coming or not.

2. Before a to-infinitive. Only whether can be followed directly by to.

"Should I wait?" she wondered. → She wondered whether to wait. ✗ She wondered if to wait.

3. After a preposition. An if-clause can never be the object of a preposition.

"Will the sale go through?" everyone wanted to know. → Everyone argued about whether the sale would go through. ✗ Everyone argued about if the sale would go through.

"Is he qualified?" the board asked. → The whole appointment turned on whether he was qualified.

4. At the head of a subject clause. When the reported question is the subject of the sentence rather than the object of a verb, only whether can start it.

"Did he apologize?" people asked. → Whether he apologized was never made clear. ✗ If he apologized was never made clear.

5. As the complement of a noun. After nouns like question, decision, doubt, and issue, the clause takes whether.

"Should we rebid the contract?" they asked. → The question whether to rebid the contract came up twice. ✗ the question if to rebid the contract

One honest footnote to that item. Bare question whether reads as legal or academic in American English, and most writers insert of: the question of whether to rebid the contract. Both are standard, and of is by far the commoner outside briefs and journals. What is never available in either version is if. See the question whether.

To that list, add a sixth case that is not a grammatical prohibition but a practical one. When if could be read as a condition, use whether. Let me know if the package arrives has two readings — "tell me in the event it arrives" and "tell me whether or not it arrives" — and only the second is a reported question. Swapping in whether removes the ambiguity, and this is the single most useful reason to reach for it.

"Did the package arrive?" → Let me know whether the package arrived. (report either way) compare: Let me know if the package arrives. (only in the event it does)

Register: whether is the more formal one#

Where both are grammatical, they are not equally likely. If dominates conversation; whether dominates edited prose, and increases with the formality of the document.

"Did you sign it?" she asked. → She asked if I'd signed it. (speech, email, fiction dialogue) "Did you sign it?" she asked. → She asked whether I had signed it. (report, brief, academic writing)

Some usage guides go further and claim that if is flatly incorrect for indirect questions. That is not a real rule and never has been. If has introduced indirect questions since Old English, it appears in every serious dictionary and reference grammar as standard, and I don't know if he's coming is unremarkable in any register short of a legal filing. Treat the choice as a matter of tone: whether is dressier, and it is the safe default in writing because it is never wrong where if would be right, while the reverse is not true.

as to whether, of whether, and other padding#

Formal American prose — business memos, agency reports, and academic writing especially — often stretches whether into a longer phrase. None of these is an error, and all of them are longer than they need to be.

The board debated as to whether the sale should proceed. better: The board debated whether the sale should proceed.

As to whether is standard but wordy after a verb that could take the clause directly. It earns its keep only where the verb needs a preposition and about would be wrong or clumsy: no guidance as to whether the deadline was firm. After debate, discuss, consider, question, and decide, drop it. See as to whether.

Not every long form is padding, though, and it is worth knowing which is which. The question of whether and the issue of whether are optional — the of can go. Regardless of whether is not optional: regardless is an adjective that requires of before any complement, so dropping it produces an error rather than a tightening.

✓ regardless of whether they call   ✗ regardless whether they call ✓ the question of whether they call   ✓ the question whether they call

Depend is a middle case worth knowing. It depends on whether they call is the form to write; It depends whether they call, with the preposition dropped, is common and unremarkable in American speech but still gets flagged by editors. Note that the choice is about the preposition, not about whether — depends on if they call is out either way, because if cannot follow a preposition at all.

Alternative questions: whether … or …#

When the direct question offers a choice, the reported clause spells the alternatives out with or. Both conjunctions are grammatical here, but whether is much the commoner because the structure is inherently a little formal.

"Do you want coffee or tea?" she asked. → She asked whether I wanted coffee or tea.

"Is it broken, or did someone unplug it?" he asked. → He asked whether it was broken or someone had unplugged it.

"Should we drive or fly?" we asked. → We couldn't decide whether to drive or fly.

The reported form is ambiguous in a way the spoken one is not. Do you want coffee or tea? is two different questions depending on the tune. With a rise on tea it is a yes/no question — roughly "do you want a hot drink at all?" — and the answer is yes or no. With a fall on tea it is an alternative question offering a closed choice, and the answer is coffee or tea. Both report as She asked whether I wanted coffee or tea, and the intonation that told them apart is gone.

Writing therefore has to disambiguate with words:

closed choice → She asked which I wanted, coffee or tea. closed choice → She asked whether I wanted coffee or whether I wanted tea. yes/no → She asked whether I wanted anything to drink — coffee, tea, either one.

And whether … or not is a third structure again, always yes/no and never alternative: She asked whether I wanted coffee or not asks about coffee only, and tea is not on the table.

Wh-questions#

The wh-word introduces the clause#

A wh-question already has a word at the front, and that word does double duty: it introduces the subordinate clause and fills a slot inside it. No if, no whether, and no that is added.

"Where is the closing?" she asked. → She asked where the closing was.

"What do you need?" he asked. → He asked what I needed.

"How much did it cost?" they asked. → They asked how much it had cost.

Never insert that after the wh-word. He asked what that I wanted is not English, and neither is She asked where that I lived.

The full set behaves alike: who, whom, whose, what, which, when, where, why, how, and the compound forms how long, how much, how many, how often, what kind of, which one. For the words themselves as clause introducers, see complementizers and noun-clause introducers.

When the wh-word is already the subject, nothing moves#

If the wh-word is the subject of the direct question, there was no inversion and no do-support to undo. Only the tense and the pronouns change.

"Who called?" he asked. → He asked who had called.

"What happened?" she asked. → She asked what had happened.

"Which door leads outside?" I asked. → I asked which door led outside.

Compare the object versions of the same questions, where the full unwinding does happen:

"Who did you call?" he asked. → He asked who I had called.

"What did she say?" I asked. → I asked what she had said.

This pair is worth memorizing side by side, because the direct forms differ by one word and the reported forms differ by their whole shape.

Stranded prepositions stay stranded#

American English leaves the preposition at the end, in the reported clause exactly as in the direct question.

"Who were you talking to?" she asked. → She asked who I had been talking to.

"What is this for?" he asked. → He asked what it was for.

"Where did you get that from?" they asked. → They asked where I had gotten it from.

Fronting the preposition is possible and formal: She asked to whom I had been speaking. Outside of legal and academic prose it sounds stiff, and whom is what makes it sound that way, not the preposition.

Who and whom#

The textbook rule is that whom is required when the wh-word is the object of the verb or of a preposition. In practice, Americans use who in nearly all of those positions, in speech and in most edited writing.

"Who did they hire?" I asked. → I asked who they had hired. (ordinary American English) → I asked whom they had hired. (formal; correct but noticeably starchy)

Whom survives healthily in one place: immediately after a preposition (for whom, to whom, with whom). Everywhere else, using who is safe, and using whom where it does not belong — as the subject of the embedded clause, as in I asked whom they thought was responsible — is a worse error than not using it at all.

Wh-word + to-infinitive#

When the answer would be about what someone should do, English offers a compressed form: the wh-word plus a to-infinitive, with the subject left unexpressed and read off the main clause.

"Where should I park?" she asked. → She asked where to park.

"What should we tell them?" they wondered. → They wondered what to tell them.

"Should I sign it?" he asked. → He asked whether to sign it.

Three constraints. The construction works with whether and with every wh-word except why (why to go is not English) and how come. The unexpressed subject must be recoverable from the main clause, so She asked where to park can only mean she was asking about herself — to report a question about someone else, use the finite form (She asked where I should park). And never stack the modal back on: what to do or what I should do, never what to should do.

How come and other awkward cases#

How come takes statement order even in a direct question, which makes it the one wh-word nobody can accidentally invert. It embeds without change, but it stays informal; why is the neutral substitute in writing.

"How come you're late?" she asked. → She asked how come I was late. (informal) → She asked why I was late. (neutral)

Negative questions report as ordinary questions, and the surprise or reproach they carried usually evaporates. If the attitude matters, put it in the reporting verb.

"Aren't you going to eat?" she asked. → She asked whether I wasn't going to eat. better: She was surprised that I wasn't going to eat.

Rhetorical questions are not really requests for information, and reporting them as questions misses the point. Report the assertion behind them.

"Who cares?" he said. → He said nobody cared. (not: He asked who cared.)

Echo questions — You did what? — depend on stress and context and generally have to be paraphrased: She couldn't believe what I had done.

Backshift inside a reported question#

Reported questions backshift on exactly the same terms as reported statements; see Backshift for the full treatment and Modals in reported speech for the modal column. The short version, applied to questions:

"Are you working?"She asked whether I was working.

"Have you called?"She asked whether I had called.

Two practical qualifications, both spelled out in full at When not to backshift. Backshift is optional when the situation is still true at the moment of reporting — She asked where I live is perfectly good if I still live there, and often preferable, because She asked where I lived can hint that I have since moved. And an already-past direct question does not have to go to the past perfect in speech: He asked if I saw the email is what Americans actually say, even though if I had seen the email is what the textbook prescribes. See which one Americans actually say and when the past perfect is genuinely needed. The past perfect earns its keep when the sequence of events would otherwise be unclear.

To put numbers on the second point: in conversation, He asked if I saw it and He asked if I had seen it are both ordinary, and the simple past is the more frequent of the two. Edited prose leans the other way. Neither is an error, and a learner who always uses the past perfect will sound careful rather than wrong — which is why it is the form worth learning first.

When the reporting verb is not in the past, nothing backshifts#

Everything above assumes asked. If the reporting verb is present, present perfect, or future, the clock has not been reset and the reported question keeps the tense it was asked in. This is not an exception to backshift; it is the absence of a trigger.

"Where are you?" → She's asking where you are. (not where you were) "Do you take cards?" → He wants to know whether you take cards. "Will the check clear?" → She keeps asking whether the check will clear. "Did you send it?" → He has asked twice whether you sent it.

The word order and do-support rules do not relax here — they have nothing to do with tense. She's asking where are you is wrong for exactly the same reason She asked where was I is.

✗ She's asking where are you.   ✓ She's asking where you are. ✗ He wants to know do you take cards.   ✓ He wants to know whether you take cards.

This matters most with want to know, is asking, and is wondering, which are the frames people actually use to relay a live question across a room or a chat window. Full treatment at present-tense reporting verbs and the reporting verb decides.

Reporting verbs for questions#

ask#

The all-purpose verb, and the only one that comfortably takes a person object, a question clause, both, or neither.

"Do you take cards?" I asked. → I asked whether they took cards. "Do you take cards?" I asked the driver. → I asked the driver whether they took cards.

Ask also heads two idioms that are not reported questions at all: ask about takes a topic rather than a clause (She asked about the deposit), and ask after means to inquire about someone's health (He asked after your mother). And ask + object + to-infinitive is a request, not a question (She asked me to wait) — see Reported commands, requests, and advice.

ask that is a request, not a question#

Ask has one more frame, and it is the one that traps writers who have just learned that that is forbidden after ask. The ban is on that as a substitute for if or whether. A different that-clause — one that reports a demand or a request rather than a question — is entirely correct after ask, and it takes a special verb form.

✗ She asked that the room was quiet. (attempted question — broken) ✓ She asked whether the room was quiet. (a question) ✓ She asked that the room be quiet. (a request — mandative subjunctive)

The verb in that last clause is the bare form — be, not is or was; he go, not he goes. In American English the bare subjunctive is the default, and it is what edited American prose uses across the board:

"Please be present at the hearing." → The judge asked that he be present at the hearing. "Send the file by Friday." → She asked that the file be sent by Friday. "He should not leave the building." → They asked that he not leave the building.

British English generally inserts should — asked that he should be present, asked that the file should be sent — and that form is not wrong in American English, merely unidiomatic; American readers hear it as British or as slightly old-fashioned. The distinction is worth getting right in a document with an American audience.

American default: They insisted that he be present. British default: They insisted that he should be present.

The same subjunctive follows demand, insist, require, request, recommend, propose, suggest, and urge, which is why the tag row further down reports "Let's get lunch, shall we?" as He suggested that we get lunch rather than that we got lunch. Three things follow from the bare form and catch people out: it does not agree with its subject (that she be, not that she is), it does not backshift no matter how far in the past the reporting verb sits (He asked yesterday that she be present), and it is negated with a bare not placed directly in front of the verb, with no do (that he not leave, never that he doesn't leave).

Full treatment, including the verbs that take both a subjunctive and an ordinary indicative with different meanings, is at the mandative subjunctive and American bare subjunctive vs. British should; for why it is immune to backshift, see the subjunctive does not shift.

wonder#

Reports an unspoken question. The distinction is real and worth keeping: He asked whether she was home means he said something out loud; He wondered whether she was home means he did not.

"Is anyone actually reading this?" → She wondered whether anyone was actually reading it.

The past continuous I was wondering is not a report at all in most uses — it is a politeness formula for making a request; see below.

inquire#

The formal register's ask, and the one learners most often break. It takes no direct person object; the person, if named, arrives with of in very formal writing.

"Has the position been filled?" she asked. → She inquired whether the position had been filled. → She inquired of the registrar whether the position had been filled. (very formal) ✗ She inquired the registrar whether the position had been filled.

want to know and demand to know#

Both report a spoken question; demand to know adds impatience or a claim of entitlement. Neither takes a person object.

"When will I get my refund?" he asked. → He wanted to know when he would get his refund. → He demanded to know when he would get his refund.

query and question#

Query is business and academic vocabulary. Its object is the item being challenged, not the person, and with a clause it means "raise a doubt about."

"Is this charge right?" the auditor asked. → The auditor queried whether the charge was correct. → The auditor queried the charge. ( ✗ queried me whether )

Question splits into two unrelated patterns, and confusing them changes the meaning entirely. Question someone means to interrogate. Question whether means to doubt — it reports skepticism, not a question asked out loud.

Police questioned him for six hours. (interrogated) Several members questioned whether the plan was affordable. (doubted it — they were not asking)

Embedded questions in polite requests#

A question can be embedded inside another question, and English uses this constantly for politeness: Where is the station? is blunt, while Could you tell me where the station is? gives the hearer an easy exit ("Sorry, I don't know"). The embedded clause obeys every rule above — statement order, no do-support — while the outer clause is a real question and behaves like one.

Where is the station? → Could you tell me where the station is? ✗ Could you tell me where is the station?

When does the next train leave? → Do you know when the next train leaves? ✗ Do you know when does the next train leave?

The frame decides the punctuation#

The question mark belongs to the outer clause, not the inner one. If the frame is a question, the sentence ends in a question mark; if the frame is a statement, it ends in a period — even though the inner clause is a question in meaning both times.

Writing I was wondering if you had a minute? is a common informal habit in email — it signals a rising, tentative intonation — but it is not standard punctuation.

Politeness ladder#

The frames are not interchangeable in tone. Roughly, from most direct to most deferential:

Where's the station? (fine among friends; brusque to a stranger) Do you know where the station is? (neutral, everyday) Could you tell me where the station is? (polite, standard with strangers) I was wondering if you could tell me where the station is. (very deferential; the past continuous is doing the softening)

The same softening applies to yes/no requests, where if and whether both work and if is far more natural in speech: I was wondering if you're free Thursday sounds ordinary; I was wondering whether you are free Thursday sounds like a letter.

Question tags in reported speech#

A tag question — You're coming, aren't you? — does two things at once: it asks a yes/no question and it signals which answer the speaker expects. The tag itself cannot be reported. There is no way to hang aren't you onto a subordinate clause, so the tag is dropped and the sentence is reported as a plain yes/no question, with the expectation moved into the reporting verb or into a following comment if it matters.

DirectReported
"You're coming, aren't you?" she said.She asked whether I was coming. / She assumed I was coming.
"You didn't tell him, did you?" he said.He asked whether I had told him — and clearly hoped I hadn't.
"It's freezing, isn't it?" she said.She remarked that it was freezing. (falling tag, small talk — not a real question)
"That's the last one, right?" he said.He asked whether that was the last one. / He wanted confirmation that it was the last one.
"Shut the window, will you?" she said.She asked me to shut the window.
"Let's get lunch, shall we?" he said.He suggested that we get lunch.

Three of those rows are not questions at all, and each takes a different report:

  • A tag on a falling intonation, used for small talk or for seeking agreement (It's freezing, isn't it?), is a statement in disguise. Report it as a statement, with remark, observe, or point out.
  • A tag on an imperative (will you, would you, won't you) makes it a request. The report uses ask someone to do something — see Reported commands, requests, and advice.
  • A tag on let's (shall we) makes it a suggestion. The report uses suggest, propose, or recommend, and in American English the that-clause takes the bare subjunctive: He suggested that we get lunch, not that we got lunch and not the British that we should get lunch. See ask that is a request, not a question.

Only the genuine information-seeking tag — the one on a rising intonation, where the speaker really does want an answer — becomes a reported question. And in every case the tag itself is deleted: there is no reported form of aren't you, so any expectation it carried has to be restated in words or allowed to disappear.

Reported short answers#

A short answer — Yes, No, I didn't, I think so — is elliptical, and the ellipsis survives into the report. The rule is that the auxiliary has to stay: it is what the clause is standing on once the rest of the predicate is left out.

"Are you coming?" — "Yes." → I asked if he was coming and he said he was.

"Did you lock it?" — "No, I didn't." → She asked whether I had locked it, and I said I hadn't.

"Can you drive a stick?" — "Yes, I can." → He asked if I could drive a stick and I said I could.

"Have they replied?" — "No." → We asked whether they had replied and she said they hadn't.

He said he was is a complete sentence; he said he is not. This is the one place learners most often trim a word too many.

Bare yes and no report as themselves, with no that and no quotation marks needed:

"Is it open?" — "Yes." → I asked whether it was open and she said yes. ✗ … and she said that yes.

Stiff alternatives exist — she answered in the affirmative, she responded in the negative — and belong to legal transcripts and police reports, not to ordinary prose.

Pro-forms so and not carry over unchanged after think, hope, believe, guess, suppose, and be afraid:

"Will it rain?" — "I hope not." → I asked whether it would rain and he said he hoped not.

"Are they still open?" — "I think so." → She asked if they were still open and I said I thought so.

Be careful with say so, which is a different construction: He said so means "he asserted it," not "he said yes." And note the negation patterns: I don't think so and I hope not are the natural forms, while I don't hope so and I think not are not (the second is archaic and reads as arch).

Quick reference#

The transformation, in order.

  1. Choose the introducer: a wh-word if the question had one, otherwise if or whether.
  2. Put the subject back in front of the tensed verb.
  3. Delete interrogative do, does, did and move its tense onto the main verb.
  4. Backshift, unless the reporting verb is present or the situation is still true.
  5. Recalculate pronouns, then time words, then place words.
  6. Replace the question mark with a period — unless the outer clause is itself a question.

The four things that are never negotiable. Statement word order. No interrogative do. No that after ask in a question. No question mark on a statement frame.

The four things that genuinely vary. If versus whether where both are legal. Backshift on a still-true situation. Past simple versus past perfect for an already-past question. The finite clause versus the wh- + to-infinitive compression.

Practice#

Report each one the next day. Unless the question was only thought rather than asked out loud, the reporter is the person who was asked — so you comes back as I.

  1. "Do you have the file?" she asked.
  2. "Where did you put it?" he asked.
  3. "Who told you that?" she asked.
  4. "Who did you tell?" she asked.
  5. "Are you coming or not?" he asked.
  6. "Should I call her today?" she wondered.
  7. "How come you didn't say anything yesterday?" he asked.
  8. "You've already sent it, haven't you?" she asked.
  9. "Where are the restrooms?" — turn it into a polite request beginning Could you tell me …
  10. "Please be there by nine," she said. — report it with ask.
Answers
  1. She asked whether I had the file.
  2. He asked where I had put it.
  3. She asked who had told me that. (wh-subject — no inversion to undo)
  4. She asked who I had told. (wh-object — inversion and did both go)
  5. He asked whether or not I was coming. / He asked whether I was coming or not. (never if or not)
  6. She wondered whether to call her that day. / She wondered whether she should call her that day.
  7. He asked why I hadn't said anything the day before. (how come → why in writing; yesterday → the day before)
  8. She asked whether I had already sent it — the tag cannot be reported, so her expectation has to be added separately if it matters.
  9. Could you tell me where the restrooms are? (statement order inside; question mark on the outer clause)
  10. She asked that I be there by nine. (a request, so that + bare subjunctive — not that I should be, which is the British pattern)
