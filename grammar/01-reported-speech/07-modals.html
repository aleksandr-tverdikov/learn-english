Modal verbs in reported speech#

What happens to will, can, may, must, and the rest of the modal system when a quotation is turned into a report — which modals shift back, which ones have nowhere left to go, and why must is three different problems wearing one spelling.

← Reported speech · All grammar topics

Modals are the easiest part of backshift to learn and the easiest part to get subtly wrong. Easy, because the list is closed — nine core modals, a dozen semi-modals, and that is the whole inventory — and because half of them cannot move at all. Wrong, because the half that cannot move includes must, and must covers three unrelated meanings that behave three different ways.

The reason the system splits so cleanly is historical. Would, could, might, and should began life as the past tenses of will, can, may, and shall, and they still do that job in reported speech even though they long ago picked up independent present-day meanings. That gives English four ready-made backshift pairs. Every other modal is stuck: would, could, might, and should are already the past forms, so they have no past of their own; ought to, used to, and had better never had one; and must is historically the preterite of a verb (mōtan) that dropped out of the language, which is why it looks like a present tense but has no past behind it. When English needs a past must, it borrows one from a different verb entirely — had to.

Everything else about turning a quotation into a report — the reporting clause, the that-clause, the punctuation — is assumed here and set out in the basics. This file covers only what happens to the modal.

Two ground rules before the tables.

Modals never inflect. Nothing in this file adds -s, -ing, or -ed to a modal, because modals do not take those endings in any context. There is no ✗ he musted go, no ✗ she canned swim, no ✗ they shoulded wait, and no third-person -s: ✗ she cans, ✗ he musts. Backshift replaces one word with another word; it never conjugates one. See the modal section of the verbs file for the full set of restrictions.

Modals never stack. Because will and can cannot appear together, a sentence that needs both has to convert one of them into a semi-modal: ✗ He said he would can help → He said he would be able to help. This is the single most common structural error in reported speech with modals, and it comes up any time the original modal has to survive alongside a backshifted would.

The changing modals in detail#

will → would#

The most frequent single change in the whole topic, because will is the ordinary way of talking about the future and quoted people talk about the future constantly.

"I will email you the file," he said. → He said he would email me the file.

"The store will close at nine," she told us. → She told us the store would close at nine.

"You'll regret it," he warned her. → He warned her she'd regret it.

Time expressions usually move with the modal, since a future spoken about in the past is no longer measured from now. The full set of shifts is in pronouns, time, and place:

"I'll call you tomorrow," she said. → She said she would call me the next day.

"We'll be there next week," they said. → They said they would be there the following week.

Two very common cases let the reporting verb swallow the modal entirely. A first-person will is a promise, and a first-person won't is a refusal:

"I'll pay you back on Friday," he said. → He promised to pay me back on Friday.

"I won't sign it," she said. → She refused to sign it.

Both are more natural English than He said he would pay me back, which reports the words but throws away the commitment.

shall → should or would#

Shall is close to dead in American English. Outside legal drafting and the fixed Shall we?, an American almost never says it, so the backshift rules below matter mostly for reporting older texts, British speakers, and contracts. Which form you land on depends entirely on which shall you started with.

First-person future shall → would. This is the shall that simply means will.

"I shall be thirty in June," he said. → He said he would be thirty in June.

"We shall never forget this," the mayor said. → The mayor said they would never forget it.

Offers, suggestions, and requests for instructions → should. This is the shall of Shall I…? and Shall we…?, and it is a question, not a statement of future fact. It reports as should, inside an indirect question.

"Shall I help you with those?" he asked. → He asked if he should help me with those.

"Shall we start without her?" she said. → She asked whether we should start without her.

"What time shall I pick you up?" he asked. → He asked what time he should pick me up.

An American speaker producing these sentences fresh would almost certainly say Should I help you with those? to begin with — which backshifts to itself, since should does not change. The shall → should rule is really a rule for reporting other people's shall.

Often the cleaner report drops the modal and names the speech act instead:

"Shall I carry that?" he asked. → He offered to carry it.

"Shall we go?" she said. → She suggested that we go.

Legal and regulatory shall → had to, was to, or shall. In contracts and statutes shall means "is required to." Report it as an obligation, not as a future:

"The tenant shall pay rent on the first of the month," the lease said. → The lease said the tenant had to pay rent on the first of the month.

"Each applicant shall submit two references," the rule stated. → The rule stated that each applicant was to submit two references.

When the document is still in force, writers routinely leave shall alone — The lease says the tenant shall pay on the first — which is the still-true exception doing its usual work.

can → could#

Can backshifts to could in all three of its meanings, and the meaning carries over unchanged.

Ability:

"I can fix it myself," he said. → He said he could fix it himself.

Permission:

"You can borrow the car," Dad said. → Dad said I could borrow the car.

Possibility (what is generally possible):

"Winters here can get brutal," she said. → She said winters there could get brutal.

Requests are questions and usually report as a request, not a statement:

"Can you hold the door?" he asked. → He asked me to hold the door. / He asked if I could hold the door.

One useful subtlety: backshifted could is free of the restriction that normally blocks could for a single completed achievement. In ordinary past narrative you say I was able to open the jar, not ✗ I could open the jar, because could resists one-time success. Reported speech does not inherit that restriction, because the could there is a backshifted can, not a past-time could:

"I can get us tickets," he said. → He said he could get us tickets. (perfectly normal)

may → might (or could)#

May splits by meaning, and only one branch is genuinely alive in American speech.

Possibility → might. This is the common case.

"It may rain before noon," she said. → She said it might rain before noon.

"He may already know," they said. → They said he might already know.

Permission → could in practice, might in theory. Textbooks pair may with might here too, and The principal said we might leave is grammatical, but it sounds stiff and faintly archaic to an American ear, and it invites a possibility reading. Ordinary American English uses could or an explicit was allowed to:

"You may leave when you finish," the teacher said. → The teacher said we could leave when we finished. (natural)

"You may leave when you finish," the teacher said. → The teacher said we might leave when we finished. (formal, easily misread)

"Visitors may park in Lot C," the sign read. → The sign said visitors were allowed to park in Lot C. (unambiguous)

Prohibition (may not) → couldn't or weren't allowed to:

"You may not bring food into the lab," she said. → She said we couldn't bring food into the lab.

Note also that the possibility may very often does not backshift at all, because the possibility is usually still open at the moment of reporting. She said it may rain tomorrow is entirely idiomatic. See the still-true exception and the section below.

must → had to#

Must is the one modal whose backshift requires you to identify the meaning first, because only one of its three meanings changes.

Meaning of mustReported asDirect → reported pair
Obligation, requirementhad to"You must wear a badge," she said. → She said we had to wear a badge.
Deduction, near-certaintymust (no change)"You must be exhausted," he said. → He said I must be exhausted.
Prohibition (must not)must not (no change)"You mustn't tell anyone," she said. → She said I mustn't tell anyone.

One usage fact the table cannot show. Obligation must is formal and comparatively rare in ordinary American speech: Americans overwhelmingly say have to, need to, or got to, and reserve must for signage, written instructions, legal text, and a somewhat stern register. That matters twice over for reported speech. Most of the obligation you will actually have to report arrives as have to in the first place, and a report that reaches for must where the original said have to quietly raises the register of the sentence. Deduction must is the healthier of the two senses and is entirely current in American speech — You must be exhausted, That must be his sister, There must be a reason.

Obligation. Must meaning "is required to" has no past form of its own, so it borrows had to.

"You must be at the gate forty minutes early," the agent said. → The agent said we had to be at the gate forty minutes early.

"I must finish this before Monday," she said. → She said she had to finish it before Monday.

Deduction. Must meaning "the evidence says this is so" is not an obligation, and the reliable report keeps it exactly as it stands:

"That must be the mailman," she said. → She said that must be the mailman. ✓

"You must be exhausted," he said. → He said I must be exhausted. ✓

One honest complication here, because the usual textbook line is stated too strongly. American English does have an epistemic have to — There has to be a mistake, It has to be around here somewhere, You have to be kidding me — and it backshifts like any other verb, so She said that had to be the mailman is not ungrammatical, and an American will read it as a deduction, not as a duty imposed on the mail carrier. The reason to avoid had to in a report is ambiguity, not ungrammaticality. The moment the clause describes something the subject could plausibly be required to do, the obligation reading takes over and the report says something the speaker never said:

"He must be at the office," she said. → She said he must be at the office. ✓ (deduction: that is where she figures he is)

"He must be at the office," she said. → ✗ She said he had to be at the office. (now reads as a requirement he was under)

So the rule to carry away is still deduction must stays must. Where the predicate names a state nobody could be ordered into — be exhausted, be the mailman, be a mistake — had to will be understood, but it buys nothing and costs clarity. Where the predicate names something doable, had to actively changes the sentence.

Past deduction uses the perfect and never moves:

"They must have gotten lost," he said. → He said they must have gotten lost.

Prohibition. Mustn't keeps its shape. Alternatives exist if you want to avoid a modal that some readers find abrupt:

"You mustn't open that door," she said. → She said we mustn't open that door. / She said we weren't to open that door. / She said we weren't allowed to open that door.

Be careful with the negatives, which are not mirror images: mustn't is prohibition ("don't do it"), while don't have to is absence of obligation ("you may skip it"). They backshift differently and mean opposite things.

"You mustn't call him," she said. → She said I mustn't call him. (forbidden)

"You don't have to call him," she said. → She said I didn't have to call him. (optional)

have to → had to#

Have to is an ordinary verb wearing modal meaning, so it backshifts like any other verb: present → past.

"I have to pick up the kids," he said. → He said he had to pick up the kids.

"She has to work Thanksgiving," they said. → They said she had to work Thanksgiving.

"You don't have to decide today," she said. → She said I didn't have to decide that day.

Since obligation must also lands on had to, the reported form is ambiguous about which word was spoken. Nothing important is lost — had to is a faithful report of both — but it means you cannot reconstruct the original from the report.

Have got to collapses into had to as well. American English has no ✗ had got to:

"I've got to get out of here," he said. → He said he had to get out of there.

be going to → was/were going to#

The be carries the tense, so this behaves like an ordinary present → past change.

"I'm going to ask for a raise," she said. → She said she was going to ask for a raise.

"They're going to tear down the old gym," he said. → He said they were going to tear down the old gym.

Was/were going to is also the standard way to report a plan that later fell through, which is why it so often carries an unspoken "but didn't": He said he was going to call. If you need to avoid that implication, would is neutral.

The modals that stay put#

Why they cannot move#

Would, could, might, and should are the past forms. They already carry the marking backshift would add, and English has no second layer of past marking for a modal. Ought to, used to, had better, would rather, and needn't are frozen phrases with no past form at all. Where real past-time meaning is needed, English reaches for the perfect infinitive (should have called), not for a new modal form.

"I would help if I had the time," he said. → He said he would help if he had the time.

"You could always take the train," she said. → She said I could always take the train.

"It might be closed on Sundays," he said. → He said it might be closed on Sundays.

"You should call your landlord," she said. → She said I should call my landlord.

"We ought to leave by six," he said. → He said we ought to leave by six.

"I used to teach high school," she said. → She said she used to teach high school.

"You'd better get that in writing," he said. → He said I'd better get that in writing.

"I'd rather not talk about it," she said. → She said she'd rather not talk about it.

"You needn't apologize," he said. → He said I needn't apologize.

A word on needn't: it is grammatical American English but genuinely rare in American speech, where don't have to does the job. Most Americans reporting "You needn't wait" will say He said I didn't have to wait, and that is the version worth learning to produce. The same goes for oughtn't, which almost every American replaces with shouldn't.

Modal + perfect: the end of the line#

A modal followed by have + past participle is already anchored in the past. Backshift has nothing left to do, and every one of these forms reports unchanged.

Only the two present-tense forms in that list move — may have drifts toward might have, and can't have toward couldn't have — and both changes are optional in practice.

There is also a one-way door here: a present modal that backshifts does not pick up a perfect. ✗ He said he would have helped is not the report of "I will help." That sentence reports "I would have helped," a counterfactual about the past. Adding have changes the meaning, not just the tense.

"I will help," he said. → He said he would help. ✓

"I will help," he said. → ✗ He said he would have helped.

The one exception is a perfect that was already in the original. Will have finished is will plus a perfect infinitive, so backshifting the will leaves the perfect exactly where it was, and would have finished is the faithful report:

"I'll have finished by noon," she said. → She said she would have finished by noon. ✓

That is the only context in which would have correctly reports a will, and it is genuinely ambiguous in isolation with the counterfactual she would have finished (if…). Where the difference matters, rephrase: She said she would be finished by noon, or She said she would have it finished by noon.

Tricky cases#

must: identify the meaning before you touch it#

Restated as a decision procedure, because this is where most errors happen:

  1. Can you replace must with am/is/are required to? Then it is obligation → had to.
  2. Can you replace it with I'm certain that… or the evidence says…? Then it is deduction → must, unchanged.
  3. Is it must not? Then it is prohibition → must not, unchanged.

Only external obligation forces had to. A one-time requirement imposed by a person, a rule, or a circumstance is exactly the case:

"You must sign in at the front desk," the guard said. → The guard said we had to sign in at the front desk.

Where the obligation is the speaker's own felt necessity rather than an outside rule, both forms are found, though had to is far more common in American English:

"I must call my mother tonight," he said. → He said he had to call his mother that night. (ordinary) / He said he must call his mother that night. (formal, literary)

And where the requirement is a standing rule still in force at the moment of reporting, must commonly stays put:

"Passengers must remain seated," the announcement said. → The announcement said passengers must remain seated. / … said passengers had to remain seated.

can: ability, permission, and possibility#

All three backshift to could, so the form gives you no trouble. The trouble is that the report is now ambiguous in a way the original was not, and the repair differs by meaning.

Meaning of canPlain backshiftUnambiguous alternative
AbilityHe said he could swim a mile.He said he was able to swim a mile.
PermissionShe said I could use the copier.She said I was allowed to use the copier.
PossibilityHe said the road could flood.He said the road sometimes flooded.
RequestShe asked if I could drive.She asked me to drive.

The ambiguity worth naming: He said he could fix it might report "I can fix it" (ability, backshifted) or "I could fix it" (a hedged offer, unchanged). English gives you no way to tell them apart, and neither does a native speaker; context does all the work. When the difference matters, use was able to for the first and would be able to or might be able to for the second.

may: permission versus possibility#

The two mays diverge in how strongly they resist backshift.

DirectReported
Possibility"I may be late," she said.She said she might be late. — or, if she still might be, She said she may be late.
Permission"You may go," he said.He said I could go. (natural) / He said I might go. (stiff)

Formal offer"May I take your coat?" he asked.He asked if he could take my coat. / He offered to take my coat.

Permission may is formal to begin with — in American speech it survives mostly in service encounters (May I help you?), signage, and school. Because it is formal, reporting it with could is not a loss of register so much as a return to normal English, and the "correct" might is the version that sounds wrong.

The dare row is a fair picture of the American situation: the modal dare (daren't, dare not) is essentially a literary and British form, and an American reports it with the ordinary verb, didn't dare. Had best is in the same neighborhood — grammatical, understood, and much rarer than had better in American speech.

Two rows are worth separating because they look alike and behave differently. Might as well is frozen and cannot move, since might is already a past form. May as well is its live-modal twin, so the may in it backshifts to might and the two expressions converge on a single reported form.

The double-modal problem#

Because two modals cannot sit side by side, any report that would produce a stack has to convert one of them into a semi-modal. The pattern comes up whenever a future in the past has to carry an ability, a permission, or an obligation along with it:

WantedImpossibleCorrect
future ability✗ will can / ✗ would can"I'll be able to finish it by Friday," he said. → He said he would be able to finish it by Friday.
future obligation✗ will must / ✗ would must"You must re-apply next year," she said. → She said I would have to re-apply the next year.
future permission✗ would may"You may join us later," he said. → He said I would be allowed to join them later.

Note what the first row is not saying. A plain can backshifts to a plain could and needs no repair at all — "I can finish it by Friday" reports perfectly well as He said he could finish it by Friday. The semi-modal is needed only where the original itself paired futurity with an ability, a permission, or an obligation, because those two meanings cannot ride on two stacked modals in the direct version either. English blocks the stack at the source; backshift just moves the problem into the report.

American English does have a genuine double modal — might could, might should, used to could — in Southern and South Midland speech (I might could help you with that). It is a real feature of a real dialect, not an error in speech, but it is nonstandard in writing and does not belong in a report unless you are quoting the dialect.

Modals in reported questions and commands#

A modal in a question keeps the change rules above and additionally loses the question word order, since the reported clause is a statement. The word-order rule, the if/whether choice, and the disappearing do-support all belong to reported questions; only the modal part is treated here.

"Can you swim?" she asked. → She asked if I could swim.

"Should I wait?" he asked. → He asked whether he should wait.

"When will they arrive?" she asked. → She asked when they would arrive.

"Must I answer that?" he asked. → He asked if he had to answer that.

Note the last one. Must in a question is almost always obligation rather than deduction, so it takes had to — and He asked if he must answer that is stiff where the had to version is ordinary.

Modal requests and orders often report as an infinitive instead, which removes the modal entirely. That construction, and the verbs that license it, are covered in reported commands and requests.

"Will you close the window?" she said. → She asked me to close the window.

"Could you hold this a second?" he asked. → He asked me to hold it a second.

"You must leave immediately," he said. → He ordered me to leave immediately.

Converting to an infinitive is usually the better report for a request, because a literal modal report of a polite question makes the politeness disappear: She asked if I would close the window is accurate but flat, and She asked me to close the window is what an English speaker would actually write.

The mandative subjunctive: American be, British should be#

This is the one place where a modal appears in the British sentence and simply is not present in the American one. After a verb or adjective of demanding, requiring, recommending, proposing, or insisting, the verb in the that clause is a bare base form in American English — no -s, no to, no modal. British English has traditionally used should plus the infinitive instead.

AmericanBritish (traditional)
Direct"I insist that he be present," she said."I insist that he should be present," she said.
ReportedShe insisted that he be present.She insisted that he should be present.

Neither version backshifts. The reporting verb moves — insist → insisted — while the clause verb sits still, because a subjunctive has no tense to shift and should has no past form to shift into:

"I insist that she be notified," he said. → He insisted that she be notified. ✓

"We demand that the fee be waived," they said. → They demanded that the fee be waived. ✓

"I recommend that he not sign it," she said. → She recommended that he not sign it. ✓ (bare not, no do)

Two traps for an American writer. First, should in these clauses is not ungrammatical in American English, but it is a marked choice that reads as British, and it is ambiguous in a way the bare form is not: He suggested that she should apply can be heard as ordinary advice rather than as a formal proposal, while He suggested that she apply can only be the proposal. Second, the British indicative — insisted that he was present, recommended that she applies — is not a stylistic variant of the subjunctive in American English but a different sentence, reporting an assertion rather than a demand:

She insisted that he be present. (she demanded his attendance)

She insisted that he was present. (she claimed, against objection, that he had been there)

The form itself is set out at the mandative subjunctive and its behavior under reporting at the subjunctive does not shift.

Modal backshift and the still-true exception#

When not to backshift sets out the general principle: when the reported content is still true, still relevant, or still in the future at the moment of reporting, the speaker may leave the tense alone. Modals participate fully, with three qualifications specific to them.

Backshift is always safe#

Non-backshift is the option; backshift is the default. Every one of these pairs is correct, and the backshifted version is correct in every context in which the non-backshifted version is correct:

"I can speak Portuguese," he said. → He said he can speak Portuguese. ✓ / He said he could speak Portuguese. ✓

"It may rain tomorrow," she said (this morning). → She said it may rain tomorrow. ✓ / She said it might rain tomorrow. ✓

Tomorrow survives in both of those because the report is being made the same day; report it a week later and the time word has to move to the next day whether or not the modal does.

If you are unsure, backshift. The reverse is not true: leaving the modal in place when the situation has moved on produces a sentence that is genuinely wrong.

Where the present-tense modal commonly stays#

CaseExample
Future event still in the future"I'll be at the meeting Thursday," she said (on Monday). → She said she'll be at the meeting Thursday.
Permanent or long-lasting ability"I can read Latin," he said. → He said he can read Latin.
Possibility still open"The flight may be delayed," she said. → She said the flight may be delayed.
Standing rule still in force"You must be twenty-one to enter," the sign said. → The sign said you must be twenty-one to enter.
Deduction the speaker still endorses"They must be stuck in traffic," he said. → He said they must be stuck in traffic.
Advice still applicable"You should get a second opinion," she said. → She said I should get a second opinion. (should never changes anyway)
Immediate relay, seconds after the words were spoken"I'll be down in five minutes," she said. → She said she'll be down in five minutes.

In casual American speech the non-backshifted version is extremely common for anything reported within a few minutes of being said — He just said he'll meet us there is what people actually produce, and correcting it to would sounds like a schoolbook. The textbook rule is not wrong, but it describes careful writing better than it describes conversation.

It is worth being blunt about how far this goes, since ESL material tends to present backshift as compulsory. In recorded American conversation, unshifted will, can, and may after a past reporting verb are not errors, not regionalisms, and not sloppiness — they are the majority pattern whenever the content is still live. The same holds for the tenses generally: a past simple in the original very often stays a past simple rather than climbing to a past perfect. Both facts are treated at length in when not to backshift, and the underlying principle — that backshift is available rather than compulsory — is set out in backshift is available, not compulsory. What this file adds is the modal-specific point: because four of the five shifting modals have a live present form and a live past form, the choice between them is visible in every sentence, and a reader will read it as meaningful.

Where the present-tense modal cannot stay#

CaseWrongRight
The future event has already passed✗ She said she'll be at Monday's meeting. (spoken on Wednesday)She said she would be at Monday's meeting.
Historical or narrative report✗ In 1962 Kennedy said the country will go to the moon.In 1962 Kennedy said the country would go to the moon.
A single completed obligation✗ He said he must show his ID at the door last night.He said he had to show his ID at the door last night.
The ability is gone✗ She said she can run a six-minute mile (before the injury).She said she could run a six-minute mile.
The speaker is distancing themselves✗ He claimed the check will clear (it never did).He claimed the check would clear.
Past-tense narrative fiction✗ She told him she can't stay.She told him she couldn't stay.

The last row is worth stating separately. Inside a story told in the past tense, reported speech backshifts as a matter of convention regardless of whether the content is still true, because the entire narrative frame is past. Non-backshift there reads as a mistake, not as a stylistic choice.

must is the odd one out#

The still-true exception interacts with must differently from every other modal, because must → had to is not backshift in the ordinary sense. The other four pairs replace a present modal with its own past form. Must has no past form, so English substitutes a different verb, and substitution is a stronger move than tense marking. Two consequences:

A standing rule keeps must more readily than other modals keep their present forms. The sign said you must be twenty-one is not merely acceptable, it is the more natural report, because had to would suggest the rule has lapsed.

A specific past obligation requires had to even when the general rule behind it is still true. The rule about badges may still be in force, but the moment you report a particular occasion, you need the past form:

"You must show your badge to get in," the guard said (last Tuesday). → The guard told me I had to show my badge to get in. ✓

✗ The guard told me I must show my badge to get in last Tuesday.

And because deduction must never changes at all, the exception is simply irrelevant to it. He said they must be stuck in traffic is the report whether or not they are still stuck, and there is no second version to choose between — the had to alternative discussed above is a different construction rather than a backshift of this one.

Practice#

Report each line. Answers follow; the pronouns and the time and place words have to move along with the modal.

  1. "I will meet you here tomorrow," she said.
  2. "You must be joking," he said.
  3. "You must hand this in by Friday," the professor said (last spring).
  4. "I can finish it tonight," he said.
  5. "Shall I wait for you?" she asked.
  6. "You may use the back entrance," the guard said.
  7. "We've got to leave now," they said.
  8. "I should have called first," he admitted.
  9. "You needn't worry about it," she said.
  10. "I insist that she be told," he said.

Answers.

  1. She said she would meet me there the next day. (will → would, you → me, here → there, tomorrow → the next day)
  2. He said I must be joking. (deduction; never ✗ had to be joking)
  3. The professor said we had to hand it in by that Friday. (a closed occasion, so had to, not must)
  4. He said he could finish it that night.
  5. She asked whether she should wait for me. (offer shall → should, statement word order)
  6. The guard said I could use the back entrance. — or, unambiguously, said I was allowed to use it.
  7. They said they had to leave right then. (never ✗ had got to)
  8. He admitted he should have called first. (a perfect modal cannot move)
  9. She said I needn't worry about it. — more naturally, said I didn't have to worry about it.
  10. He insisted that she be told. (the reporting verb shifts; the subjunctive does not)
