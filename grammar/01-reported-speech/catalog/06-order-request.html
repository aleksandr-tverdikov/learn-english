Ordering, requesting, and forbidding#

The verbs that report one person telling another what to do — or what not to do.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

These are the verbs that report a directive: an utterance whose whole point was to get somebody to act. Almost all of them run one frame — verb + person + to-infinitive, negated as not to — so what separates them is not structure but force, from begged me to at one end, through asked me to in the middle, to ordered me to and commanded me to at the other. The exceptions are worth learning as a block: demand, decree, mandate, and stipulate refuse a personal object and take a that-clause with the bare subjunctive (demanded that he leave, never ✗ demanded him to leave); the prohibition verbs prohibit, ban, and bar take from plus an -ing form and refuse the infinitive outright; and let — with literary bid beside it — takes a bare infinitive with no to. Everything else in the group is a question of tone, and choosing the tone accurately is most of the work.

The force scale#

The frame is identical down the whole column. Only the verb changes, and the verb carries everything.

ForceVerbsOriginal sounds likeReport
Desperate pleabeg, implore, plead with, beseech, entreat"Please — I'm asking you."She begged me to stay.
Formal petitionpetition, appeal to, request"We respectfully ask that you reconsider."They petitioned the board to reconsider.
Neutral requestask"Could you stay?"She asked me to stay.
Reminder, nudgeremind, prompt"Don't forget to stay for the vote."She reminded me to stay.
Persistent pressurepress, push, nag, urge, exhort, call on"Come on, stay."She pressed me to stay.
Non-negotiabledemand, insist on, require"You're staying."She demanded that I stay.
Instruction with authoritytell, instruct, direct, charge, summon"Stay."She told me to stay.
Absolute authorityorder, command, decree, mandate, bid, adjure"You will stay."She ordered me to stay.
Permissionlet, allow, permit, authorize"You can stay."She let me stay.
Prohibitionforbid, prohibit, ban, bar, proscribe, enjoin"You may not stay."She forbade me to stay.

The four frames#

Every verb in this file takes one or more of these. The Patterns line on each entry says which.

FrameNotationVerbs that take itExample
Person plus infinitiveV + sb + to-infinitivethe majority — order, tell, ask, beg, urge, remind, allow…He told me to wait.
Bare subjunctive clauseV + that-clausedemand, require, request, order, decree, mandate, stipulate, insistHe demanded that I wait.
Preposition plus gerundV + sb + from + -ingprohibit, ban, bar, forbid, enjoin (in its negative sense)The rule bars me from waiting there.
Bare infinitiveV + sb + bare infinitivelet, and literary bidHe let me wait.

Two of these are where errors cluster. Verbs in the second row take no personal object at all before the clause — ✗ demanded me to wait — and verbs in the third row take no infinitive at all — ✗ prohibited me to wait. The subjunctive in row two is the bare base form for every subject, and it never backshifts: He demanded that she be there, not was there, even under a past reporting verb. Full grammar in Reported commands and requests.

British English puts should in that clause, and it is not an error. They demanded that he should resign, the court directed that the exhibits should be returned, and she insisted that I should see it are ordinary British usage, common in British newspapers and in older writing on both sides of the Atlantic. American English drops the should and leaves the bare form. Every subjunctive-taking verb in this file — demand, require, request, order, command, decree, mandate, stipulate, insist, urge, ask, petition, direct, instruct, enjoin, exhort, beg, beseech, implore, entreat, forbid — behaves the same way. Read should as British, not as wrong; write the bare form in American prose. What is wrong in both dialects is the plain indicative in a directive clause: ✗ demanded that he resigns.

adjure#

Pronunciation
/əˈdʒʊr/ · uh-JOOR
Русский
заклинать — торжественно требовать во имя святого или под клятвой; книжн., устар.
Type
reporting verb — solemn command
Register
formal, archaic; legal and biblical
Patterns
adjure + sb + to-infinitive · adjure + sb + that-clause (archaic) · adjure + sb + by + noun phrase (adjured him by all he held sacred)
Never
adjure someone from doing something — the from + -ing frame belongs to prohibit and bar. adjure to do something with no object is also impossible: somebody is always being sworn.
Reports
a command laid on someone under oath or in the name of something sacred — it sits between command and implore and carries an appeal to conscience that neither of its neighbors has. Unlike a plain order, it presumes the hearer wants to do right.

"In the name of everything you believe in, tell me the truth." → He adjured me to tell the truth. "Swear you will not repeat this." → She adjured us not to repeat it.

  1. The witness was adjured to answer truthfully or not at all.
  2. He adjured them to remember what the founders had risked.
  3. The high priest adjured him to say whether the charge was true.
  4. In the last paragraph she adjures her readers to check the primary sources themselves.
  5. Nobody had adjured me to secrecy; I simply kept quiet.
  6. She adjured him by the memory of his mother not to sell the farm.
See also
beseech, command, entreat, charge, implore

allow#

Pronunciation
/əˈlaʊ/ · uh-LOW
Русский
разрешать, позволять — просто не возражать; слабее permit и authorize
Type
reporting verb — permission
Register
neutral
Patterns
allow + sb + to-infinitive · allow + -ing (no person named: allows smoking) · allow + noun phrase · allow + sb + noun phrase (allowed us ten minutes) · passive be allowed to (very common) · allow for + noun phrase (= make room for, a different verb: allow for traffic) · allow + that-clause (a different verb — see below)
Never
allow that you park here as a grant of permission. Permission never takes a that-clause in English. Repairs: allows you to park here, allows parking here. And allow someone doing something — the gerund frame drops the person entirely.
Two verbs
allow + that-clause is a concession, not a permission — I'll allow that he had a point means "I concede." It reports agreement, not authorization, and it does not take the subjunctive: allow that he had a point, not have.
Reports
permission as the withdrawal of an objection — weaker than permit, which is institutional, and than authorize, which requires someone with formal power. Its negative is the everyday way English forbids things: They don't allow dogs in here.

"You can use my car." → She allowed me to use her car. "Dogs are fine on the patio." → The restaurant allows dogs on the patio.

  1. My parents never allowed us to eat in the living room.
  2. The airline allows one carry-on and one personal item.
  3. Her visa allowed her to stay another ninety days.
  4. The building doesn't allow subletting under any circumstances.
  5. The judge allowed the question, over the objection.
  6. I'll allow that the timing looks bad, but nothing was hidden.
See also
permit, let, authorize, forbid, prohibit

appeal to#

Pronunciation
/əˈpil tə/ · uh-PEEL tuh
Русский
обращаться с призывом к — публично просить тех, кто не обязан подчиняться
Type
reporting verb — urgent public request
Register
neutral to formal; journalistic
Patterns
appeal to + sb + to-infinitive · appeal to + sb + for + noun phrase · appeal for + noun phrase (no addressee named) · appeal to + sb (bare: appealed to the crowd)
Never
appeal someone to do something — the preposition to is not optional before the person. Repairs: appealed to him to come forward, or switch verbs: urged him to come forward.
American note
in the courtroom sense, American English makes appeal transitive — she appealed the ruling. British English keeps the preposition — appealed against the ruling. In American writing, ✗ appealed against the verdict reads as an import.
Reports
an urgent request broadcast to people who are under no obligation to comply — the verb of press conferences, open letters, and relief campaigns. It has no authority behind it at all, which is exactly what distinguishes it from demand and call on.

"If anyone saw anything that night, please come forward." → Police appealed to witnesses to come forward. "We need volunteers, urgently." → The shelter appealed for volunteers.

  1. The mayor appealed to residents to conserve water through the weekend.
  2. Aid groups have appealed for another forty million dollars.
  3. She appealed to her brother for help with the mortgage.
  4. Both sides appealed to the union to return to the table.
  5. He appealed to the crowd's better instincts and got nowhere.
  6. The family appealed to anyone with information to call the tip line.
See also
call on, urge, plead, beg, petition

ask#

Pronunciation
/æsk/ · ASK
Русский
просить — нейтральная просьба без оттенков; в другом значении «спрашивать»
Type
reporting verb — request (also the neutral question verb)
Register
neutral
Patterns
ask + sb + to-infinitive · ask + to-infinitive (the subject acts) · ask + (sb) + for + noun phrase · ask + that-clause (subjunctive; formal) · ask + sb + wh-clause · ask + sb + if/whether-clause · ask + sb + noun phrase (asked me a question) · ask + sb + about + noun phrase
Never
ask to someone to do something — the addressee takes no preposition. And ask for to do something — for introduces a noun, never an infinitive: asked for the check, not ✗ asked for to see the check.
The object flips the subject
this is the one thing about ask that must be right. She asked to leave means she leaves; She asked me to leave means I do. No other verb in this file makes the distinction so cheaply.
Reports
the unmarked request — neutral in force, neutral in register, and silent about how politely the original was phrased. "Close the window," "Could you close the window?" and "I hate to ask, but would it be possible to close the window?" all come out as She asked me to close the window; if the manner mattered, use an adverb or a stronger verb.

"Could you close the window?" → She asked me to close the window. "Can I leave early today?" → She asked to leave early that day.

  1. He asked me to water his plants while he was in Tulsa.
  2. She asked to speak to whoever was in charge.
  3. I asked the waiter for a glass of tap water.
  4. We ask that all phones be silenced during the performance.
  5. Nobody asked him to do any of this.
  6. She asked whether the position was still open.
See also
request, beg, tell, order, demand

authorize#

Pronunciation
/ˈɔθəraɪz/ · AW-thuh-ryze
Русский
уполномочивать, санкционировать — давать право властью должности; офиц.
Type
reporting verb — formal permission
Register
formal, institutional
Patterns
authorize + sb + to-infinitive · authorize + noun phrase (authorized the payment) · passive be authorized to (very common)
Never
authorize to release the funds with nothing in between — authorize always takes an object, either the person empowered or the thing approved. And authorize someone doing something — no gerund frame.
Spelling
American English writes authorize, always. Authorise is the British and Commonwealth spelling — a dialect variant rather than an error, and note that Oxford British style prefers -ize too. In American prose, write authorize.
Reports
permission conferred by someone whose office gives them the power to confer it. allow removes an objection; authorize creates a right, and it implies a record — a signature, a resolution, a memo. Its passive is where it lives: You are not authorized to be in this room.

"You may release the funds." → The board authorized us to release the funds. "The transfer is approved." → She authorized the transfer.

  1. Congress authorized the agency to issue subpoenas.
  2. Only two people are authorized to sign checks over ten thousand dollars.
  3. Her supervisor authorized the overtime in writing.
  4. Nobody authorized this purchase, and now it's on the audit.
  5. The warrant authorized them to search the garage but not the house.
  6. I'm not authorized to discuss an open investigation.
See also
permit, allow, let, mandate, direct

ban#

Pronunciation
/bæn/ · BAN
Русский
запрещать — официальный запрет деятельности властью или организацией
Type
reporting verb — official prohibition
Register
neutral; journalistic
Patterns
ban + noun phrase · ban + -ing · ban + sb + from + -ing · ban + sb + from + noun phrase (banned him from the building)
Never
ban someone to do something — there is no infinitive frame at all. Repairs: banned him from driving, banned driving, banned drivers from using phones.
Reports
the suppression of an activity by a government, an institution, or a governing body — the activity is the target, and the people affected are whoever happens to be doing it. Contrast bar, which targets a person, and forbid, which is what one person says to another.

"No one may fly drones in the park." → The city banned drones from the park. "You are not to set foot in this store again." → The manager banned him from the store.

  1. Several states have banned the sale of flavored vaping products.
  2. The league banned him for the rest of the season.
  3. Our building banned smoking on the balconies in 2019.
  4. The country banned imports of beef from three provinces.
  5. She was banned from the online forum for a week.
  6. You can't ban a book from a public library that easily.
See also
bar, prohibit, forbid, proscribe, enjoin

bar#

Pronunciation
/bɑr/ · BAR
Русский
не допускать, отстранять — запрет, закрывающий доступ конкретному человеку; юр.
Type
reporting verb — prohibition by exclusion
Register
neutral to formal; legal
Patterns
bar + sb + from + -ing · bar + sb + from + noun phrase · bar + noun phrase (legal: the statute bars recovery)
Never
bar someone to do something, and bar someone doing something without from. The preposition is obligatory whenever a person is named.
Reports
prohibition achieved by shutting someone out — of a place, an office, a profession, a remedy. ban abolishes an activity; bar excludes a person from it. In legal writing it is also the verb for a rule that forecloses a claim: The statute of limitations bars the suit.

"You are not to enter the courtroom." → The judge barred him from entering the courtroom. "Former employees may not bid on these contracts." → The rule bars former employees from bidding.

  1. The state barred him from practicing medicine for five years.
  2. Security barred us from the loading dock without a badge.
  3. A conflict of interest bars her from voting on the measure.
  4. Nothing in the lease bars tenants from keeping a cat.
  5. The court held that the settlement barred any further claims.
  6. He was barred from the building the same afternoon he was fired.
See also
ban, prohibit, forbid, enjoin, proscribe

beg#

Pronunciation
/bɛɡ/ · BEG
Русский
умолять, упрашивать — просить от безысходности, не имея никаких рычагов
Type
reporting verb — desperate request
Register
neutral, emotionally marked
Patterns
beg + sb + to-infinitive · beg + to-infinitive (the subject acts) · beg + (sb) + for + noun phrase · beg + of + sb (formal and dated: I beg of you) · beg + that-clause (subjunctive; literary)
Never
beg to someone to do something — no preposition before the person. Compare plead, which requires one.

The object flips the subject, exactly as with ask: He begged to differ — he differs. He begged me to reconsider — I reconsider.

Reports
a request made from need, with the speaker's dignity spent — the bottom of the force scale. Reporting a request as begged is a strong editorial claim about the original: it says the speaker had no leverage.

"Please, please don't tell him." → She begged me not to tell him. "I'm asking you — one more week." → He begged for another week.

  1. She begged her landlord to hold the apartment until Friday.
  2. He begged to be excused from the table.
  3. The dog begs for scraps every single night.
  4. I begged him not to put it in writing.
  5. They begged the committee for one more hearing.
  6. I beg to differ with almost everything in that paragraph.
See also
implore, plead, beseech, entreat, ask

beseech#

Pronunciation
/bɪˈsitʃ/ · bih-SEECH
Русский
молить, умолять — книжн., литург.: высокий стиль beg
Type
reporting verb — humble plea
Register
formal, literary, liturgical
Patterns
beseech + sb + to-infinitive · beseech + sb + for + noun phrase · beseech + that-clause (archaic)
Never
beseech to someone — like beg and unlike plead, it takes a bare object. And beseech someone doing something.
Past tense
besought and beseeched are both correct. Besought is the older form and still the one in scripture and poetry; beseeched is more common in modern American prose and is what to write if you are not aiming for an archaic effect.
Reports
an urgent, humbled plea — beg transposed into a liturgical or high-literary key. Almost nobody uses it of an ordinary conversation; using it of one is a deliberate exaggeration.

"I implore you — spare the city." → He besought them to spare the city. "Hear our prayer." → The congregation beseeches God to hear their prayer.

  1. She beseeched the governor to grant a stay of execution.
  2. In the letter he besought his brother for forgiveness.
  3. We beseech thee, O Lord, to look upon this house.
  4. The ambassador beseeched both capitals to keep the line open.
  5. I beseeched him — half joking, half not — to stop sending me drafts at midnight.
  6. The petition beseeches the council to reconsider the closure.
See also
implore, entreat, beg, adjure, plead

bid#

Pronunciation
/bɪd/ · BID
Русский
велеть, повелеть — устар., книжн.; берёт инфинитив без to (bade her enter)
Type
reporting verb — command
Register
literary, archaic
Patterns
bid + sb + bare infinitive (bade her enter) · bid + sb + to-infinitive (older and rarer) · bid + sb + noun phrase (bade them farewell)
Never
bid that she leave — in its command sense, modern bid takes no that-clause. And bid to someone to do something.
Forms
past bade, pronounced /bæd/ · BAD in traditional American usage, though /beɪd/ · BAYD is widespread and unremarkable. Past participle bidden. The auction and card-game verb is a different word with a regular past — bid, bid.
Bare infinitive
bid is one of the very few directive verbs to drop to — He bade her sit down, not ✗ He bade her to sit down in current literary use. The only other verb in this file that does it is let.
Reports
a command in a raised, old-fashioned register — the verb of scripture, fairy tales, and historical fiction. In everyday American English it survives mainly in bid farewell and do as you're bidden.

"Come in." → He bade her enter. "Say nothing to anyone." → She bade us hold our tongues.

  1. The king bade the messenger rise.
  2. She bade us farewell at the platform and did not look back.
  3. He did as he was bidden and asked no questions.
  4. The innkeeper bade them come in out of the rain.
  5. Custom bids us wait until the eldest is seated.
  6. I bid you good night.

call on#

Pronunciation
/ˈkɔl ɑn/ · KAWL awn (also call upon /ˈkɔl əˈpɑn/ · KAWL uh-PAHN)
Русский
призывать — публично требовать действий от того, кого нельзя принудить
Type
reporting verb — public demand
Register
formal; journalistic and diplomatic
Patterns
call on + sb + to-infinitive · call on + sb + for + noun phrase · call for + noun phrase (no addressee: called for calm) · call for + -ing (called for tightening the rules)
Never
call someone to resign — without on, the verb means "telephone," and the sentence changes meaning entirely. call on someone doing something is impossible too.
Reports
a demand made in public, addressed to someone with the power to act, by someone with no power to compel them. It is the standard verb of resolutions, statements, and editorials. Call upon is the same verb, one register higher.
Second sense
call on someone also means to pay a visit, or to invite a student to speak — The professor called on me first. Context separates them without difficulty.

"The minister must resign." → The opposition called on the minister to resign. "There should be an immediate ceasefire." → The resolution called for an immediate ceasefire.

  1. Three senators called on the agency to release the full report.
  2. The union called for a strike vote by the end of the month.
  3. She called upon her colleagues to put the vote off a week.
  4. The editorial calls on the mayor to apologize publicly.
  5. Nobody called for his resignation, but nobody defended him either.
  6. He called on us for a decision before the market opened.
See also
appeal to, urge, demand, press, exhort

charge#

Pronunciation
/tʃɑrdʒ/ · CHARJ
Русский
поручать, возлагать обязанность — торжественное наставление; офиц., юр.
Type
reporting verb — formal instruction assigning a duty
Register
formal; legal and ceremonial
Patterns
charge + sb + to-infinitive · charge + sb + with + -ing (charged him with organizing the drive) · charge + sb + with + noun phrase · passive be charged with (the commonest form) · charge + that-clause (a different verb — see below)
Never
charge someone that they do something — the directive verb takes no that-clause, and no personal object may stand in front of one. Repairs: charged him to finish it, charged him with finishing it. And charge someone to do something when you mean accuse: the infinitive frame only ever assigns a duty.

Three verbs wearing one spelling. The complement decides which one you have written, and they are not near neighbors:

FrameReportsExample
charge + sb + to-infinitivea solemn instructionThe judge charged the jury to weigh it carefully.
charge + sb + with + -ing / nouna duty assignedShe charged him with running the fundraiser.
charge + that-clausean accusationThe complaint charges that the company knew.

The middle row is ambiguous outside context — charged him with theft accuses, charged him with the accounts assigns — and only the third row is the accusation verb proper, which never takes a personal object before its clause: ✗ charged him that the company knew.

Reports
a solemn instruction that hands somebody a responsibility — a judge to a jury, an outgoing officer to a successor, a commencement speaker to a graduating class. It is instruct with ceremony attached.

"Consider only the evidence you heard in this room." → The judge charged the jury to consider only the evidence. "You'll be running the fundraiser this year." → She charged him with running the fundraiser.

  1. The judge charged the jurors to disregard the last exchange entirely.
  2. The commission was charged with reviewing every permit issued since 2020.
  3. He charged his successor to finish what the department had started.
  4. She charged us to keep the news to ourselves until Monday.
  5. The task force is charged with producing recommendations by spring.
  6. In his last letter he charged them to look after their mother.
See also
instruct, direct, command, enjoin, adjure

command#

Pronunciation
/kəˈmænd/ · kuh-MAND
Русский
приказывать, повелевать — приказ, ослушаться которого нельзя; воен., книжн.
Type
reporting verb — absolute order
Register
formal; military and literary
Patterns
command + sb + to-infinitive · command + that-clause (subjunctive) · command + noun phrase (commanded silence)
Never
command to advance with no object — somebody must be commanded. And command that he advances — the that-clause takes the bare subjunctive, not the indicative.
Subjunctive
command that takes the base form for every subject and never backshifts: He commanded that the gates be shut, never were shut.
Second sense
command also means "elicit without asking" — commands respect, commands a high price, commands a view of the harbor. That verb reports nothing at all; it takes only a noun phrase.
Reports
the strongest directive in the language — an order from someone whose authority makes refusal a breach of duty. Where order is administrative and can come from a doctor or a court, command is military, royal, or divine, and in ordinary American office English it always sounds like an exaggeration.

"Fall back to the ridge." → The general commanded them to fall back to the ridge. "The gates are to be shut at sundown." → He commanded that the gates be shut at sundown.

  1. The captain commanded the crew to stand down.
  2. She commanded him to leave the house and never come back.
  3. The statute commands that every ballot be preserved for twenty-two months.
  4. He does not so much ask as command.
  5. Her work commands respect even from people who disagree with it.
  6. The officer commanded us not to move until the all-clear.
See also
order, direct, instruct, decree, bid

decree#

Pronunciation
/dɪˈkri/ · dih-KREE
Русский
постановлять, издавать указ — приказ, сразу ставший законом; часто иронично
Type
reporting verb — sovereign order
Register
formal; legal, historical, often ironic
Patterns
decree + that-clause (subjunctive) · decree + noun phrase (decreed a general amnesty)
Never
decree someone to do something — decree takes no personal object before a complement. Repairs: decreed that he step down, decreed his removal.
Subjunctive
the base form throughout, and no backshift: The emperor decreed that the temple be rebuilt, not was rebuilt.
Reports
a command that becomes law the instant it is uttered, issued by an authority answerable to no one. Applied to an ordinary person it is heavily ironic — My sister has decreed that we're doing Thanksgiving at her place.

  2. The court decreed that the estate be divided equally among the three children.
  3. Some committee somewhere has decreed that we all use the new template.
  4. In 1685 the king decreed the revocation of the edict.
  5. Nature has not decreed that offices must be cold.
  6. The board decreed that no exceptions be made for anyone.

demand#

Pronunciation
/dɪˈmænd/ · dih-MAND
Русский
требовать — настойчиво, не оставляя собеседнику возможности отказать
Type
reporting verb — non-negotiable request
Register
neutral
Patterns
demand + that-clause (subjunctive) · demand + to-infinitive (the subject acts: demanded to see the manager) · demand + noun phrase · demand + noun phrase + of + sb (demanded an explanation of him) · demand + of + sb + that-clause (formal)
Never
demand someone to do something — one of the highest-frequency errors in this whole file, because demand looks exactly like order and require, both of which accept the object plus infinitive. It does not. Repairs: demanded that he resign, demanded his resignation, demanded to see him.
Subjunctive
bare base form, all persons, no backshift. She demanded that he be present, not was present; He demanded that the invoice be paid; negative with plain not — demanded that we not tell the press, never ✗ that we don't tell. British writing has demanded that he should be present, which is a dialect difference and not an error.
The mirror-image error
demand's object-plus-infinitive gap has an exact counterpart worth knowing — want and expect take the object plus infinitive and refuse the that-clause. ✗ I want that you come and ✗ I demanded him to come are the same learner making opposite errors.
Reports
a request presented as an entitlement, with no room left for a no. It differs from require in that a person demands and a rule requires, and from insist on in that demand is a single act while insist implies repetition against resistance.

"I want to see the manager, right now." → She demanded to see the manager. "He has to resign. Today." → They demanded that he resign that day.

  1. The union demanded that the layoffs be rescinded.
  2. He demanded to know who had authorized the transfer.
  3. Protesters demanded the release of the detained journalists.
  4. She demanded of him an explanation he was in no position to give.
  5. The tenants demanded that the heat be fixed before the weekend.
  6. Nobody is demanding perfection; we're asking for a deadline.
See also
require, insist on, order, request, ask

dictate#

Pronunciation
/ˈdɪkteɪt/ · DIK-tayt
Русский
диктовать, навязывать условия — с осуждением: так о себе не говорят
Type
reporting verb — imposed terms
Register
neutral, usually disapproving
Patterns
dictate + that-clause · dictate + noun phrase (dictated the terms) · dictate + noun phrase + to + sb (dictated the terms to them) · dictate + wh-clause (dictates who gets in) · dictate to + sb (intransitive: nobody dictates to me)
Never
dictate someone to do something — no object plus infinitive. Repairs: dictated that he step down, dictated the terms of his exit.
Mood
the that-clause goes subjunctive when dictate imposes a rule (policy dictates that the form be notarized) and indicative when it merely predicts or explains (common sense dictates that this will take longer). Both are current; the second is far commoner in American writing.

  4. Weather dictated the schedule more than anything on the whiteboard did.
  5. The budget dictates how many people we can bring on this year.
  6. She dictated two letters and a memo before nine.

Never

Often not a speech act at all. In most of its uses, the subject of prompt is an event rather than a speaker — The leak prompted an internal review. Only prompt someone to do something, with a human subject, reports an utterance; the rest reports causation.

Reports
the smallest directive in the file — one line, once, and only because the listener had stalled. An actor who forgets a line is prompted; so is a witness who needs the question repeated.

"Go on — tell them what you told me." → She prompted me to repeat what I had told her. "And your date of birth?" → The clerk prompted him for his date of birth.

  1. A whisper from the wings prompted him to pick up the line.
  2. Her question prompted me to look at the numbers again.
  3. The audit prompted a complete rewrite of the approval process.
  4. The system prompts you for a second factor after ninety days.
  5. He had to be prompted twice before he thanked anyone.
  6. Nothing in the email prompted us to think it was urgent.
See also
remind, nag, press, urge, push

proscribe#

Pronunciation
/proʊˈskraɪb/ · proh-SKRYBE
Русский
осуждать и запрещать — книжн.: объявлять недопустимым; не путать с prescribe
Type
reporting verb — formal condemnation and prohibition
Register
formal, scholarly
Patterns
proscribe + noun phrase · proscribe + -ing
Never
proscribe someone to do something, and proscribe someone from doing something — the from frame that works so naturally with prohibit is not established here. Proscribe takes the forbidden thing as its object, not the person.

Not prescribe. These two are near-opposites, they differ by one letter, and confusing them reverses the sentence:

VerbMeansExample
proscribeforbids, condemnsThe style guide proscribes the passive voice.
prescribeorders, lays down as requiredThe style guide prescribes the serial comma.
Reports
a formal denunciation that also forbids — historically, outlawing a person; now, ruling a practice out of bounds. It is the verb of style guides, professional codes, and academic writing about norms.

"The passive voice is not to be used in these documents." → The manual proscribes the passive voice. "Members may not accept outside money." → The charter proscribes accepting outside money.

  1. Most professional codes proscribe undisclosed conflicts of interest.
  2. The regime proscribed the party and jailed its leadership.
  3. Nothing in the guidelines proscribes citing preprints; they just discourage it.
  4. Church law proscribed the practice for four centuries.
  5. Grammar handbooks once proscribed the split infinitive, and nobody listened.
  6. The treaty proscribes an entire class of weapon.
See also
prohibit, forbid, ban, bar, decree

push#

Pronunciation
/pʊʃ/ · PUUSH
Русский
давить, продавливать — разг.: настойчиво склонять к решению в своих интересах
Type
reporting verb — sustained pressure
Register
informal to neutral
Patterns
push + sb + to-infinitive · push + sb + into + -ing · push for + noun phrase · push + sb + on + noun phrase
Never
push someone doing something without into, and push that someone do something — no that-clause.

The into frame entails success: He pushed me into signing means I signed. He pushed me to sign leaves the outcome open. The same split runs through talk into, pressure into, and bully into.

Reports
repeated pressure toward a decision, with a strong hint that the person pushed was reluctant and the person pushing had an interest. It is press with the speaker's motives in view.

"You should really apply. Apply. Have you applied?" → My advisor pushed me to apply. "Sign it. Everyone else has signed it." → They pushed her into signing.

  1. Her parents pushed her to take the safer offer.
  2. Two members of the board have been pushing for a change at the top since March.
  3. He pushed me into buying the extended warranty and I've regretted it since.
  4. Don't push him on it tonight; wait until he's slept.
  5. The agency is pushing utilities to publish outage data.
  6. Nobody pushed me; I wanted to go.

press, urge, nag, prompt, exhort

remind#

Pronunciation
/rɪˈmaɪnd/ · rih-MYND
Русский
напоминать — побуждать к тому, что человек и сам раньше собирался сделать
Type
reporting verb — directive resting on prior intent
Register
neutral
Patterns
remind + sb + to-infinitive · remind + sb + that-clause · remind + sb + of + noun phrase · remind + sb + about + noun phrase · remind + sb + wh-clause
Never
remind to bring your passport with no object — remind, like tell, always names the person. And remind someone doing something.

Two frames, two speech acts. The infinitive reports a directive; the that-clause reports information. They are not interchangeable:

FrameReportsExample
remind + sb + to-infinitivea directiveShe reminded me to lock the door. (Lock it.)
remind + sb + that-clausea factShe reminded me that the door locks itself. (It does.)
Reports
a directive whose whole force comes from the listener's own earlier intention — you can only be reminded to do something you had already meant to do. That presupposition is what separates it from tell: She told me to lock the door implies she decided; She reminded me to lock the door implies I had.

"Don't forget your passport." → She reminded me to bring my passport. "The deposit's due Friday, remember." → He reminded me that the deposit was due Friday.

  1. Please remind me to move the car before street cleaning.
  2. The dentist's office reminds you twice, by text and by phone.
  3. She reminded the committee that the vote had already been taken once.
  4. He reminded us of the last time somebody tried this.
  5. Nobody reminded me the clocks changed, and I sat in an empty room for an hour.
  6. Let me remind you who signed the check.
See also
tell, prompt, nag, instruct, press

request#

Pronunciation
/rɪˈkwɛst/ · rih-KWEST
Русский
просить официально — офиц., обычно письменно; «холодный» вариант ask
Type
reporting verb — formal request
Register
formal, written
Patterns
request + noun phrase · request + that-clause (subjunctive) · request + to-infinitive (the subject acts: requested to be reassigned) · request + sb + to-infinitive (formal and stiff; the passive is the natural home)
Never
request for a copy as a verb — for belongs to the noun (a request for a copy), not the verb. Repair: requested a copy. And request someone something — ✗ requested him a copy.
Subjunctive
request that takes the base form and does not backshift: They requested that the session not be recorded, never ✗ that the session isn't recorded.

The active object plus infinitive is stiff. We request you to complete the form is grammatical and reads like a form letter from 1955. American English strongly prefers the passive — Passengers are requested to remain seated — or the that-clause.

Reports
a request lodged in a formal register, usually in writing. It is ask with the temperature dropped: nobody requests a glass of water from a friend.

"Please turn off all phones." → The theater requested that all phones be turned off. "Could I be moved to a different team?" → She requested to be reassigned.

  1. He requested a copy of the inspection report in writing.
  2. The court requested that both parties submit briefs by the tenth.
  3. Guests are requested to keep noise down after ten.
  4. She requested to be excused from the panel.
  5. We request your presence at a reception following the ceremony.
  6. The family has requested that donations go to the food bank instead of flowers.

ask, demand, require, petition, call on

require#

Pronunciation
/rɪˈkwaɪər/ · rih-KWYRE
Русский
требовать — обязанность исходит от правила или условий, а не от человека
Type
reporting verb — imposed obligation
Register
neutral to formal
Patterns
require + that-clause (subjunctive) · require + sb + to-infinitive · require + noun phrase · require + -ing (requires wearing a badge) · passive be required to (the commonest form of all)
Never
require that every visitor signs in in American prose — the plain indicative in a directive that-clause is a British pattern and reads as an error in American writing. Repair: require that every visitor sign in. British English also has require that every visitor should sign in. Both British forms are dialect rather than error; neither belongs in American prose. What is impossible in either dialect is require someone doing something.

The contrast with demand. These two mean nearly the same thing and behave differently, which is why the error migrates between them:

+ sb + to-infinitive+ that + subjunctive+ noun phrase
require✓ requires visitors to sign in✓ requires that visitors sign in✓ requires a signature
demand✗ demands visitors to sign in✓ demands that visitors sign in✓ demands a signature
Reports
an obligation that comes from a rule, a policy, or the nature of the task rather than from a person's will. A person demands; a regulation requires. This is why require so often appears with an inanimate subject and in the passive: You are required to disclose it.

"Every visitor has to sign in." → Policy requires that every visitor sign in. / Policy requires every visitor to sign in. "You'll need two forms of ID." → The bank requires two forms of ID.

  1. The job requires you to be on call one weekend a month.
  2. State law requires that landlords return deposits within twenty-one days.
  3. The recipe requires buttermilk, and nothing else works the same way.
  4. Employees are required to report outside income over five thousand dollars.
  5. The grant requires filing a progress report every six months.
  6. Nothing requires you to answer that question.

demand, mandate, stipulate, order, instruct

stipulate#

Pronunciation
/ˈstɪpjəleɪt/ · STIP-yuh-layt
Русский
оговаривать, предусматривать в договоре — юр.: условие, записанное на бумаге
Type
reporting verb — written condition
Register
formal; legal and contractual
Patterns
stipulate + that-clause · stipulate + noun phrase · stipulate + wh-clause (stipulates how the money may be spent) · stipulate to + noun phrase (courtroom sense — see below)
Never
stipulate someone to do something — no personal object before the complement. Repair: stipulated that he give thirty days' notice.

Mood tells you what it is doing. Stipulate imposes a condition with the subjunctive and merely records a term with the indicative, and both are correct:

SentenceMoodReports
The contract stipulates that the work be finished first.subjunctivea condition being imposed
The contract stipulates that payment is due in thirty days.indicativea term being described
Second sense
in American courtrooms, stipulate also means to agree that a fact need not be proved — The parties stipulated to the authenticity of the emails. That sense takes to.
Reports
a requirement written into an agreement as a precondition of it — the difference from require is that a stipulation is negotiated, part of a bargain, and usually on paper.

"The work has to be finished before we pay." → The contract stipulates that the work be finished before payment. "Thirty days' notice, in writing." → The lease stipulates thirty days' written notice.

  1. The will stipulates that the house not be sold for ten years.
  2. Her contract stipulates a minimum of four weeks' notice.
  3. The grant stipulates how much can go to overhead.
  4. They stipulated that neither side would discuss the settlement publicly.
  5. The rules stipulate that entries be submitted electronically.
  6. Both parties stipulated to the timeline, which saved a day of testimony.

Pronunciation
/ˈsʌmən/ · SUM-un
Русский
вызывать — приказ явиться, которому нельзя не подчиниться; офиц., юр.
Type

Register
formal
Patterns

Never
summon someone that they come — no that-clause. And summon to someone — the person summoned is a direct object.

Not summons. The noun is a summons (plural summonses). American legal usage also has a verb summons meaning "serve with a summons" — He was summonsed for failing to appear — which is a different word from summon and best left to lawyers.

  3. She summoned the waiter with a look that took about a second.
  4. The board was summoned into an emergency session on a Sunday.
  5. Someone finally summoned help from the road above.
  6. Being summoned to headquarters is never good news.

tell#

Pronunciation
/tɛl/ · TEL
Русский
велеть, сказать сделать — с инфинитивом это приказ, с that-clause — сообщение
Type
reporting verb — command (also the general information verb)
Register
neutral
Patterns
tell + sb + to-infinitive · tell + sb + that-clause · tell + sb + wh-clause · tell + sb + noun phrase (told me a story) · tell + sb + about + noun phrase
Never
tell to sit down and tell that he had left — tell always requires an object, in every one of its uses. And say someone to do something — its partner verb say refuses an object entirely. The object requirement is the sharpest line between the two.

Two verbs in one. Tell reports a command with an infinitive and a statement with a that-clause, and switching the frame switches the speech act:

FrameReportsExample
tell + sb + to-infinitivea commandHe told me to go home. (an order)
tell + sb + that-clauseinformationHe told me that he had gone home. (a fact)
No subjunctive
unlike order and demand, tell does not license a mandative clause. ✗ He told that I leave is not English; the that-clause after tell is always an ordinary statement, backshifted per Backshift.

And say to is fine without an object. Textbooks say say cannot report a command; in real American speech He said to wait and She said not to bother are entirely normal. What is impossible is say with an object. To name the addressee, you must switch to tell.

Reports
the plainest directive verb in the language — force-neutral, register-neutral, and by far the most common. Everything more specific in this file is tell plus an attitude.

"Go home." → He told me to go home. "Don't worry about it." → She told me not to worry about it.

  1. My boss told me to take the rest of the week off.
  2. She told the kids not to open the door for anyone.
  3. He told us that the flight had already been canceled.
  4. Tell me what actually happened, not what's in the report.
  5. Nobody told me the meeting had moved.
  6. The sign tells you to stay on the trail, and people ignore it.

order, instruct, ask, remind, direct
