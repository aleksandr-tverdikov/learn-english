# Admitting, denying, and revealing

> The verbs that report what a speaker finally owned up to, refused to own up to, or let out without meaning to.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

These verbs report a speaker's stance toward a fact rather than the fact itself — that they granted it at last, that they contested it, or that it got out of them. The pattern running through the whole group is the **-ing** complement where the neutral verbs take a *that*-clause (*admitted taking the money*, *denied knowing her*), together with a flat refusal of the *to*-infinitive that learners keep reaching for: ***denied to take***, ***admitted to have taken***, and ***accepted to help*** are all broken. Most of these verbs also carry a judgment the writer cannot switch off — *admit* and *confess* presuppose that the thing admitted counts against the speaker, *reveal* and *disclose* presuppose that it was hidden until now, *insinuate* presupposes that it was never quite said. Choosing one of them over plain *said* is therefore an editorial act rather than a stylistic one; see [Verbs that carry judgment](../08-special-cases.md#verbs-that-carry-judgment) for what each one tells a reader.

### accept

**Pronunciation:** /əkˈsɛpt/ &middot; *uhk-SEPT*
**Type:** reporting verb — concession &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *accept* + **that**-clause &middot; *accept* + noun phrase &middot; *accept* + noun phrase + **as** + noun phrase / adjective &middot; passive *it is accepted that*
**Never:** ***accept to do something*** — *accept* takes no *to*-infinitive in any of its senses, and ✗*She accepted to help* is the most persistent error made with this verb by speakers of French, Spanish, and Italian. Repairs: *agreed to help*, *accepted the job*, *accepted that she had to help*. Also ***accept me that*** — no personal object stands before the clause.
**Reports:** coming around to a proposition you had been resisting, usually after evidence or argument. Weaker than [concede](#concede), which needs a specific point surrendered in a dispute, and it carries none of the fault that [admit](#admit) does — you can accept a fact that reflects on nobody.

> "All right, the numbers are what they are," he said.  →  He **accepted that** the numbers **were** what they were.

1. The board finally *accepted* that the launch date had to move.
2. She *accepts* that nothing she says is going to change his mind.
3. Even his critics *accept* the basic finding.
4. I'll *accept* that I was wrong about the route, but not about the time.
5. The court *accepted* her account as accurate.
6. Nobody in the room *accepted* that the outage had lasted only six hours.

**See also:** [acknowledge](#acknowledge), [concede](#concede), [grant](#grant), [agree](03-agree-refuse.md#agree)

### acknowledge

**Pronunciation:** /ækˈnɑlɪdʒ/ &middot; *ak-NAH-lij*
**Type:** reporting verb — admission &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *acknowledge* + **that**-clause &middot; *acknowledge* + **-ing** &middot; *acknowledge* + noun phrase &middot; *acknowledge* + noun phrase + **as** / **to be** &middot; *acknowledge* + **wh**-clause &middot; *acknowledge* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it is acknowledged that*
**Never:** ***acknowledge to do something*** and ***acknowledge me that*** — there is no *to*-infinitive after this verb, and the listener needs a preposition: ✓*acknowledged to me that he was late*, ✓*acknowledged being late*.
**Spelling:** American English writes **acknowledgment**, with no *e* before the *-ment*, on the same pattern as *judgment*. **British English writes *acknowledgement***, keeping the *e*; that spelling is British, not an American variant, and it should not be copied into American work.
**Reports:** a formal admission that something is so. The lightest verb in the family — [admit](#admit) presupposes fault and [confess](#confess) presupposes guilt, but you can acknowledge a debt, a favor, or an email with nothing at stake.

> "Yes, we were late filing," the company said.  →  The company **acknowledged that** it **had been** late filing.

1. The mayor *acknowledged* that the city had missed the deadline.
2. She *acknowledged* receiving the letter but said she never opened it.
3. He *acknowledged* the mistake without apologizing for it.
4. The study *acknowledges* what the earlier work got right.
5. It is widely *acknowledged* that the original estimate was too low.
6. He *acknowledged* to the committee that no one had read the contract.

**See also:** [accept](#accept), [admit](#admit), [concede](#concede), [grant](#grant)

### admit

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈmɪt/ &middot; *uhd-MIT*
**Type:** reporting verb — admission &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *admit* + **that**-clause &middot; *admit* + **-ing** &middot; *admit* + **to** + **-ing** &middot; *admit* + noun phrase &middot; *admit* + **to** + noun phrase &middot; *admit* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *admit* + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *it is admitted that* &middot; (unrelated senses: *admit* + sb + **to** + noun phrase "let in"; *admit* + **of** + noun phrase "leave room for")
**Never:** ***admit to do something*** and ***admit to have done something*** — the complement is an *-ing* form or a *that*-clause, never an infinitive. ✗*He admitted to take the money.* ✗*He admitted to have taken the money.* ✓*He admitted taking the money.* ✓*He admitted that he had taken the money.* Also ***admit me that*** — the listener needs *to*: ✓*admitted to me that*.
**-ing or to + -ing:** both are standard American and they mean the same thing; *admitted taking it* is a shade more written, *admitted to taking it* a shade more spoken. The *to* in the second is a **preposition**, which is precisely why no infinitive can follow it.
**Spelling:** the *t* doubles before a vowel — *admitted*, *admitting* — but not in *admission*.
**Two other verbs in the same spelling:** *admit* also means "let in" (*She was admitted to the ward that night*; British English drops the article — *admitted to hospital*) and, in formal writing, "leave room for" (*The wording admits of no other reading*). Neither reports speech, and the first is why *admitted to* has to be read twice: *admitted to the clinic* was let in, *admitted to taking it* owned up.
**Reports:** conceding something that counts against you. The fault is presupposed rather than asserted, which is why *admitted* is unfair attribution for a fact the speaker volunteered freely.
**The admit family at a glance:**

| Verb | Presupposes | Takes *-ing* | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| *acknowledge* | nothing against the speaker | yes | neutral to formal |
| *concede* | a point lost in an argument | rarely | neutral to formal |
| *grant* | a point lost, given up graciously | no | neutral, in fixed frames |
| *admit* | fault, or reluctance | yes | neutral |
| *own up to* | fault, and prior evasion | yes, after *to* | informal |
| *confess* | guilt | yes, after *to* | neutral to formal |

> "Okay, I took the money," he said.  →  He **admitted taking** the money. / He **admitted that** he **had taken** the money.

1. She *admitted* that she had never read the contract.
2. He *admitted* to me that the whole story was secondhand.
3. The airline *admitted* overbooking the flight.
4. Two of them *admitted* to falsifying the timesheets.
5. I'll *admit* it — I forgot the appointment completely.
6. He would never *admit* how much the repair actually cost.
7. Nobody *admitted* to being in the building after six.

**See also:** [acknowledge](#acknowledge), [concede](#concede), [confess](#confess), [own up to](#own-up-to), [deny](#deny)

### allow

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlaʊ/ &middot; *uh-LOW* — the second syllable rhymes with *now*, not with *low*
**Type:** reporting verb — concession &middot; **Register:** formal and somewhat dated in this sense; the everyday *allow* is the permission verb
**Patterns:** *allow* + **that**-clause &middot; *allow* + **of** + noun phrase (*allows of no exception*) &middot; (in its unrelated permission sense: *allow* + sb + **to**-infinitive, *allow* + **-ing**, *allow* + noun phrase)
**Never:** in the concession sense, ***allow to do*** and ***allow -ing*** are both impossible — ✗*I allow to be wrong*, ✗*I allow being wrong*, ✓*I allow that I was wrong*. Also ***allow me that he was right***: no personal object stands before the clause. Take care with *allow* + sb + **to**-infinitive, which is perfectly grammatical but is the other verb — *I allow him to be right* grants him permission and concedes nothing.
**Two verbs in one spelling:** *The historian allows that the date may be wrong* concedes a point; *The landlord allows pets* grants permission. The *that*-clause is the reliable signal — only the concession verb takes one.
**Regional:** *allow as how* survives in Southern and rural American speech (*He allowed as how it might rain*) and reads as dialect anywhere else. The complementizer *as how* has its own entry under [conjunctions](../../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#as-how).
**Reports:** granting a point, in a register that now sounds bookish. Like [concede](#concede) it presupposes resistance — nothing gets allowed that was not first in doubt — but the surrender is calm rather than forced out. Choose it when the concession is being made in writing; *concede* is the everyday verb.

> "Fine — the argument has something to it," she said.  →  She **allowed that** the argument **had** something to it.

1. The historian *allows* that the letter may be a forgery.
2. I'll *allow* that the price was fair, given the market.
3. He *allowed* that he had been unfair to her.
4. Even the dissent *allows* that the statute is ambiguous.
5. She *allowed* as how the drive would take most of a day.
6. Critics generally *allow* that the early novels are the stronger ones.

**See also:** [concede](#concede), [grant](#grant), [accept](#accept), [acknowledge](#acknowledge)

### announce

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnaʊns/ &middot; *uh-NOWNSS*
**Type:** reporting verb — public statement, often a revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *announce* + **that**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase &middot; *announce* + **wh**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *announce* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it was announced that*
**Never:** ***announce me the news*** and ***announce me that*** — *announce* never takes a bare indirect object, and the audience always goes into a *to*-phrase. ✗*She announced us the results.* ✓*She announced the results to us.* ✓*She announced to us that the results were in.* Also ***announce to do***.
**Word order:** when there is both a listener and a clause, the *to*-phrase comes first: *announced to the staff that the office was closing*. Putting it last (✗*announced that the office was closing to the staff*) attaches it to the wrong verb and says something absurd.
**Reports:** making something public and official. Where [reveal](#reveal) presupposes concealment and [leak](#leak) presupposes that the release was unauthorized, *announce* presupposes only that the speaker had the standing to say it.

> "We're closing the Denver office," the CEO said.  →  The CEO **announced that** the company **was closing** the Denver office.

1. She *announced* that she was leaving at the end of the month.
2. The district *announced* the closure by text at six in the morning.
3. He *announced* to the whole table that he had gotten the job.
4. They still haven't *announced* who won.
5. It was *announced* on Friday that the merger had fallen through.
6. My brother *announced* his engagement on a group call.

**See also:** [reveal](#reveal), [disclose](#disclose), [unveil](#unveil), [leak](#leak)

### betray

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtreɪ/ &middot; *bih-TRAY*
**Type:** reporting verb — unintentional revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Patterns:** *betray* + noun phrase &middot; *betray* + **the fact that** &middot; *betray* + **wh**-clause &middot; *betray* + **no** / **little** + noun phrase &middot; *betray* + oneself
**Never:** ***betray that he was nervous*** — the bare *that*-clause is dispreferred in careful writing; use a noun phrase or the *the fact that* bridge: ✓*betrayed his nervousness*, ✓*betrayed the fact that he was nervous*. Also ***betray to do***, ***betray -ing***, and ***betray me that***.
**The subject is usually a sign, not a speaker:** a voice, a face, an accent, a pause. The person given away appears as the object: *her voice betrayed her*.
**Reports:** letting something show without meaning to. The other *betray* — turning on someone — is the same word, and the overlap is why this one always carries a faint suggestion that the revealer was on the wrong side of the secret.

> "I'm fine, really," she said.  →  Her voice **betrayed** how little she **meant** it.

1. His accent *betrayed* him within about five words.
2. Her expression *betrayed* nothing at all.
3. The letter *betrays* the fact that he already knew about the sale.
4. He *betrayed* no surprise when the verdict was read out.
5. A pause on the line *betrayed* how bad the news was going to be.
6. She *betrayed* herself by asking about a detail nobody had told her.

**See also:** [give away](#give-away), [let slip](#let-slip), [let on](#let-on), [reveal](#reveal)

### blurt out

**Pronunciation:** /ˈblɜrt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *BLURT OWT*
**Type:** reporting verb — unintentional revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning informal
**Patterns:** *blurt out* + **that**-clause &middot; *blurt out* + noun phrase &middot; *blurt* + noun phrase + *out* &middot; *blurt out* + **wh**-clause &middot; *blurt out* + direct quotation
**Never:** ***blurt out to do***, ***blurt out -ing***, and ***blurt me out that***. The particle also cannot follow a pronoun object: ✗*blurted out it*, ✓*blurted it out*.
**Separable:** a full noun phrase can sit on either side of the particle (*blurted out the answer* / *blurted the answer out*), but a pronoun must sit between the verb and the particle.
**Reports:** saying something suddenly and without thinking, usually something the speaker meant to hold back. Plain *blurt* exists, but *blurt out* is the ordinary American form.

> "She's pregnant!" he said before he could stop himself.  →  He **blurted out that** she **was** pregnant.

1. He *blurted out* that he had already accepted another offer.
2. She *blurted* the whole thing *out* before anyone could stop her.
3. Don't *blurt out* the ending — half of them haven't seen it.
4. I *blurted out* the first number that came into my head.
5. Someone in the back *blurted out* what everyone was thinking.
6. He *blurted it out* and then spent an hour apologizing for it.
7. "It wasn't me," he *blurted out* before anyone had even asked.

**See also:** [let slip](#let-slip), [spill](#spill), [give away](#give-away)

### come clean

**Pronunciation:** /kʌm ˈklin/ &middot; *kum KLEEN*
**Type:** reporting verb — admission &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *come clean* (no complement) &middot; *come clean* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *come clean* + **about** + **-ing** &middot; *come clean* + **with sb** &middot; *come clean* + **to sb** + **about**
**Never:** ***come clean that he had lied*** — the idiom takes no *that*-clause at all. Repairs: *came clean about lying*, *admitted that he had lied*. Also ***come clean to do*** and ***come clean the truth*** — there is no direct object either.
**Fixed form:** *clean* here is an adjective and never inflects — *came clean*, *coming clean*, never ✗*came cleanly*.
**Reports:** finally telling the truth after a stretch of concealment. It presupposes a prior lie or cover-up more strongly than [admit](#admit) does, and the tone is conversational throughout.

> "All right — I've been out of work since March," he said.  →  He finally **came clean about** having been out of work since March.

1. He *came clean* about the credit card debt.
2. She decided to *come clean* with her parents over dinner.
3. It took him a year to *come clean* about missing the deadline.
4. If you *come clean* now, this stays between us.
5. The company never *came clean* about how long the data had been exposed.
6. I'd rather *come clean* than keep patching the story.

**See also:** [own up to](#own-up-to), [fess up](#fess-up), [confess](#confess), [admit](#admit)

### concede

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsid/ &middot; *kuhn-SEED*
**Type:** reporting verb — concession &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *concede* + **that**-clause &middot; *concede* + noun phrase &middot; *concede* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *concede* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it is conceded that*
**Never:** ***concede to do*** and ***concede me that*** — the listener needs *to*: ✓*conceded to reporters that the race was over*. The bare *-ing* that [admit](#admit) takes so easily is unnatural here: ✗*conceded making a mistake*, ✓*conceded that he had made a mistake*. The **wh**-clause is shaky too — *He would not concede how much it had cost* wants *admit*.
**Reports:** giving up a point under pressure, in an argument or a contest. What it presupposes is not fault but resistance — there has to have been a dispute for anything to be conceded. In journalism it is as loaded as *admitted*, and it should be used only when the resistance is on the record.

> "All right, your figures are better than mine," she said.  →  She **conceded that** my figures **were** better than hers.

1. He *conceded* that the first draft had been rushed.
2. She *conceded* the point but not the argument.
3. The senator *conceded* the race a little after midnight.
4. Even the study's authors *concede* that the sample was small.
5. She would not *concede* that any part of it had been her idea.
6. The company *conceded* two of the five claims and fought the rest.
7. He *conceded* to reporters that the campaign had run out of money.

**See also:** [accept](#accept), [acknowledge](#acknowledge), [grant](#grant), [admit](#admit), [dispute](#dispute)

### confess

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈfɛs/ &middot; *kuhn-FESS*
**Type:** reporting verb — admission of guilt &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *confess* + **that**-clause &middot; *confess* + **to** + **-ing** &middot; *confess* + **to** + noun phrase &middot; *confess* + noun phrase &middot; *confess* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *confess* + **to sb** &middot; *confess* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *confess* (no complement)
**Never:** ***confess to do*** — the *to* is a preposition, so no infinitive can follow it: ✗*confessed to steal the car*, ✓*confessed to stealing the car*. Also ***confess me that***, which needs *to*: ✓*confessed to her that*. The bare *-ing* without *to* is possible but dispreferred — *confessed to taking it* beats *confessed taking it*.
**Two things *to* can introduce:** the offense or the audience. *He confessed to the burglary* names what he did; *He confessed to the detective* names who heard it. When a sentence needs both, one of them has to move — *He confessed the burglary to the detective*, or *He confessed to the detective that he had done it*.
**Reports:** admitting to something you can be blamed or punished for. Stronger than [admit](#admit) by exactly the distance between fault and guilt, and it keeps a religious and legal residue — you confess to a priest, to the police, or to a court. The weakened social use (*I confess I've never read it*) is a politeness formula and carries none of that.

> "It was me. I broke it," he said.  →  He **confessed to breaking** it. / He **confessed that** he **had broken** it.

1. He *confessed* to taking the cash out of the register.
2. She *confessed* that she had known about it for months.
3. Two suspects *confessed* within the first day.
4. I'll *confess* I've never actually finished the book.
5. He *confessed* his part in it to the auditors.
6. After six hours she still hadn't *confessed*.

**See also:** [admit](#admit), [own up to](#own-up-to), [come clean](#come-clean), [acknowledge](#acknowledge)

### confide

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈfaɪd/ &middot; *kuhn-FYDE*
**Type:** reporting verb — private revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *confide* + **that**-clause &middot; *confide* + **in sb** &middot; *confide* + **in sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *confide* + **in sb** + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *confide* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *confide* + **to sb** + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***confide me that*** and ***confide me the news*** — the listener takes *in* or *to*, never a bare object. ✓*confided in me that*, ✓*confided to me that*, ✓*confided her plans to me*. Also ***confide to do*** and ***confide -ing***.
**in or to:** *confide in someone* names the relationship — trusting them as a rule; *confide something to someone* names one act of telling. *She confides in her sister* and *She confided the news to her sister* are both correct and mean different things.
**Reports:** telling something private on the understanding that it stays private. The secrecy is presupposed; the fault is not.

> "Don't tell anyone, but I'm quitting," she said.  →  She **confided that** she **was** quitting.

1. He *confided* that the marriage had been over for years.
2. She *confided* in her roommate and in nobody else.
3. He *confided* his doubts to a colleague over lunch.
4. She *confided* to me that she had turned the offer down.
5. He has never *confided* in anyone about the diagnosis.
6. They *confided* in each other for thirty years.

**See also:** [reveal](#reveal), [disclose](#disclose), [let on](#let-on), [divulge](#divulge), [volunteer](#volunteer)

### contradict

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɑntrəˈdɪkt/ &middot; *kahn-truh-DIKT*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial of someone else's statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *contradict* + sb &middot; *contradict* + noun phrase &middot; *contradict* + oneself &middot; passive *be contradicted by*
**Never:** ***contradict that*** — no *that*-clause of any kind. ✗*He contradicted that he had been there.* Use [deny](#deny) for a speaker's denial about himself (*He denied that he had been there*), and *contradict* plus a noun phrase for the rejection of somebody else's statement (*He contradicted her account*). Also ***contradict to do***, ***contradict -ing***, and ***contradict me that***.
**Reports:** saying that what someone else said is untrue. Alone among the denial verbs it takes a **person** as its object — you contradict *her*, but you deny *the claim* — and it needs a prior statement to work on: with nothing on the record, there is nothing to contradict.

> "That's not what happened at all," he told her.  →  He **contradicted her** flatly.

1. Nobody at the table dared *contradict* him.
2. The second witness *contradicted* the first on every detail.
3. He *contradicted* himself twice in the same paragraph.
4. The lab results *contradict* the company's timeline.
5. She *contradicted* the official account within an hour of reading it.
6. I'm not trying to *contradict* you — I just remember it differently.

**See also:** [deny](#deny), [dispute](#dispute), [rebut](#rebut), [refute](#refute)

### deny

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈnaɪ/ &middot; *dih-NY*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *deny* + **that**-clause &middot; *deny* + **-ing** &middot; *deny* + **having** + past participle &middot; *deny* + noun phrase &middot; *deny* + **it** &middot; passive *it is denied that*
**Never:** ***deny to do something*** — the commonest error made with this verb anywhere. ✗*He denied to take the money.* ✓*He denied taking the money.* ✓*He denied that he had taken the money.* There is no *to*-infinitive after *deny* in any sense, and no *to* + *-ing* either: ✗*denied to taking it*. Also ***deny me that he was there*** — no personal object stands before the clause.
**Two verbs in one spelling:**

| Frame | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *deny* + *that*-clause / *-ing* / noun phrase | say that it is not true | *She denied leaking the memo.* |
| *deny* + sb + noun phrase | refuse to give | *The bank denied him a loan.* |

Only the first reports speech. *They denied her access to the file* reports no statement at all, and the two frames never blend — you cannot deny someone that something happened.
**Reports:** asserting that a claim about you is false. The mirror image of [admit](#admit), and it presupposes the same thing: that an accusation is already on the table.

> "I never touched it," he said.  →  He **denied touching** it. / He **denied that** he **had touched** it.

1. She *denied* any knowledge of the payments.
2. He *denied* knowing the other driver.
3. The department *denies* that the recording was edited.
4. Ask him directly and he'll *deny* it.
5. Both companies *denied* having been contacted by regulators.
6. He never *denied* being at the party — only that he had stayed late.

**See also:** [dispute](#dispute), [disclaim](#disclaim), [contradict](#contradict), [admit](#admit), [refuse](03-agree-refuse.md#refuse)

### disavow

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪsəˈvaʊ/ &middot; *diss-uh-VOW*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial of connection &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *disavow* + noun phrase &middot; *disavow* + **any** / **all** + noun phrase &middot; *disavow* + noun phrase + **as** + noun phrase / adjective &middot; passive *be disavowed by*
**Never:** ***disavow that***, ***disavow -ing***, ***disavow to do***, and ***disavow me sth***. The verb takes a noun phrase and nothing else: ✗*He disavowed that he had written it*, ✓*He disavowed the letter*, ✓*He denied writing it*.
**Reports:** publicly cutting yourself off from something you are being associated with — a statement, a group, an ad, a decision. Where [deny](#deny) says the fact is untrue, *disavow* usually concedes the fact and rejects the connection.

> "That group does not speak for me," the senator said.  →  The senator **disavowed** the group.

1. The campaign *disavowed* the ad within hours.
2. He *disavowed* any connection to the earlier filings.
3. The agency *disavowed* the report as unauthorized.
4. She has never *disavowed* the essay, even under pressure.
5. The party quietly *disavowed* its own platform from four years earlier.
6. Both founders *disavowed* the decision once it went public.

**See also:** [disclaim](#disclaim), [deny](#deny), [retract](#retract), [recant](#recant)

### disclaim

**Pronunciation:** /dɪsˈkleɪm/ &middot; *diss-KLAYM*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial of responsibility &middot; **Register:** formal, especially legal
**Patterns:** *disclaim* + noun phrase &middot; *disclaim* + **all** / **any** + noun phrase &middot; passive *be disclaimed*
**Never:** ***disclaim that***, ***disclaim -ing***, ***disclaim to do***, and ***disclaim me sth***. The object is always a noun: ✗*disclaimed that he was responsible*, ✓*disclaimed responsibility*.
**Reports:** formally refusing responsibility, ownership, or credit. Narrower than [deny](#deny): you deny a fact, but you disclaim a liability, a warranty, an interest, or a connection. The noun *disclaimer* is far commoner than the verb and is where most Americans meet the word.

> "The manufacturer takes no responsibility for this," the notice said.  →  The notice **disclaimed** all responsibility.

1. The seller *disclaims* any warranty beyond thirty days.
2. She *disclaimed* all credit for the idea.
3. The estate *disclaimed* an interest in the property.
4. He *disclaimed* responsibility for the numbers in the appendix.
5. The label *disclaims* liability for damage in transit.
6. Both parties *disclaimed* knowledge of the third contract.

**See also:** [disavow](#disavow), [deny](#deny), [dispute](#dispute)

### disclose

**Pronunciation:** /dɪsˈkloʊz/ &middot; *diss-KLOHZ*
**Type:** reporting verb — revelation &middot; **Register:** formal, especially legal and financial
**Patterns:** *disclose* + **that**-clause &middot; *disclose* + noun phrase &middot; *disclose* + **wh**-clause &middot; *disclose* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *disclose* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it was disclosed that*
**Never:** ***disclose me the terms*** — no double object; the recipient takes *to*: ✓*disclosed the terms to us*. Also ***disclose to do***, ***disclose -ing***, and ***disclose me that***.
**Reports:** making known something you had a duty or an option to keep quiet about. The same act as [reveal](#reveal), in the register of contracts, filings, and regulators — and it adds an obligation that *reveal* does not carry, which is why a company *fails to disclose* rather than *fails to reveal*.

> "We hold shares in the supplier," the director said.  →  The director **disclosed that** the board **held** shares in the supplier.

1. The company *disclosed* that two executives had sold stock in March.
2. He failed to *disclose* the payments on his ethics form.
3. She would not *disclose* how much the settlement was worth.
4. The filing *discloses* a second lawsuit in Ohio.
5. Nothing was *disclosed* to shareholders until after the vote.
6. Doctors must *disclose* any financial ties to the manufacturer.
7. The bank *disclosed* to regulators that the account had been open for years.

**See also:** [reveal](#reveal), [divulge](#divulge), [announce](#announce), [leak](#leak)

### dispute

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈspjut/ &middot; *dih-SPYOOT* as a verb; the noun is usually /ˈdɪspjut/ &middot; *DISS-pyoot*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *dispute* + **that**-clause &middot; *dispute* + noun phrase &middot; *dispute* + **wh**-clause &middot; *dispute* + **whether**-clause &middot; passive *be disputed*
**Never:** ***dispute to do***, ***dispute -ing***, and ***dispute me that***. ✗*He disputed having said it*, ✓*He disputed that he had said it*, ✓*He denied saying it*.
**A negative habit:** the *that*-clause version turns up under negation far more often than not — *no one disputes that*, *nobody is disputing that*, *few would dispute that*. That frame asserts something while appearing merely to report agreement, and a careful reader notices.
**Reports:** challenging a claim as wrong without necessarily denying it outright. [Deny](#deny) says it is false; *dispute* says it is contestable, which is how the same fact can be *disputed* by both sides at once.

> "Those figures are not correct," the auditor said.  →  The auditor **disputed that** the figures **were** correct.

1. She *disputes* the charge on principle, not on the amount.
2. Nobody *disputes* that the building needs a new roof.
3. The union *disputed* the company's account of the meeting.
4. Historians still *dispute* whether the letter is genuine.
5. He *disputed* every line of the invoice.
6. The finding has been *disputed* by two other labs.

**See also:** [deny](#deny), [contradict](#contradict), [rebut](#rebut), [concede](#concede)

### divulge

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈvʌldʒ/ &middot; *dih-VULJ*; also /daɪˈvʌldʒ/ &middot; *dy-VULJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — revelation &middot; **Register:** formal, faintly literary
**Patterns:** *divulge* + noun phrase &middot; *divulge* + **that**-clause &middot; *divulge* + **wh**-clause &middot; *divulge* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *divulge* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *be divulged*
**Never:** ***divulge me the details*** and ***divulge me that*** — no double object, and the recipient always takes *to*: ✓*divulged the details to me*, ✓*divulged to me that*. Also ***divulge to do*** and ***divulge -ing***.
**A negative habit:** *divulge* appears under refusal far more often than not — *would not divulge*, *declined to divulge*, *without divulging*. In a plain positive sentence it sounds arch, and [reveal](#reveal) or [disclose](#disclose) is usually the better verb there.
**Reports:** giving out information that was supposed to stay in. The most secretive of the revealing verbs, and the one that most clearly presupposes a confidence being broken.

> "I can't tell you what he paid," she said.  →  She **refused to divulge** what he **had paid**.

1. He would not *divulge* the source of the recording.
2. The office declined to *divulge* how many people had applied.
3. She *divulged* the terms to a reporter that evening.
4. Nothing was *divulged* before the embargo lifted.
5. Employees agree not to *divulge* anything covered by the contract.
6. He *divulged* that the buyer was a competitor.
7. She *divulged* to a colleague that the audit had already started.

**See also:** [disclose](#disclose), [reveal](#reveal), [leak](#leak), [confide](#confide)

### expose

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈspoʊz/ &middot; *ik-SPOHZ*
**Type:** reporting verb — hostile revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral, journalistic
**Patterns:** *expose* + noun phrase &middot; *expose* + noun phrase + **as** + noun phrase / adjective &middot; *expose* + **the fact that** &middot; *expose* + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *be exposed as*
**Never:** ***expose that the reports were falsified*** — the bare *that*-clause is dispreferred in careful American writing. Repairs: *revealed that the reports were falsified*, *exposed the falsified reports*, *exposed the fact that the reports were falsified*. Also ***expose to do***, ***expose -ing***, and ***expose me that***.
**Reports:** revealing something the subject was actively hiding, and revealing it against them. [Reveal](#reveal) is neutral about whose interest is served; *expose* always serves the reader against the person exposed, which makes it a verdict as much as a report.

> "These credentials are fake," the reporter wrote.  →  The reporter **exposed** the credentials **as** forgeries.

1. The investigation *exposed* a decade of falsified inspections.
2. He was *exposed* as the author of the anonymous memo.
3. The audit *exposed* the fact that nobody had signed off on the payments.
4. Her testimony *exposed* how the contracts were awarded.
5. A single email *exposed* the whole arrangement.
6. The documentary *exposed* conditions the company had denied for years.

**See also:** [reveal](#reveal), [leak](#leak), [betray](#betray), [disclose](#disclose)

### fess up

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɛs ˈʌp/ &middot; *FESS UP*
**Type:** reporting verb — admission &middot; **Register:** informal, American
**Patterns:** *fess up* (no complement) &middot; *fess up* + **to** + noun phrase &middot; *fess up* + **to** + **-ing** &middot; *fess up* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *fess up* + **to sb**
**Never:** ***fess up that he took it*** — no *that*-clause; say *fessed up to taking it*, or move to plain *admitted that he took it*. Also ***fess up to do*** (the *to* is a preposition) and ***fess up the truth*** — there is no direct object.
**Origin and range:** a clipped American reshaping of *confess*. It belongs to conversation and to headlines that are being playful; in anything formal it is out of place.
**Reports:** owning up to something minor, with none of the gravity of [confess](#confess). The tone is nearly always light — you fess up to eating the last slice, not to a felony.

> "Fine, it was me who ate it," he said.  →  He **fessed up to eating** it.

1. Somebody needs to *fess up* about the dent in the door.
2. She *fessed up* to forgetting the appointment entirely.
3. He *fessed up* the second his mother asked.
4. Just *fess up* — everybody already knows.
5. Nobody *fessed up* to sending the email.
6. He *fessed up* to me about it a week later.

**See also:** [own up to](#own-up-to), [come clean](#come-clean), [admit](#admit), [confess](#confess)

### give away

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɪv əˈweɪ/ &middot; *giv uh-WAY*
**Type:** reporting verb — unintentional revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning informal
**Patterns:** *give away* + noun phrase &middot; *give* + noun phrase + *away* &middot; *give* + oneself + *away* &middot; *give away* + **the fact that** &middot; *give away* + **wh**-clause
**Never:** the particle cannot follow a pronoun — ✗*gave away it*, ✓*gave it away*. A bare *that*-clause is dispreferred too: ✗*His face gave away that he knew*, ✓*His face gave away the fact that he knew*, ✓*His face gave him away*. Also ***give away to do*** and ***give away -ing***.
**Other senses:** the same phrasal verb means "donate" (*gave away the furniture*) and "escort a bride down the aisle." Only the revelation sense reports anything.
**Reports:** letting a secret out by accident, usually through a sign rather than a statement. Close to [betray](#betray) but plainer and far commoner in speech; it is also the ordinary verb for spoiling a plot.

> "I don't know anything about a party," she said, smiling.  →  Her smile **gave her away**.

1. Don't *give away* the ending.
2. His accent *gave him away* before he finished the sentence.
3. She *gave away* the surprise a week early.
4. One glance at the schedule *gave away* what they were planning.
5. He *gave* the whole thing *away* by congratulating her too soon.
6. Nothing in the report *gives away* who wrote it.

**See also:** [betray](#betray), [let slip](#let-slip), [blurt out](#blurt-out), [spill](#spill)

### grant

**Pronunciation:** /ɡrænt/ &middot; *GRANT* — British English has the broad *a*, *GRAHNT*
**Type:** reporting verb — concession &middot; **Register:** neutral, mostly in fixed frames
**Patterns:** *grant* + **that**-clause &middot; *grant* + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *grant* + noun phrase (*grant the point*) &middot; *grant* + sb + noun phrase (*I'll grant you that much*) &middot; *granted (that)* as a concessive opener &middot; *granting that* in a participial phrase
**Never:** ***grant to do*** and ***grant -ing*** — ✗*He granted to be wrong*, ✗*He granted being wrong*, ✓*He granted that he was wrong*. Take care with *grant* + sb + noun phrase, which is two verbs at once: with a concessive object it is this one (*I'll grant you that much*, *I'll grant you the point*), but with a thing handed over it is the other *grant* (*The court granted him bail*), which reports no speech at all.
**Reports:** conceding a point graciously and usually in passing, on the way to disagreeing about something larger. It is almost always a step toward a *but*: *I'll grant you the price is good, but the location is impossible.*

> "Yes, all right, the price is fair," he said.  →  He **granted that** the price **was** fair.

1. I'll *grant* that the timing was unlucky.
2. She *grants* the point and moves straight past it.
3. *Granted*, nobody expected the road to be closed.
4. I'll *grant* you that much and no more.
5. He *granted* that the earlier version had been clearer.
6. Even *granting* that the data are old, the trend is hard to miss.

**See also:** [concede](#concede), [allow](#allow), [accept](#accept), [acknowledge](#acknowledge)

### hint

**Pronunciation:** /hɪnt/ &middot; *HINT*
**Type:** reporting verb — indirect statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *hint* + **that**-clause &middot; *hint* + **at** + noun phrase &middot; *hint* + **at** + **-ing** &middot; *hint* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *hint* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *hint* + **broadly** / **heavily**
**Never:** ***hint his resignation*** — *hint* takes no direct object, and the thing hinted at needs *at*: ✓*hinted at his resignation*. Also ***hint me that*** → ✓*hinted to me that*, and ***hint to do***.
**Reports:** pointing at something without saying it, and leaving the hearer to finish the job. It is neutral about motive, which is what separates it from [insinuate](#insinuate) — a hint can be kind; an insinuation cannot.

> "I wouldn't make any plans for Friday if I were you," she said.  →  She **hinted that** something **was** happening on Friday.

1. He *hinted* that there might be an opening on his team.
2. She *hinted* at a change in the schedule but wouldn't say more.
3. The manager *hinted* to us that the branch was closing.
4. He *hinted* heavily about what he wanted for his birthday.
5. Nothing in the memo even *hints* at a delay.
6. She *hinted* at leaving the firm and then said nothing for a year.

**See also:** [imply](#imply), [insinuate](#insinuate), [intimate](#intimate), [suggest](#suggest)

### imply

**Pronunciation:** /ɪmˈplaɪ/ &middot; *im-PLY*
**Type:** reporting verb — indirect statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *imply* + **that**-clause &middot; *imply* + noun phrase &middot; passive *it is implied that*
**Never:** ***imply me that*** — there is no bare personal object, and even the prepositional *implied to me that* is stiff where *suggested to me that* is ordinary. Also ***imply to do*** and ***imply -ing***.
**imply or infer:** the speaker **implies**; the listener **infers**. They are the two ends of one act, not synonyms, however often *infer* is used for *imply* in speech.

| Verb | Who does it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *imply* | the speaker or the text | *She implied that the job was mine.* |
| *infer* | the listener or the reader | *I inferred that the job was mine.* |

**Reports:** conveying something without stating it, so that the speaker can disown it later. Unlike [hint](#hint), it need not be deliberate — a sentence can imply something its author never noticed.

> "I'd hate to see anything happen to that contract," he said.  →  He **implied that** the contract **was** at risk.

1. She *implied* that the decision had already been made.
2. Nothing in the email *implies* any criticism of your team.
3. Are you *implying* that I made it up?
4. The policy *implies* a level of oversight that nobody actually provides.
5. He *implied* as much without ever quite saying it.
6. The wording *implies* that the deadline is negotiable.

**See also:** [hint](#hint), [insinuate](#insinuate), [intimate](#intimate), [suggest](#suggest), [indicate](#indicate)

### indicate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪndɪˌkeɪt/ &middot; *IN-dih-kayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — evidential statement &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *indicate* + **that**-clause &middot; *indicate* + noun phrase &middot; *indicate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *indicate* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it is indicated that*
**Never:** ***indicate me that*** → ✓*indicated to me that*. Also ***indicate to do*** and ***indicate -ing***.
**Two uses:** with a non-human subject it reports evidence (*the data indicate that*); with a human subject it is a bureaucratic hedge that reports speech while declining to quote it (*he indicated that he would resign*) — useful when the exact words are unavailable, and a dodge when the writer does not want to commit to them.
**Reports:** pointing toward a conclusion. More cautious than [reveal](#reveal), and unlike [imply](#imply) it is normally about evidence rather than intent.

> "The numbers are trending down," the analyst said.  →  The analyst **indicated that** the numbers **were** trending down.

1. Early results *indicate* that turnout was higher than expected.
2. He *indicated* to the board that he would step down in June.
3. The report does not *indicate* who authorized the transfer.
4. Her silence *indicated* the answer well enough.
5. Nothing in the file *indicates* a second payment.
6. The witness *indicated* that she had seen the car twice.

**See also:** [imply](#imply), [suggest](#suggest), [reveal](#reveal), [disclose](#disclose)

### insinuate

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈsɪnjuˌeɪt/ &middot; *in-SIN-yoo-ayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — hostile implication &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *insinuate* + **that**-clause &middot; *insinuate* + noun phrase (rare) &middot; *insinuate* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; (in an unrelated sense: *insinuate oneself into*)
**Never:** ***insinuate me that*** → ✓*insinuated to me that*. Also ***insinuate to do*** and ***insinuate -ing***.
**Reports:** implying something damaging while keeping it deniable. The judgment is baked in and cannot be switched off: to call a statement an insinuation is to accuse the speaker of bad faith. That is why the verb almost never appears in the first person — *I'm implying nothing of the kind* is a defense, but *I insinuated that* is nearly a confession.

> "Funny how the money went missing the week you started," he said.  →  He **insinuated that** she **had taken** it.

1. He *insinuated* that the reference letter had been written by a friend.
2. Nobody said it outright, but the piece *insinuates* a cover-up.
3. She *insinuated* to the committee that the data had been chosen after the fact.
4. What exactly are you *insinuating*?
5. The article *insinuates* far more than it can prove.
6. He spent an hour *insinuating* that the deal had been arranged in advance.

**See also:** [imply](#imply), [hint](#hint), [intimate](#intimate)

### intimate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪntɪˌmeɪt/ &middot; *IN-tih-mayt* — the verb has three full syllables and ends in /eɪt/; the unrelated adjective is /ˈɪntɪmɪt/ &middot; *IN-tih-mit*
**Type:** reporting verb — indirect statement &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *intimate* + **that**-clause &middot; *intimate* + noun phrase (rare) &middot; *intimate* + **to sb** + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***intimate me that*** → ✓*intimated to me that*. Also ***intimate to do*** and ***intimate -ing***.
**Reports:** hinting, one register above [hint](#hint) and without the malice of [insinuate](#insinuate). It lives in written English and in reported diplomacy; in speech it reads as affected, and the pronunciation trap — saying it like the adjective — is why many writers leave it alone.

> "It may not be possible for me to continue," he said.  →  He **intimated that** he **might** not continue.

1. The ambassador *intimated* that the deadline could move.
2. She *intimated* to her editor that a second book was under way.
3. He *intimated* as much in his letter of resignation.
4. The chairman *intimated* that the vote would not be close.
5. Nothing was said directly, but the reply *intimated* a refusal.
6. He *intimated* that his support came with conditions.

**See also:** [hint](#hint), [imply](#imply), [insinuate](#insinuate), [suggest](#suggest)

### leak

**Pronunciation:** /lik/ &middot; *LEEK*
**Type:** reporting verb — unauthorized revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral, journalistic
**Patterns:** *leak* + noun phrase &middot; *leak* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *leak* + **that**-clause &middot; passive *be leaked to*
**Never:** ***leak the reporter the memo*** — no double object; the recipient takes *to*: ✓*leaked the memo to the reporter*. Also ***leak to do*** and ***leak -ing***.
**Reports:** releasing information deliberately and without authorization, usually to the press. The unauthorized part is the whole meaning — what an official body puts out is [announced](#announce) or [disclosed](#disclose), never leaked. 3. He *leaked* that the plant would close by summer.
4. Nobody has figured out who *leaked* it.
5. The findings were *leaked* before the study had been peer-reviewed.
6. She *leaked* the roster to a rival team's staff.

### let on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛt ˈɑn/ &middot; *let AHN*
**Type:** reporting verb — revelation, usually under negation &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *let on* + **that**-clause &middot; *let on* + **wh**-clause &middot; *let on* (no complement) &middot; *let on* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *let on* + **to sb** + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***let on sb that*** → ✓*let on to anyone that*. Also ***let on to do***, ***let on -ing***, and no direct object: ✗*He let on the secret*, ✓*He let the secret slip*, ✓*He let on that he knew*.
**Negative polarity:** *let on* lives under negation — *never let on*, *didn't let on*, *without letting on*, *don't let on*. A plain positive (*He let on that he knew*) is grammatical but rare enough to sound slightly off unless a *to*-phrase follows.
**Reports:** allowing someone to find out what you know or feel, usually by failing to hide it. *Let on* leaks a state of mind; [let slip](#let-slip) leaks a fact.

> "I have no idea what you're talking about," he said, though he did.  →  He **never let on that** he **knew**.

1. She knew for weeks and never *let on*.
2. Don't *let on* that I told you.
3. He didn't *let on* how much the treatment had cost.
4. If she was disappointed, she didn't *let on*.
5. He *let on* to a couple of people that he was interviewing elsewhere.
6. Whatever you do, don't *let on* to your sister.
7. He never *let on* about the second interview.

**See also:** [let slip](#let-slip), [give away](#give-away), [confide](#confide), [betray](#betray)

### let slip

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛt ˈslɪp/ &middot; *let SLIP*
**Type:** reporting verb — accidental revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *let slip* + **that**-clause &middot; *let slip* + noun phrase &middot; *let* + noun phrase + *slip* &middot; *let* + **it** + *slip* + **that**-clause &middot; *let slip* + **to sb** + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***let slip to do***, ***let slip -ing***, and ***let slip me that*** → ✓*let slip to me that*. A pronoun object cannot follow *slip*: ✗*let slip it*, ✓*let it slip*.
**Word order:** both *let slip that he was leaving* and *let it slip that he was leaving* are standard; the second is commoner in speech, and the first slightly more literary.
**Reports:** revealing something by accident in the course of saying something else. Where [blurt out](#blurt-out) is sudden and loud, *let slip* is quiet — the speaker often does not notice having done it.

> "…anyway, after the layoffs — oh," he said.  →  He **let slip that** there **had been** layoffs.

1. He *let slip* that the offer had already been accepted.
2. She *let it slip* to a coworker and it was everywhere by noon.
3. Somebody *let slip* the date of the announcement.
4. He *let slip* a detail that only three people knew.
5. Try not to *let* anything *slip* before Friday.
6. She *let slip* to her brother that the house was going on the market.

**See also:** [blurt out](#blurt-out), [give away](#give-away), [let on](#let-on), [betray](#betray)

### own up to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊn ˈʌp tə/ &middot; *OHN UP tuh* — the *to* reduces to a schwa before a verb or a consonant
**Type:** reporting verb — admission &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Patterns:** *own up* (no complement) &middot; *own up* + **to** + noun phrase &middot; *own up* + **to** + **-ing** &middot; *own up* + **to sb** &middot; (literary, without the particle: *own* + **that**-clause)
**Never:** ***own up to do*** — the *to* is a preposition, so what follows it is a noun or an *-ing* form: ✗*owned up to take the money*, ✓*owned up to taking the money*. The *that*-clause is not available either: ✗*He owned up that he had lied*, ✓*He owned up to lying*, ✓*He admitted that he had lied*.
**The bare verb:** *own* by itself once meant "admit," and it survives in formal writing — *I own I was surprised*, *he owned himself beaten*. It sounds deliberately old-fashioned now, and the phrasal *own up to* has taken over the ordinary work.
**Reports:** admitting fault after having tried not to. The evasion is presupposed — nobody owns up to something they were never hiding.

> "All right — I'm the one who broke it," she said.  →  She **owned up to breaking** it.

1. He finally *owned up to* sending the email.
2. Nobody *owned up*, so the whole class stayed late.
3. She *owned up to* her share of the mistake and no more.
4. It took him three days to *own up* to his father.
5. If you *own up* now, the fine is smaller.
6. He has never *owned up to* any part of it.

**See also:** [admit](#admit), [confess](#confess), [come clean](#come-clean), [fess up](#fess-up)

### rebut

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈbʌt/ &middot; *rih-BUT*
**Type:** reporting verb — denial with argument &middot; **Register:** formal, especially legal and academic
**Patterns:** *rebut* + noun phrase &middot; *rebut* + **the claim / charge / argument that** &middot; *rebut* (no complement, in debate) &middot; passive *be rebutted*
**Never:** ***rebut that he was involved*** — no bare *that*-clause; the verb needs a noun phrase, and *the claim that* is the usual bridge: ✓*rebutted the claim that he was involved*. Also ***rebut to do***, ***rebut -ing***, and ***rebut me that***.
**Spelling:** the *t* doubles before a vowel — *rebutted*, *rebutting*, *rebuttal*.
**Reports:** answering a charge with a counterargument. Where [deny](#deny) simply says no, *rebut* presupposes that reasons were given — and unlike [refute](#refute) it makes no claim that the reasons worked.

> "That figure ignores three years of maintenance," the engineer said.  →  The engineer **rebutted** the figure.

1. She *rebutted* the charge point by point.
2. The defense had thirty minutes to *rebut*.
3. His letter *rebuts* the claim that the study was funded by the industry.
4. The presumption can be *rebutted* by documentary evidence.
5. Nobody at the hearing seriously *rebutted* the numbers.
6. The paper *rebuts* an argument that nobody had actually made.

**See also:** [refute](#refute), [dispute](#dispute), [contradict](#contradict), [deny](#deny)

### recant

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈkænt/ &middot; *rih-KANT*
**Type:** reporting verb — withdrawal of a statement &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *recant* + noun phrase &middot; *recant* (no complement) &middot; passive *be recanted*
**Never:** ***recant that***, ***recant -ing***, ***recant to do***, and ***recant me sth***. ✗*He recanted that he had seen her*, ✓*He recanted his statement*, ✓*He recanted*.
**Reports:** publicly taking back something you had professed, usually under pressure and usually a belief, a confession, or testimony. Heavier than [retract](#retract), which can apply to one sentence in a press release; *recant* keeps the weight of heresy trials and witness stands.

> "What I said in that statement wasn't true," the witness said.  →  The witness **recanted** her statement.

1. The witness *recanted* on the second day of the trial.
2. He *recanted* his confession as soon as he had a lawyer.
3. She refused to *recant* anything she had written.
4. Two of the three signers later *recanted*.
5. The scientist was pressed to *recant* and would not.
6. Her earlier testimony was *recanted* but never struck from the record.

**See also:** [retract](#retract), [disavow](#disavow), [deny](#deny)

### refute

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈfjut/ &middot; *rih-FYOOT*
**Type:** reporting verb — disproof &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *refute* + noun phrase &middot; *refute* + **the claim / allegation that** &middot; passive *be refuted*
**Never:** ***refute that he was involved*** — no bare *that*-clause. ✓*refuted the allegation that he was involved*; or, if a simple denial is what you mean, ✓*denied that he was involved*. Also ***refute to do***, ***refute -ing***, and ***refute me that***.
**Contested usage:** in careful American usage *refute* means **disprove** — it says the counterargument succeeded. Press offices and headline writers routinely use it to mean *deny*, and the AP and most newsroom style guides tell them not to, because *He refuted the charge* announces that the charge is false rather than that he said so. The loose use is common enough that dictionaries now record it; it is still the kind of thing an editor changes.

| Verb | What it claims | Neutral? |
|---|---|---|
| *deny* | the speaker says it is false | yes |
| *dispute* | the speaker says it is contestable | yes |
| *rebut* | the speaker answered it with reasons | yes |
| *refute* | the reasons succeeded, so it is false | no — it takes a side |

**Reports:** proving a claim wrong. Use it only when you are prepared to endorse the result.

> "Here are the records showing I was in Denver that week," he said.  →  The records **refuted** the allegation.

1. The paper *refutes* the earlier finding with a much larger sample.
2. Nothing in the file *refutes* her account.
3. He believes he has *refuted* the objection; his critics disagree.
4. The claim was *refuted* within a day by the agency's own data.
5. She set out to *refute* the standard explanation and ended up confirming it.
6. A single receipt *refuted* the whole timeline.

**See also:** [rebut](#rebut), [deny](#deny), [dispute](#dispute), [contradict](#contradict)

### retract

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈtrækt/ &middot; *rih-TRAKT*
**Type:** reporting verb — withdrawal of a statement &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *retract* + noun phrase &middot; passive *be retracted*
**Never:** ***retract that***, ***retract -ing***, ***retract to do***, and ***retract me sth***. ✗*He retracted that he had said it*, ✓*He retracted the remark*.
**Reports:** formally taking back something said or published. Lighter and more procedural than [recant](#recant) — a journal retracts a paper and a spokesman retracts a comment, and neither implies the change of heart that *recant* does.

> "I shouldn't have said that, and I take it back," he said.  →  He **retracted** the remark.

1. The magazine *retracted* the story a week later.
2. He *retracted* the comment but not the sentiment behind it.
3. Two of her statements were *retracted* before the hearing.
4. The journal *retracted* the paper after the images were questioned.
5. She refused to *retract* a single word.
6. He *retracted* the accusation in open court.

**See also:** [recant](#recant), [disavow](#disavow), [deny](#deny), [disclaim](#disclaim)

### reveal

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈvil/ &middot; *rih-VEEL*
**Type:** reporting verb — revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *reveal* + **that**-clause &middot; *reveal* + noun phrase &middot; *reveal* + **wh**-clause &middot; *reveal* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *reveal* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *reveal* + oneself + **as** / **to be** &middot; passive *it was revealed that*
**Never:** ***reveal me the truth*** — no double object; the recipient takes *to*: ✓*revealed the truth to me*, ✓*revealed to me that*. Also ***reveal to do*** and ***reveal -ing***.
**Reports:** making known something that had been hidden. The concealment is presupposed, which is why *revealed* is unfair for a fact that was simply announced, and why a headline saying a study *revealed* a result oversells a study that merely found one.

> "There's a second version of the contract," she said.  →  She **revealed that** there **was** a second version of the contract.

1. The interview *revealed* that she had been offered the job twice.
2. He never *revealed* who had told him.
3. The X-ray *revealed* a second fracture.
4. She *revealed* the plan to her staff an hour before the press got it.
5. It emerged later that the emails had been deleted — a detail no one had *revealed* at the time.
6. The documents *reveal* how the account was set up.

**See also:** [disclose](#disclose), [divulge](#divulge), [announce](#announce), [expose](#expose), [leak](#leak)

### spill

**Pronunciation:** /spɪl/ &middot; *SPIL*
**Type:** reporting verb — revelation &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *spill* + noun phrase (*the beans*, *the details*, *it*, *everything*) &middot; *spill* (no complement, as an invitation) &middot; *spill* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *spill* + noun phrase + **about** + noun phrase (*spill the beans about the party*)
**Never:** ***spill that she was leaving*** — no *that*-clause; say *spilled the news that she was leaving*, or simply *told everyone that she was leaving*. Also ***spill to do***, ***spill -ing***, and ***spill me the details***.
**The idiom:** *spill the beans* is fixed — the beans never become anything else — and it is dated enough that plain *spill* has largely taken over in speech.
**Reports:** telling a secret readily, usually to someone who asked. No fault is presupposed, only a confidence broken, and the register is conversational throughout: it reads as slang in anything formal.

> "Okay, fine — they're getting married in June," she said.  →  She **spilled** the whole thing.

1. He *spilled* the details before I even asked.
2. Come on — *spill*.
3. She *spilled* the beans about the surprise party.
4. Somebody *spilled* everything to a reporter.
5. Two drinks in and he *spilled* the whole story.
6. Don't *spill* it before the announcement goes out.

**See also:** [blurt out](#blurt-out), [give away](#give-away), [let slip](#let-slip), [leak](#leak)

### suggest

**Pronunciation:** /səɡˈdʒɛst/ &middot; *suhg-JEST* — American English pronounces the /ɡ/; British English usually drops it, /səˈdʒɛst/
**Type:** reporting verb — implication (the evidential sense) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *suggest* + **that**-clause &middot; *suggest* + noun phrase &middot; *suggest* + **wh**-clause &middot; *suggest* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it is suggested that*
**Never:** ***suggest someone to do something*** — *suggest* takes no personal object before its complement in either of its senses: ✗*She suggested me to go*, ✗*She suggested him that he leave*. Repairs: ✓*She suggested that I go*, ✓*She suggested going*, or switch verbs — ✓*She advised me to go*. Also ***suggest me that*** → ✓*suggested to me that*.
**Two verbs in one spelling:** the mood of the clause tells them apart.

| Sense | Mood in the clause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| implication — this entry | indicative | *The data suggest that the drug **works**.* |
| proposal — see [suggest](05-advise-suggest.md#suggest) | bare subjunctive | *I suggest that he **be** there by nine.* |

*She suggested that he **was** at the meeting* reports a claim about a fact; *She suggested that he **be** at the meeting* proposes an action. Only the proposal sense takes an *-ing* complement (*suggested leaving*), and it is the proposal sense that draws the American subjunctive.
**Reports:** evidence or wording pointing toward a conclusion without asserting it. Softer than [imply](#imply), which points at an intention the speaker could have made explicit — a set of numbers can suggest something without meaning anything at all.

> "The pattern here looks deliberate to me," the analyst said.  →  The analyst **suggested that** the pattern **was** deliberate.

1. The timing *suggests* that somebody was warned in advance.
2. Are you *suggesting* that the numbers were changed?
3. Everything about the room *suggested* a hasty exit.
4. The evidence *suggests* he had been there before.
5. Her tone *suggested* that the conversation was over.
6. Nothing in the file *suggests* who approved the payment.

**See also:** [imply](#imply), [indicate](#indicate), [hint](#hint), [intimate](#intimate), [suggest (proposal)](05-advise-suggest.md#suggest)

### unveil

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈveɪl/ &middot; *un-VAYL*
**Type:** reporting verb — public revelation &middot; **Register:** neutral; journalistic and promotional
**Patterns:** *unveil* + noun phrase &middot; *unveil* + noun phrase + **at** / **on** / **in** &middot; *unveil* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; passive *be unveiled*
**Never:** ***unveil that***, ***unveil -ing***, ***unveil to do***, and ***unveil sb sth***. ✗*The company unveiled that a new model was coming*, ✓*The company unveiled a new model*.
**Reports:** showing something publicly for the first time. It takes a **thing** rather than a proposition — a product, a plan, a statue, a logo — which is exactly why no clause can follow it. Press releases have worn it thin; [announce](#announce) is the plainer verb and usually the more accurate one.

> "Here it is — the new model," the CEO said.  →  The CEO **unveiled** the new model.

1. The city *unveiled* its budget proposal on Tuesday.
2. They *unveiled* the memorial on the anniversary.
3. The company *unveils* a new phone every September.
4. The plan was *unveiled* at a press conference in Chicago.
5. She *unveiled* the design to a room of skeptical engineers.
6. Two agencies *unveiled* competing proposals in the same week.

**See also:** [announce](#announce), [reveal](#reveal), [disclose](#disclose)

### volunteer

**Pronunciation:** /ˌvɑlənˈtɪr/ &middot; *vah-luhn-TEER*
**Type:** reporting verb — unprompted statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *volunteer* + **that**-clause &middot; *volunteer* + noun phrase &middot; *volunteer* + noun phrase + **to sb** &middot; *volunteer* + **to sb** + **that**-clause &middot; (a different sense: *volunteer* + **to**-infinitive)
**Never:** ***volunteer me that*** → ✓*volunteered to me that*. Also ***volunteer -ing***, and ***volunteer sb sth***: ✗*She volunteered us the information*, ✓*She volunteered the information to us*.
**Two senses:** with a *that*-clause it reports speech nobody asked for (*He volunteered that he'd been fired*); with a *to*-infinitive it reports an offer to act (*He volunteered to drive*). Only the first is a reporting verb.
**Reports:** giving information that was not requested. The absence of a question is the whole meaning — you cannot volunteer an answer to something you were asked.

> "Nobody's asked, but I was there that night," he said.  →  He **volunteered that** he **had been** there that night.

1. She *volunteered* that she had seen the car twice that week.
2. He *volunteered* the information before anyone thought to ask for it.
3. Nobody *volunteered* anything useful about the schedule.
4. The witness *volunteered* that she had known the family for years.
5. He *volunteered* to me that the numbers had already been revised.
6. She *volunteered* nothing and answered only what she was asked.

**See also:** [confide](#confide), [reveal](#reveal), [disclose](#disclose), [let slip](#let-slip)
