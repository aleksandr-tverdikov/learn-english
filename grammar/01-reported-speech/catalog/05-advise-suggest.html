Advising, suggesting, and warning#

The verbs that report someone trying to steer what another person does — advice offered, a course proposed, a danger pointed out, a mind changed.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

Every verb in this file reports a speech act aimed at somebody's future behavior. What separates them is force and direction: suggest floats an option, urge leans on you, warn tells you what will go wrong, dissuade reports that you were pulled back from something you meant to do. They also differ in whether they claim success — urge and advise say only that the words were spoken, while persuade, convince, talk into, and prevail on say the words worked.

But the fault line that actually breaks learners' sentences is grammatical, not semantic. Some of these verbs take a person as a direct object followed by a to-infinitive (advised me to go, warned him not to sign); others refuse a personal object entirely (suggested that I go, never suggested me to go). Nothing about the meaning predicts which group a verb falls into — advise and recommend mean nearly the same thing and behave in opposite ways — so the split has to be learned verb by verb, and it is drawn explicitly below before the entries begin.

The split: object + to-infinitive vs. no personal object#

Group A — takes an object plus a to-infinitive#

The person being steered goes straight after the verb, with no preposition, and the directive becomes a to-infinitive. Negate with not to, never to not.

"You should see a doctor," she said. → She advised me to see a doctor. "Please stay one more day." → They urged us to stay one more day. "Don't sign anything today." → He warned me not to sign anything that day.

advise, urge, warn, caution, counsel, admonish, encourage, exhort, invite, persuade, convince, press, prompt, nudge, push, enjoin, forewarn

Group B — refuses a personal object#

These verbs never put a person between themselves and their complement. To name the person, you either put them inside a that-clause or attach them with a preposition — and the preposition version has to sit alongside the clause, not replace it.

"Let's meet Thursday," she said. → She suggested that we meet Thursday. (not suggested us to meet) "You should try the branzino." → He recommended trying the branzino. (not recommended me to try) "Why don't we postpone?" → She proposed postponing. (not proposed us to postpone)

suggest, recommend, propose, advocate, move, put forward, float, prescribe, endorse, flag

Group C — needs a preposition before the second verb#

The stopping-and-steering verbs. The person is a direct object, but what follows is a preposition plus a gerund, never an infinitive.

"Don't take the job." → She talked me out of taking the job. "You really shouldn't drive tonight." → They dissuaded him from driving that night.

dissuade, discourage, deter, talk into, talk out of, steer — and, with shapes of their own, push and nudge (into + -ing: pushed me into signing) and prevail on, where the person follows the preposition and an infinitive follows the person (prevailed on her to stay).

Group D — takes a person, but carries no instruction#

Two verbs take a personal object and still refuse to do. You alert somebody to a fact or that something is so; you tip somebody off. Neither can hold the content of an instruction, so if what was said was do this, you need a different verb.

"Heads up — the second server is down." → He alerted me that the second server was down. (not alerted me to check it)

alert, tip off — and two more that report an outcome rather than any words at all: win over and egg on take a person and nothing else (won her over, egged him on), with egg someone on to do something the one thin exception.

Three cells deserve a second look. Warn, advise, caution, and counsel all take against, never from — ✗ warned us from going, ✓ warned us against going — while dissuade, deter, and discourage take from and never against. Recommend keeps its against even though it refuses a personal object: The report recommends against expanding the program is standard, and it is the one preposition that verb allows. And prevail on is the odd member of Group C: the object sits after the preposition, and what follows it is an infinitive, not a gerund (prevailed on her to stay).

The three repairs for Group B#

When a Group B verb is what you mean, there are exactly three ways to say it, and one of them is always available.

RepairFormExample
1. that-clauseV that + subject + base formShe suggested that I apply.
2. Bare clausedrop that; the subjunctive staysShe suggested I apply.
3. GerundV + -ing, no person namedShe suggested applying.

Repair 3 throws away the information about who was supposed to act, so use it only where context restores that. If you need to name the person you were speaking to, a preposition can carry them — but only alongside a clause or gerund, never instead of one:

She suggested to me that I apply. ✓ · She suggested to me to apply. ✗ He recommended the place to us. ✓ · He recommended us the place. ✗

That last line is a second, quieter error: Group B verbs take no indirect object either. ✗ recommended me a book, ✗ suggested us a restaurant — the thing recommended comes first and the person follows to.

The mandative subjunctive after advise, suggest, recommend, and propose#

All four of these take a that-clause whose verb is a bare base form — no -s in the third person, no tense at all, regardless of the tense of the reporting verb. This is the mandative subjunctive, and in American English it is the default, not a stylistic flourish. The full treatment is in Reported commands, requests, and advice; what follows is what these four verbs specifically do with it.

Reporting verbSubjunctive clauseNot
adviseShe advised that he be present.that he is present
suggestHe suggested that she leave early.that she leaves early
recommendThey recommended that the report be revised.that the report is revised
proposeThe board proposed that the fee not apply to students.that the fee doesn't apply

"You should really be there by nine," she said. → She advised that I be there by nine. "The committee ought to reconsider." → He proposed that the committee reconsider.

Three things fall out of this.

The negative takes a bare not, with no do. ✓ recommended that he not sign — ✗ that he doesn't sign, ✗ that he not to sign.

It never backshifts. A subjunctive has no tense to move, so a past-tense reporting verb changes nothing: She suggests that he go and She suggested that he go have the identical clause. See When not to backshift.

British English prefers should, and Americans hear it as a different sentence. She suggested that he should leave is entirely normal in Britain and sounds either stiff or oddly permissive in American English. Use the bare form. What you must not do is reach for the plain indicative, because after suggest and its neighbors the indicative changes the meaning outright:

ClauseReads as
She suggested that he be more careful.advice — she told him to be careful
She suggested that he was careless.evidence — she implied it as a fact

Recommend, propose, urge, insist, demand, and move behave the same way. Advise is the mildest of the set and tolerates a should-clause more comfortably than the others, but the bare form is still the American default. For the verb that most often straddles the demand/claim line, see insist.

Which verbs in this file trigger it. Ten: advise, advocate, counsel, enjoin, move, prescribe, propose, recommend, suggest, and urge — plus exhort, which takes a subjunctive clause only in archaic and biblical English (I exhort that supplications be made). The near-misses matter as much, because they look like they should trigger it and do not: encourage, warn, caution, alert, persuade, convince, endorse, float, and put forward take no mandative clause at all. ✗ encouraged that he apply → ✓ encouraged him to apply. ✗ cautioned that the fee be waived → ✓ cautioned against waiving the fee. When one of these verbs does take a that-clause, the clause is an ordinary indicative statement — warned that the storm could stall, cautioned that the figures were preliminary — and it reports a fact, not an instruction.

What these verbs commit the writer to#

Choosing one of these verbs is almost never neutral. Three kinds of commitment ride along with the choice, and a writer who cannot see them will editorialize by accident.

Success. Persuade, convince, talk into, prevail on, win over, dissuade, talk out of, and deter all assert that the words worked. Write she persuaded him to resign and you have said he resigned. If you know only that the attempt was made, the honest verbs are urge, press, push, exhort, encourage, and advise, none of which claims an outcome. The full split is in the table under persuade.

Judgment. Some of these verbs grade the advice as they report it, and the grade is the writer's, not the speaker's. Egg on says the reporter thinks the urging was reckless — nobody is ever egged on to do something sensible. Admonish says the hearer was already at fault. Push and press say the hearer would rather not. Steer says the advice was indirect, and often that it served the adviser. Where you want none of that, suggest, recommend, and tell someone to are the flat options.

Presupposition. A few carry a fact you cannot cancel afterward. Win over presupposes the person started out opposed — you cannot win over someone who already agreed. Tip off presupposes the information was worth having. Forewarn presupposes there was time to act on the warning. Prescribe presupposes authority over the hearer, which is why the doctor prescribed rest and my brother recommended rest are not interchangeable. Deny the presupposition and the sentence collapses: ✗ She won him over, though he had supported her all along.

The entries#

admonish#

Pronunciation: /ədˈmɑnɪʃ/ · ad-MAH-nish Type: reporting verb — warning with reproof · Register: formal Patterns: admonish + sb + to-infinitive · admonish + sb + that-clause · admonish + sb + for + -ing · admonish + sb + against + -ing · admonish + sb (bare object: she admonished me gently) Never: admonish to do something without an object, and admonish someone from doing. The preposition is against for the warning sense and for for the rebuking sense: admonished her for interrupting, admonished her against interrupting again. There is no mandative clause — ✗ admonished that he be present — and no gerund complement: ✗ admonished interrupting. Reports: a warning delivered from a position of authority, mixed with mild reproach — unlike caution, which implies no fault on the hearer's part, admonish says the hearer has already gone wrong or is about to.

"Counselor, do not interrupt the witness again." → The judge admonished him not to interrupt the witness again.

  1. The judge admonished the jury to disregard the remark.
  2. Her editor admonished her for missing a second deadline.
  3. We were admonished against discussing the case outside the room.
  4. He admonished the team that another lapse would mean suspension.
  5. The coach admonished the players to stop arguing with the referee.
  6. She admonished me gently, and I took the point.

See also: caution, warn, counsel, urge, command, charge

advise#

Pronunciation: /ədˈvaɪz/ · ad-VYZE · the noun advice is /ədˈvaɪs/, ad-VYSE, with an /s/ and no plural Type: reporting verb — advice · Register: neutral, tilting formal Patterns: advise + sb + to-infinitive · advise + that-clause (subjunctive) · advise + sb + that-clause · advise + -ing · advise + sb + against + -ing · advise + (sb) + wh-clause · advise + noun phrase · advise + sb + of + noun phrase Never: advise to do something with no object — this is the quiet error inside an otherwise well-behaved verb: ✗ I advise to book early → ✓ I advise booking early / I advise you to book early. The infinitive needs somebody to attach to. Also advise someone from doing something — the preposition is against: advised us against selling. And do not write the noun: I adviced him is not a word. Spelling: advise is spelled -ise in American English too. It is not one of the -ize/-ise pairs, because the ending is not the Greek suffix; advize is wrong everywhere. The noun advice is uncountable — ✗ an advice, ✗ some advices → ✓ a piece of advice, some advice. Subjunctive: advise takes the bare mandative form — advised that she be notified, not that she is notified. Of the four subjunctive verbs in this file it is the one most tolerant of should, but the bare form is still standard American. Reports: the general-purpose advice verb, and the only common one that works in all three frames — object plus infinitive, gerund, and subjunctive clause. Weaker than urge, more authoritative than suggest. The advise someone of something pattern is a separate, bureaucratic sense meaning formally inform: Please advise us of any change of address.

"You should see a doctor about that," she said. → She advised me to see a doctor about it. "I wouldn't sell right now." → He advised against selling right now.

  1. Her lawyer advised her to say nothing until the hearing.
  2. I'd advise waiting until the prices come down.
  3. The doctor advised that he stay off the ankle for two weeks.
  4. We advise against booking through a third party.
  5. The manual advises what to do if the light stays red.
  6. Please advise us of any change in your billing address.
  7. She advised me that the deadline had already passed.
  8. Her agent advised her what to say if anyone asked about the delay.

See also: counsel, recommend, suggest, urge, caution, inform

advocate#

Pronunciation: /ˈædvəkeɪt/ · AD-vuh-kayt · the noun is /ˈædvəkət/, AD-vuh-kut, with a reduced last syllable — the two are spelled alike and said differently Type: reporting verb — public recommendation · Register: formal Patterns: advocate + noun phrase · advocate + -ing · advocate + that-clause (subjunctive) · advocate for + noun phrase or -ing · advocate against + noun phrase or -ing Never: advocate someone to do something, and advocate to do something — the verb takes no infinitive at all, with or without an object: ✗ advocates to build more housing. Repairs: advocated that they be released, advocated releasing them, advocated their release. Reports: open, public support for a policy or course of action — a recommendation made to an audience rather than to an individual. Unlike suggest, it implies the speaker has taken a side and will defend it. Contested: advocate for was condemned for a century as a redundancy, on the grounds that advocate is already transitive. That battle is over in American English: advocate for a cause, advocate for patients is now standard, and it is the only natural option when the object is a person or group rather than a proposal. Keep the bare transitive for policies (advocates a flat tax) and use for for constituencies (advocates for veterans).

"The city needs to build more housing, and I'll keep saying so." → She advocated building more housing.

  1. The report advocates a phased rollout rather than a single launch.
  2. He has advocated for years that the deposit be refundable.
  3. Nobody at the meeting advocated cutting the program entirely.
  4. She advocates for families going through the appeals process.
  5. The senator advocated an immediate freeze on new permits.
  6. They advocate replacing the whole system, which strikes me as expensive.
  7. Two members advocated against holding the vote before the audit.

See also: endorse, propose, recommend, push, argue

alert#

Pronunciation: /əˈlɜrt/ · uh-LURT Type: reporting verb — warning, notification · Register: neutral Patterns: alert + sb + to + noun phrase · alert + sb + to the fact + that-clause · alert + sb + that-clause · alert + noun phrase Never: alert someone to do something — there is no infinitive frame, and the trap is that to is already the verb's preposition, so the wrong sentence looks right. ✗ alerted us to check the logs → ✓ alerted us that the logs needed checking, or simply told us to check the logs. And no bare alert that: an object is required, ✗ alerted that the servers were down. There is no subjunctive either: the that-clause states a fact. Reports: giving someone notice of something they need to know about now, usually a risk or an anomaly. Neutral about blame and silent about what to do next, which is what separates it from warn — you alert someone to a fact, you warn them about a consequence.

"Heads up — two of the servers are down." → He alerted us that two of the servers were down.

  1. A neighbor alerted the fire department.
  2. She alerted me to a typo on the first page.
  3. The bank alerted him that his card had been used overseas.
  4. Nobody alerted the night shift to the change in procedure.
  5. The app alerts you when your balance drops below fifty dollars.
  6. We should alert legal before this goes any further.

See also: tip off, warn, forewarn, flag, notify

caution#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɔʃən/ · KAW-shun Type: reporting verb — warning · Register: formal, common in journalism Patterns: caution + sb + to-infinitive · caution + sb + against + -ing · caution + sb + that-clause · caution + that-clause · caution + sb + about + noun phrase or -ing Never: caution someone from doing something — it is against, like warn and advise: cautioned investors against reading too much into one quarter. And caution that + subjunctive: despite the family resemblance to advise and urge, caution is not a mandative trigger, and its that-clause is an ordinary indicative statement. ✗ cautioned that the fee be waived → ✓ cautioned against waiving the fee / cautioned them not to waive the fee. British: in British usage caution has a police sense with no American equivalent — to caution a suspect is to read them the formal warning before questioning, and a caution is a recorded reprimand issued instead of a prosecution. He was cautioned therefore means something much more specific in Britain than in the United States. Reports: a measured, unalarmed warning — the register of press releases and analysts. Warn says danger; caution says be careful. Note that the objectless caution that is common in reporting (Officials cautioned that the figures were preliminary), where the equivalent warn that would sound more dire.

"Don't read too much into one quarter," the analyst said. → She cautioned investors against reading too much into one quarter.

  1. The mayor cautioned residents to stay off the roads until morning.
  2. Economists cautioned that the drop might be seasonal.
  3. He cautioned me against making an offer on the first day.
  4. The label cautions users about mixing the two solutions.
  5. She cautioned that the numbers had not been audited.
  6. I'd caution you to get that in writing.

See also: warn, admonish, advise, alert

convince#

Pronunciation: /kənˈvɪns/ · kuhn-VINSS Type: reporting verb — persuasion (belief) · Register: neutral Patterns: convince + sb + that-clause · convince + sb + of + noun phrase · convince + sb + to-infinitive Never: convince that with no object — someone has to be convinced: ✗ He convinced that the plan would work → ✓ He convinced us that it would work. And convince someone into doing something is not idiomatic; that frame belongs to talk into and persuade. Reports: a change of belief that actually took hold. The verb is a success verb — you cannot convince someone who remains unconvinced — which distinguishes it from urge and press, both of which report only the attempt. Contested: the traditional rule says convince takes belief (convince that, convince of) and persuade takes action (persuade to do), so convince her to resign was long treated as an error. In current American English it is entirely standard, appears throughout edited prose, and no ordinary reader notices. Use it. The distinction still does real work in the other direction: I convinced him that he was wrong and I persuaded him to admit it are not the same claim.

"Look at the numbers — this can work." → She convinced me that it could work.

  1. It took an hour, but she convinced the committee to reconsider.
  2. Nothing I said convinced him that the check had cleared.
  3. The evidence convinced the jury of his innocence.
  4. I'm not convinced the second option is any cheaper.
  5. He convinced his parents to let him take a year off.
  6. She convinced herself that nobody had noticed.

See also: persuade, talk into, win over, prevail on

counsel#

Pronunciation: /ˈkaʊnsəl/ · KOWN-suhl · identical in sound to council, which is a body of people and never a verb Type: reporting verb — advice · Register: formal, professional Patterns: counsel + sb + to-infinitive · counsel + sb + against + -ing · counsel + that-clause (subjunctive) · counsel + noun phrase · counsel + sb + on/about + noun phrase Never: counsel someone from doing something, and counseled that he should be removed is British-flavored — American practice takes the bare subjunctive, counseled that he be removed. The bare gerund is not the frame either: prefer a noun, counsel delay, over ✗ counsel delaying. British: the past and the -ing form double the l in British spelling — counselled, counselling, counsellor — against American counseled, counseling, counselor. Every example below uses the American form. Reports: advice given in a professional or pastoral capacity, or advice with unusual gravity. Counsel patience, counsel restraint, counsel delay — the bare noun-phrase frame — are the set collocations of this verb, the formal counterpart of advise caution. Reach for the noun rather than a gerund: ✓ counseled delay, ✗ counseled delaying.

"I'd wait before you respond to any of this." → Her attorney counseled her to wait before responding.

  1. His advisor counseled him to defer the semester rather than withdraw.
  2. The elders counseled patience.
  3. We were counseled against putting anything in writing.
  4. She counsels students on financial aid appeals.
  5. The report counsels that the review be conducted by an outside firm.
  6. He counseled restraint, and for once everyone listened.

See also: advise, admonish, recommend, urge

deter#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈtɜr/ · dih-TUR · the past is deterred, with a doubled r Type: reporting verb — discouragement (result) · Register: formal Patterns: deter + sb + from + -ing · deter + sb + from + noun phrase · deter + noun phrase (a person or an activity: deter casual thieves, deter theft) Never: deter someone to do something, deter someone doing something, and deter someone against doing something. The only preposition is from. There is no that-clause: ✗ deterred him that it was risky. Reports: the effect rather than the words — that something stopped a person from acting, whether or not anyone spoke. Its subject is more often a circumstance than a person (the price deterred us), which is what separates it from dissuade, where somebody argued you out of it.

"The permits alone will cost you six thousand dollars." → The cost deterred us from applying.

  1. The fine is too small to deter anyone.
  2. Bad reviews deterred her from booking the same hotel again.
  3. Nothing we said deterred him from going.
  4. Higher premiums have deterred younger drivers from buying coverage.
  5. The lock is mostly there to deter casual thieves.
  6. She was not deterred by the first rejection.
  7. The new fee has deterred a number of families from the program.

See also: dissuade, discourage, talk out of, warn, prohibit

discourage#

Pronunciation: /dɪsˈkɜrɪdʒ/ · dis-KUR-ij Type: reporting verb — discouragement · Register: neutral Patterns: discourage + sb + from + -ing · discourage + sb + from + noun phrase · discourage + -ing · discourage + noun phrase Never: discourage someone to do something and discourage someone against doing something. No that-clause either: ✗ discouraged us that we should apply. And note the asymmetry with its opposite: encourage takes an object plus an infinitive, discourage never does — ✓ encouraged me to apply but ✗ discouraged me to apply → ✓ discouraged me from applying. Reports: the exact opposite number of encourage, and the mildest of the from + -ing group — it reports advice or a policy that pushes against an action without forbidding it. The bare gerund frame is the natural way to state a blanket policy: The city discourages idling.

"I really wouldn't apply for that one," she said. → She discouraged me from applying.

  1. The building discourages deliveries after nine.
  2. My advisor discouraged me from taking two labs in the same term.
  3. Rain discouraged most of the crowd.
  4. The policy is meant to discourage speculative bidding.
  5. Nothing anybody said discouraged her from trying again.
  6. We discourage photography in the reading room.
  7. The forecast discouraged half the group from the longer route.

See also: dissuade, deter, encourage, talk out of, ban

dissuade#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈsweɪd/ · dih-SWAYD Type: reporting verb — persuasion away from an action · Register: formal Patterns: dissuade + sb + from + -ing · dissuade + sb + from + noun phrase · dissuade + sb (+ from it) Never: dissuade someone to do something, dissuade someone of doing something, dissuade someone against doing. Only from. There is no positive verb ✗ suade — the opposite of dissuade is persuade. Reports: successful argument against a course of action — the person had intended to do it and, after the conversation, did not. That success condition is what separates it from discourage, which reports only the push. The near-synonym in ordinary speech is talk out of; dissuade is what you write, talked her out of it is what you say.

"Please don't quit over this," we said. → We dissuaded her from quitting.

  1. His friends dissuaded him from posting the whole thread.
  2. I tried to dissuade her from driving through the pass at night.
  3. Nothing could dissuade them from selling the house.
  4. She was easily dissuaded.
  5. The realtor dissuaded us from bidding above asking.
  6. He talked for an hour and dissuaded nobody.
  7. Her family dissuaded her from the whole scheme in a single evening.

See also: talk out of, deter, discourage, persuade

egg on#

Pronunciation: /ˌɛɡ ˈɔn/ · eg-AWN · stress on on; unrelated to the noun egg, from an Old Norse word eggja meaning to goad — the same root as edge Type: reporting verb — encouragement toward something unwise · Register: informal Patterns: egg + sb + on · egg + sb + on + to-infinitive (less common) Never: egg on him — with a pronoun object the particle must follow it: egged him on, egged them on, never ✗ egged on him. (With a full noun phrase both orders occur — egged the crowd on and egged on the crowd — but the split order is the usual one, and the passive egged on by his friends is the commonest form of all.) There is no that-clause and no gerund frame: ✗ egged him on doing it. Reports: urging someone toward behavior the reporter considers reckless or ill-advised. The judgment is built in: nobody is ever egged on to do something sensible.

"Go on, do it — everyone's watching!" → His friends egged him on.

  1. The crowd egged him on until he climbed the fence.
  2. Don't egg her on; she'll actually do it.
  3. They egged each other on all afternoon.
  4. He needed no egging on.
  5. Two of them egged the argument on from the sidelines.
  6. She egged me on to send the email, and I have regretted it since.

See also: encourage, talk into, nudge, push

encourage#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈkɜrɪdʒ/ · in-KUR-ij Type: reporting verb — encouragement · Register: neutral Patterns: encourage + sb + to-infinitive · encourage + -ing · encourage + noun phrase · encourage + sb + in + noun phrase Never: encourage that with a subjunctive clause — ✗ encouraged that he apply → ✓ encouraged him to apply. And encourage someone that: it takes no that-clause of any kind. The infinitive also needs an object — ✗ The policy encourages to recycle → ✓ encourages recycling / encourages residents to recycle. Reports: positive pressure toward an action the speaker considers good for the hearer. Softer than urge, warmer than advise, and unlike either it says nothing about the speaker's expertise — you encourage a friend, you advise a client.

Like dissuade, it is a success verb: the plan was abandoned.

"Don't quit over one bad week. Sleep on it." → We talked her out of quitting.

  1. His brother talked him out of selling the truck.
  2. I talked myself out of calling her back.
  3. She talked the crew out of walking off the job.
  4. They tried to talk us out of the corner unit, which made us want it more.
  5. He got talked out of it by someone who had done the drive before.
  6. Nobody could talk her out of going.

See also: talk into, dissuade, discourage, deter

tip off#

Pronunciation: /ˌtɪp ˈɔf/ · tip-AWF · the noun is a tip-off, /ˈtɪp ɔf/, TIP-awf, with the stress moved forward and a hyphen Type: reporting verb — confidential warning · Register: informal Patterns: tip + sb + off · tip + sb + off + that-clause · tip + sb + off + about/to + noun phrase · tip off + noun phrase (unsplit, with a full noun object: tipped off investigators) Never: tip off him — a pronoun object must sit inside the phrasal verb: tipped him off, never ✗ tipped off him. (A full noun phrase may go either side: tipped off investigators / tipped investigators off.) And tip off someone to do something: there is no infinitive frame, and no subjunctive — the that-clause states a fact. Reports: passing on information privately, usually about something the hearer was not supposed to know or something illicit. The word carries a whiff of the informant — police are tipped off, reporters are tipped off — and it presupposes the information turned out to be worth having.

"Between us — they're announcing layoffs Tuesday." → Someone tipped her off that layoffs were coming Tuesday.

  1. An employee tipped off investigators about the missing inventory.
  2. She tipped me off that the position was about to be posted.
  3. Somebody must have tipped them off.
  4. He was tipped off to the raid an hour beforehand.
  5. A neighbor tipped off the reporter, and the story ran that week.
  6. The accent tipped me off that she wasn't from around there.

See also: alert, warn, flag, forewarn, inform

urge#

Pronunciation: /ɜrdʒ/ · URJ Type: reporting verb — strong recommendation · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: urge + sb + to-infinitive · urge + that-clause (subjunctive) · urge + noun phrase (urge caution) · urge + sb + on · urge + noun phrase + on/upon + sb (formal) Never: urge someone doing something and urge someone from doing something. The negative is not to: urged us not to wait. Subjunctive: urge is a full mandative trigger — urged that the report be released, urged that she not resign. British writing often has urged that he should be released; American writing does not. Reports: recommending with pressure and often with urgency, and saying nothing about whether it worked. Urge is the standard verb of press releases and official statements (officials urged residents to evacuate), which is why it reads as slightly formal in conversation.

"Please, you have to leave now — the road will close within the hour." → They urged us to leave immediately.

  1. Officials urged residents along the coast to evacuate.
  2. She urged me not to sign anything before Monday.
  3. The letter urges that the hearing be rescheduled.
  4. Her doctor urged caution about the dosage.
  5. He kept urging the horse on.
  6. I'd urge you to read the whole thing before deciding.

See also: press, exhort, advise, encourage, warn, insist, demand

warn#

Pronunciation: /wɔrn/ · WORN · a homophone of worn Type: reporting verb — warning · Register: neutral Patterns: warn + sb + to-infinitive · warn + sb + not to-infinitive · warn + sb + that-clause · warn + that-clause · warn + sb + of/about + noun phrase or -ing · warn + (sb) + against + -ing · warn + sb + off Never: warn someone from doing something — it is against: ✗ warned us from swimming there → ✓ warned us against swimming there. And warn someone to not do something; the negative goes before to. There is no subjunctive: ✗ warned that he be careful → ✓ warned him to be careful. Note that warn is the one warning verb in this file that takes both an infinitive and a clause, so both repairs are always open to it. Reports: notice of a danger or a bad consequence, with the implication that the hearer can still avoid it. Stronger and more alarmed than caution, more consequential than alert — you alert someone to a fact, you warn them what the fact will cost. Note the objectless warn that, standard in news writing: Forecasters warned that the storm could stall.

"Don't sign anything until a lawyer has seen it," she said. → She warned me not to sign anything until a lawyer had seen it.

  1. The ranger warned us about the ice on the upper trail.
  2. Forecasters warned that the storm could stall over the valley.
  3. He warned her that the offer would expire at midnight.
  4. Signs warn swimmers against going out past the buoys.
  5. The guidebook warns against hiking the ridge after noon.
  6. She warned me off the seafood, and she was right.
  7. I warned him, and he did it anyway.

See also: caution, alert, forewarn, admonish, tip off, threaten

win over#

Pronunciation: /ˌwɪn ˈoʊvər/ · win-OH-ver Type: reporting verb — successful persuasion of an opponent · Register: neutral to informal Patterns: win + sb + over · win over + noun phrase · win + sb + over to + noun phrase Never: win someone over to do something and win someone over that. No infinitive, no clause — this verb reports a result and cannot carry the content of what was said. With a pronoun the particle follows: won her over, never won over her. Reports: turning a skeptic or an opponent into a supporter. It says nothing about method and everything about the starting point — you can only win over somebody who was against you, which distinguishes it from persuade, where the hearer may simply have been undecided.

"All right. I still have doubts, but I'm with you." → She won him over.

  1. Her presentation won over the two holdouts on the board.
  2. It took a year to win the neighborhood over.
  3. He won me over with the second draft.
  4. The candidate is trying to win over voters who backed her opponent.
  5. Nobody was won over by the apology.
  6. She won them over to the idea of a shorter season.

See also: persuade, convince, prevail on, talk into
