Admitting, denying, and revealing#

The verbs that report what a speaker finally owned up to, refused to own up to, or let out without meaning to.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

These verbs report a speaker's stance toward a fact rather than the fact itself — that they granted it at last, that they contested it, or that it got out of them. The pattern running through the whole group is the -ing complement where the neutral verbs take a that-clause (admitted taking the money, denied knowing her), together with a flat refusal of the to-infinitive that learners keep reaching for: denied to take, admitted to have taken, and accepted to help are all broken. Most of these verbs also carry a judgment the writer cannot switch off — admit and confess presuppose that the thing admitted counts against the speaker, reveal and disclose presuppose that it was hidden until now, insinuate presupposes that it was never quite said. Choosing one of them over plain said is therefore an editorial act rather than a stylistic one; see Verbs that carry judgment for what each one tells a reader.

accept#

Pronunciation
/əkˈsɛpt/ · uhk-SEPT
Русский
признать, принять — согласиться с тем, чему сопротивлялся; вины не подразумевает
Type
reporting verb — concession
Register
neutral
Patterns
accept + that-clause · accept + noun phrase · accept + noun phrase + as + noun phrase / adjective · passive it is accepted that
Never
accept to do something — accept takes no to-infinitive in any of its senses, and ✗She accepted to help is the most persistent error made with this verb by speakers of French, Spanish, and Italian. Repairs: agreed to help, accepted the job, accepted that she had to help. Also accept me that — no personal object stands before the clause.
Reports
coming around to a proposition you had been resisting, usually after evidence or argument. Weaker than concede, which needs a specific point surrendered in a dispute, and it carries none of the fault that admit does — you can accept a fact that reflects on nobody.

"All right, the numbers are what they are," he said. → He accepted that the numbers were what they were.

  1. The board finally accepted that the launch date had to move.
  2. She accepts that nothing she says is going to change his mind.
  3. Even his critics accept the basic finding.
  4. I'll accept that I was wrong about the route, but not about the time.
  5. The court accepted her account as accurate.
  6. Nobody in the room accepted that the outage had lasted only six hours.

acknowledge#

Pronunciation
/ækˈnɑlɪdʒ/ · ak-NAH-lij
Русский
признать — официально подтвердить, что нечто так; самое нейтральное в ряду, без вины
Type
reporting verb — admission
Register
neutral to formal
Patterns
acknowledge + that-clause · acknowledge + -ing · acknowledge + noun phrase · acknowledge + noun phrase + as / to be · acknowledge + wh-clause · acknowledge + to sb + that-clause · passive it is acknowledged that
Never
acknowledge to do something and acknowledge me that — there is no to-infinitive after this verb, and the listener needs a preposition: ✓acknowledged to me that he was late, ✓acknowledged being late.
Spelling
American English writes acknowledgment, with no e before the -ment, on the same pattern as judgment. British English writes acknowledgement, keeping the e; that spelling is British, not an American variant, and it should not be copied into American work.
Reports
a formal admission that something is so. The lightest verb in the family — admit presupposes fault and confess presupposes guilt, but you can acknowledge a debt, a favor, or an email with nothing at stake.

"Yes, we were late filing," the company said. → The company acknowledged that it had been late filing.

  1. The mayor acknowledged that the city had missed the deadline.
  2. She acknowledged receiving the letter but said she never opened it.
  3. He acknowledged the mistake without apologizing for it.
  4. The study acknowledges what the earlier work got right.
  5. It is widely acknowledged that the original estimate was too low.
  6. He acknowledged to the committee that no one had read the contract.

admit#

Pronunciation
/ədˈmɪt/ · uhd-MIT
Русский
признать, сознаться — уступить в том, что говорит против тебя; вина подразумевается
Type
reporting verb — admission
Register
neutral
Patterns
admit + that-clause · admit + -ing · admit + to + -ing · admit + noun phrase · admit + to + noun phrase · admit + to sb + that-clause · admit + wh-clause · passive it is admitted that · (unrelated senses: admit + sb + to + noun phrase "let in"; admit + of + noun phrase "leave room for")
Never
admit to do something and admit to have done something — the complement is an -ing form or a that-clause, never an infinitive. ✗He admitted to take the money. ✗He admitted to have taken the money. ✓He admitted taking the money. ✓He admitted that he had taken the money. Also admit me that — the listener needs to: ✓admitted to me that.
-ing or to + -ing
both are standard American and they mean the same thing; admitted taking it is a shade more written, admitted to taking it a shade more spoken. The to in the second is a preposition, which is precisely why no infinitive can follow it.
Spelling
the t doubles before a vowel — admitted, admitting — but not in admission.
Two other verbs in the same spelling
admit also means "let in" (She was admitted to the ward that night; British English drops the article — admitted to hospital) and, in formal writing, "leave room for" (The wording admits of no other reading). Neither reports speech, and the first is why admitted to has to be read twice: admitted to the clinic was let in, admitted to taking it owned up.
Reports
conceding something that counts against you. The fault is presupposed rather than asserted, which is why admitted is unfair attribution for a fact the speaker volunteered freely.

allow#

Pronunciation
/əˈlaʊ/ · uh-LOW — the second syllable rhymes with now, not with low
Русский
признать, допустить — книжн., устар.: спокойно уступить пункт; обиходный глагол — concede
Type
reporting verb — concession
Register
formal and somewhat dated in this sense; the everyday allow is the permission verb
Patterns
allow + that-clause · allow + of + noun phrase (allows of no exception) · (in its unrelated permission sense: allow + sb + to-infinitive, allow + -ing, allow + noun phrase)
Never
in the concession sense, allow to do and allow -ing are both impossible — ✗I allow to be wrong, ✗I allow being wrong, ✓I allow that I was wrong. Also allow me that he was right: no personal object stands before the clause. Take care with allow + sb + to-infinitive, which is perfectly grammatical but is the other verb — I allow him to be right grants him permission and concedes nothing.
Two verbs in one spelling
The historian allows that the date may be wrong concedes a point; The landlord allows pets grants permission. The that-clause is the reliable signal — only the concession verb takes one.
Regional
allow as how survives in Southern and rural American speech (He allowed as how it might rain) and reads as dialect anywhere else. The complementizer as how has its own entry under conjunctions.
Reports
granting a point, in a register that now sounds bookish. Like concede it presupposes resistance — nothing gets allowed that was not first in doubt — but the surrender is calm rather than forced out. Choose it when the concession is being made in writing; concede is the everyday verb.

"Fine — the argument has something to it," she said. → She allowed that the argument had something to it.

  1. The historian allows that the letter may be a forgery.
  2. I'll allow that the price was fair, given the market.
  3. He allowed that he had been unfair to her.
  4. Even the dissent allows that the statute is ambiguous.
  5. She allowed as how the drive would take most of a day.
  6. Critics generally allow that the early novels are the stronger ones.
See also
concede, grant, accept, acknowledge

announce#

Pronunciation
/əˈnaʊns/ · uh-NOWNSS
Русский
объявить, огласить — сделать нечто публичным и официальным
Type
reporting verb — public statement, often a revelation
Register
neutral
Patterns
announce + that-clause · announce + noun phrase · announce + wh-clause · announce + noun phrase + to sb · announce + to sb + that-clause · passive it was announced that
Never
announce me the news and announce me that — announce never takes a bare indirect object, and the audience always goes into a to-phrase. ✗She announced us the results. ✓She announced the results to us. ✓She announced to us that the results were in. Also announce to do.
Word order
when there is both a listener and a clause, the to-phrase comes first: announced to the staff that the office was closing. Putting it last (✗announced that the office was closing to the staff) attaches it to the wrong verb and says something absurd.
Reports
making something public and official. Where reveal presupposes concealment and leak presupposes that the release was unauthorized, announce presupposes only that the speaker had the standing to say it.

"We're closing the Denver office," the CEO said. → The CEO announced that the company was closing the Denver office.

  1. She announced that she was leaving at the end of the month.
  2. The district announced the closure by text at six in the morning.
  3. He announced to the whole table that he had gotten the job.
  4. They still haven't announced who won.
  5. It was announced on Friday that the merger had fallen through.
  6. My brother announced his engagement on a group call.
See also
reveal, disclose, unveil, leak

betray#

Pronunciation
/bɪˈtreɪ/ · bih-TRAY
Русский
выдать, невольно обнаружить — о голосе, лице, паузе: показать то, что скрывали
Type
reporting verb — unintentional revelation
Register
neutral to literary
Patterns
betray + noun phrase · betray + the fact that · betray + wh-clause · betray + no / little + noun phrase · betray + oneself
Never
betray that he was nervous — the bare that-clause is dispreferred in careful writing; use a noun phrase or the the fact that bridge: ✓betrayed his nervousness, ✓betrayed the fact that he was nervous. Also betray to do, betray -ing, and betray me that.
The subject is usually a sign, not a speaker
a voice, a face, an accent, a pause. The person given away appears as the object: her voice betrayed her.
Reports
letting something show without meaning to. The other betray — turning on someone — is the same word, and the overlap is why this one always carries a faint suggestion that the revealer was on the wrong side of the secret.

"I'm fine, really," she said. → Her voice betrayed how little she meant it.

  1. His accent betrayed him within about five words.
  2. Her expression betrayed nothing at all.
  3. The letter betrays the fact that he already knew about the sale.
  4. He betrayed no surprise when the verdict was read out.
  5. A pause on the line betrayed how bad the news was going to be.
  6. She betrayed herself by asking about a detail nobody had told her.
See also
give away, let slip, let on, reveal

Русский
выпалить, ляпнуть — сказать сгоряча, не подумав, то, что собирался утаить
Type
reporting verb — unintentional revelation
Register
neutral, leaning informal
Patterns
blurt out + that-clause · blurt out + noun phrase · blurt + noun phrase + out · blurt out + wh-clause · blurt out + direct quotation
Never
blurt out to do, blurt out -ing, and blurt me out that. The particle also cannot follow a pronoun object: ✗blurted out it, ✓blurted it out.
Separable

  4. I blurted out the first number that came into my head.
  5. Someone in the back blurted out what everyone was thinking.
  6. He blurted it out and then spent an hour apologizing for it.

come clean#

Pronunciation
/kʌm ˈklin/ · kum KLEEN
Русский
признаться начистоту — разг.: наконец сказать правду после долгого сокрытия
Type
reporting verb — admission
Register
informal
Patterns
come clean (no complement) · come clean + about + noun phrase · come clean + about + -ing · come clean + with sb · come clean + to sb + about
Never
come clean that he had lied — the idiom takes no that-clause at all. Repairs: came clean about lying, admitted that he had lied. Also come clean to do and come clean the truth — there is no direct object either.
Fixed form
clean here is an adjective and never inflects — came clean, coming clean, never ✗came cleanly.
Reports
finally telling the truth after a stretch of concealment. It presupposes a prior lie or cover-up more strongly than admit does, and the tone is conversational throughout.

"All right — I've been out of work since March," he said. → He finally came clean about having been out of work since March.

  1. He came clean about the credit card debt.
  2. She decided to come clean with her parents over dinner.
  3. It took him a year to come clean about missing the deadline.
  4. If you come clean now, this stays between us.
  5. The company never came clean about how long the data had been exposed.
  6. I'd rather come clean than keep patching the story.

concede#

Pronunciation
/kənˈsid/ · kuhn-SEED
Русский
уступить, признать — сдать пункт под давлением; предполагает предшествующий спор
Type
reporting verb — concession
Register
neutral to formal
Patterns
concede + that-clause · concede + noun phrase · concede + noun phrase + to sb · concede + to sb + that-clause · passive it is conceded that
Never
concede to do and concede me that — the listener needs to: ✓conceded to reporters that the race was over. The bare -ing that admit takes so easily is unnatural here: ✗conceded making a mistake, ✓conceded that he had made a mistake. The wh-clause is shaky too — He would not concede how much it had cost wants admit.
Reports
giving up a point under pressure, in an argument or a contest. What it presupposes is not fault but resistance — there has to have been a dispute for anything to be conceded. In journalism it is as loaded as admitted, and it should be used only when the resistance is on the record.

"All right, your figures are better than mine," she said. → She conceded that my figures were better than hers.

  1. He conceded that the first draft had been rushed.
  2. She conceded the point but not the argument.
  3. The senator conceded the race a little after midnight.
  4. Even the study's authors concede that the sample was small.
  5. She would not concede that any part of it had been her idea.
  6. The company conceded two of the five claims and fought the rest.
  7. He conceded to reporters that the campaign had run out of money.

confess#

Pronunciation
/kənˈfɛs/ · kuhn-FESS
Русский
сознаться, признаться в вине — в том, за что могут осудить или наказать
Type
reporting verb — admission of guilt
Register
neutral to formal
Patterns
confess + that-clause · confess + to + -ing · confess + to + noun phrase · confess + noun phrase · confess + noun phrase + to sb · confess + to sb · confess + to sb + that-clause · confess (no complement)
Never
confess to do — the to is a preposition, so no infinitive can follow it: ✗confessed to steal the car, ✓confessed to stealing the car. Also confess me that, which needs to: ✓confessed to her that. The bare -ing without to is possible but dispreferred — confessed to taking it beats confessed taking it.

Two things to can introduce: the offense or the audience. He confessed to the burglary names what he did; He confessed to the detective names who heard it. When a sentence needs both, one of them has to move — He confessed the burglary to the detective, or He confessed to the detective that he had done it.

Reports
admitting to something you can be blamed or punished for. Stronger than admit by exactly the distance between fault and guilt, and it keeps a religious and legal residue — you confess to a priest, to the police, or to a court. The weakened social use (I confess I've never read it) is a politeness formula and carries none of that.

"It was me. I broke it," he said. → He confessed to breaking it. / He confessed that he had broken it.

  1. He confessed to taking the cash out of the register.
  2. She confessed that she had known about it for months.
  3. Two suspects confessed within the first day.
  4. I'll confess I've never actually finished the book.
  5. He confessed his part in it to the auditors.
  6. After six hours she still hadn't confessed.
See also
admit, own up to, come clean, acknowledge

contradict#

Pronunciation
/ˌkɑntrəˈdɪkt/ · kahn-truh-DIKT
Русский
возразить, уличить в неправде — дополнение здесь лицо, а не утверждение (ср. deny)
Type
reporting verb — denial of someone else's statement
Register
neutral
Patterns
contradict + sb · contradict + noun phrase · contradict + oneself · passive be contradicted by
Never
contradict that — no that-clause of any kind. ✗He contradicted that he had been there. Use deny for a speaker's denial about himself (He denied that he had been there), and contradict plus a noun phrase for the rejection of somebody else's statement (He contradicted her account). Reports
saying that what someone else said is untrue. Alone among the denial verbs it takes a person as its object — you contradict her, but you deny the claim — and it needs a prior statement to work on: with nothing on the record, there is nothing to contradict.

  2. The second witness contradicted the first on every detail.
  3. He contradicted himself twice in the same paragraph.
  4. The lab results contradict the company's timeline.
  5. She contradicted the official account within an hour of reading it.

dispute#

Pronunciation
/dɪˈspjut/ · dih-SPYOOT as a verb; the noun is usually /ˈdɪspjut/ · DISS-pyoot
Русский
оспаривать — заявлять, что утверждение спорно, а не прямо ложное (ср. deny)
Type
reporting verb — denial
Register

Patterns

Never
dispute to do, dispute -ing, and dispute me that. ✗He disputed having said it, ✓He disputed that he had said it, ✓He denied saying it.
A negative habit
the that-clause version turns up under negation far more often than not — no one disputes that, nobody is disputing that, few would dispute that. That frame asserts something while appearing merely to report agreement, and a careful reader notices.

challenging a claim as wrong without necessarily denying it outright. Deny says it is false; dispute says it is contestable, which is how the same fact can be disputed by both sides at once.

  3. The union disputed the company's account of the meeting.
  4. Historians still dispute whether the letter is genuine.
  5. He disputed every line of the invoice.
  6. The finding has been disputed by two other labs.

"Okay, fine — they're getting married in June," she said. → She spilled the whole thing.

  1. He spilled the details before I even asked.
  2. Come on — spill.
  3. She spilled the beans about the surprise party.
  4. Somebody spilled everything to a reporter.
  5. Two drinks in and he spilled the whole story.
  6. Don't spill it before the announcement goes out.

suggest#

Pronunciation
/səɡˈdʒɛst/ · suhg-JEST — American English pronounces the /ɡ/; British English usually drops it, /səˈdʒɛst/
Русский
наводить на мысль, свидетельствовать — здесь о данных и уликах; значение «предлагать» — другое
Type
reporting verb — implication (the evidential sense)
Register
neutral
Patterns
suggest + that-clause · suggest + noun phrase · suggest + wh-clause · suggest + to sb + that-clause · passive it is suggested that
Never
suggest someone to do something — suggest takes no personal object before its complement in either of its senses: ✗She suggested me to go, ✗She suggested him that he leave. Repairs: ✓She suggested that I go, ✓She suggested going, or switch verbs — ✓She advised me to go. Also suggest me that → ✓suggested to me that.
Two verbs in one spelling
the mood of the clause tells them apart.
SenseMood in the clauseExample
implication — this entryindicativeThe data suggest that the drug works.
proposal — see suggestbare subjunctiveI suggest that he be there by nine.

She suggested that he was at the meeting reports a claim about a fact; She suggested that he be at the meeting proposes an action. Only the proposal sense takes an -ing complement (suggested leaving), and it is the proposal sense that draws the American subjunctive.

Reports
evidence or wording pointing toward a conclusion without asserting it. Softer than imply, which points at an intention the speaker could have made explicit — a set of numbers can suggest something without meaning anything at all.

"The pattern here looks deliberate to me," the analyst said. → The analyst suggested that the pattern was deliberate.

  1. The timing suggests that somebody was warned in advance.
  2. Are you suggesting that the numbers were changed?
  3. Everything about the room suggested a hasty exit.
  4. The evidence suggests he had been there before.
  5. Her tone suggested that the conversation was over.
  6. Nothing in the file suggests who approved the payment.

Reports
showing something publicly for the first time. It takes a thing rather than a proposition — a product, a plan, a statue, a logo — which is exactly why no clause can follow it. Press releases have worn it thin; announce is the plainer verb and usually the more accurate one.

"Here it is — the new model," the CEO said. → The CEO unveiled the new model.

  2. They unveiled the memorial on the anniversary.
  3. The company unveils a new phone every September.
  4. The plan was unveiled at a press conference in Chicago.
  5. She unveiled the design to a room of skeptical engineers.
  6. Two agencies unveiled competing proposals in the same week.

Pronunciation
/ˌvɑlənˈtɪr/ · vah-luhn-TEER
Русский
сказать по своей инициативе — сообщить то, о чём никто не спрашивал
Type
reporting verb — unprompted statement
Register
neutral
Patterns
volunteer + that-clause · volunteer + noun phrase · volunteer + noun phrase + to sb · volunteer + to sb + that-clause · (a different sense: volunteer + to-infinitive)
Never
volunteer me that → ✓volunteered to me that. Also volunteer -ing, and volunteer sb sth: ✗She volunteered us the information, ✓She volunteered the information to us.
Two senses
with a that-clause it reports speech nobody asked for (He volunteered that he'd been fired); with a to-infinitive it reports an offer to act (He volunteered to drive). Only the first is a reporting verb.
Reports
giving information that was not requested. The absence of a question is the whole meaning — you cannot volunteer an answer to something you were asked.

"Nobody's asked, but I was there that night," he said. → He volunteered that he had been there that night.

  1. She volunteered that she had seen the car twice that week.
  2. He volunteered the information before anyone thought to ask for it.
  3. Nobody volunteered anything useful about the schedule.
  4. The witness volunteered that she had known the family for years.
  5. He volunteered to me that the numbers had already been revised.
  6. She volunteered nothing and answered only what she was asked.
