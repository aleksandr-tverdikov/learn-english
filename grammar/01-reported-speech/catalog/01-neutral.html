Neutral reporting verbs#

The workhorses of reported speech — the verbs that tell you someone said something without telling you what to think about it.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

A neutral reporting verb makes no claim about whether the message was true, whether the speaker had any right to say it, or how the speaker felt while saying it. Substitute claimed, admitted, or insisted into any sentence in this file and you have added a judgment that was not there before; substitute stated or mentioned or noted and you have only changed the weight and the register. What actually varies inside this group is not attitude but grammar — specifically, how each verb handles the listener: some refuse one outright (say, mention, announce), some demand one with no preposition (tell me, inform me, notify me), and some accept one only behind a preposition (explain to me, describe to me, share with me, discuss it with me). That one distinction produces nearly every error learners make with these verbs, and it is why say and tell, which mean almost the same thing, can never be swapped for each other.

The anchor: say vs. tell#

These two carry more reported speech than every other verb in English combined, and they split on exactly one point: the person you are talking to.

saytell
Personal objectNever — say me is impossibleRequired — tell that is impossible
How the listener attachesBehind to: said to meBare, immediately after the verb: told me
With a that-clauseShe said (that) she was tired.She told me (that) she was tired.

The fatal errorShe said me she was tired.She told to me she was tired.

Two sentences that mean the same thing, correctly built each way:

"I'm not coming," she said. → She said (to me) that she wasn't coming. "I'm not coming," she said. → She told me that she wasn't coming.

The three listener patterns#

Almost every verb in this file falls into one of three groups. Learn the group and you have learned the verb.

GroupThe listener is…VerbsCorrectWrong
No objectimpossible, or only behind tosay, mention, announce, state, remark, comment, note, observe, add, continue, report, indicate, repeat, relate, recount, convey, communicate, describe, explain, writeexplained to me that…explained me that…
Bare object requiredmandatory, with no prepositiontell, inform, notify, advise — and, catalogued in other files, remind, assure, convinceinformed me that…informed that…
Prepositional objectattaches with with, not toshare, discussshared the news with meshared me the news

Four entries sit outside the three groups because they never name a listener at all: quote, put it, go on, and the two fixed phrases. Quote is the one to watch, because it does take a bare personal object — but that object is the person quoted, not the person spoken to: Don't quote me means "don't attribute this to me." Mention has the same trap in reverse: He mentioned you makes you the subject of the remark, never its audience.

The one thing almost no verb here allows is the double-object pattern with an abstract message: informed me the change, notified us the date, announced them the results, explained me the rule. English gives that frame to tell — told me the answer — and, for something written, to write: wrote me a long letter. Nothing else in this file takes it.

What each verb lets you put after it#

The listener is one axis; the complement is the other. Most errors that survive the say/tell rule are errors of complement — a that-clause after a verb that refuses one, or a person plus a to-infinitive after a verb that has no such frame.

ComplementVerbs here that take itVerbs here that refuse it
that-clausesay, tell (+ sb), advise (+ sb), add, announce, comment, communicate, continue, convey, explain, indicate, inform (+ sb), mention, note, notify (+ sb), observe, relate, remark, repeat, report, share, state, writedescribe, discuss, quote outright; recount, put it, and go on only stiffly or not at all in American prose
wh-clauseannounce, communicate, convey, describe, discuss, explain, indicate, inform (+ sb), mention, note, observe, recount, relate, repeat, report, share, state, tell (+ sb), write, advise (+ sb); say only under negation or a modal (I couldn't say what he meant)comment and remark, which need on first — remarked on how much it had changed; notify, which needs of — notify us of what you decide
-ingcontinue, discuss, go on, mention, report; and behind as, describe and quotesay, tell, state, announce, explain, inform — no -ing complement at all
person + to-infinitivetell, and nothing else in this fileevery other verb here: said me to wait, explained me to wait, informed me to wait, mentioned me to wait, wrote me to come

The last row is the one to memorize. Advise looks like an exception — advised us to leave early — but that is the other advise, the one that recommends, and it lives in advise, suggest. In the notification sense catalogued here, advise takes a that-clause, not an infinitive.

The verbs, A–Z#

add#

Pronunciation: /æd/ · AD Type: reporting verb — a further statement · Register: neutral Patterns: add + that-clause · add + direct quotation · add + noun phrase · add + noun phrase + to + noun phrase · impersonal it should be added that Never: add me that / add someone something — add takes no personal object at all, and unlike explain or announce it cannot rescue one with a preposition either: added to me that is not the repair. The to after add names what the thing was added to, never who heard it. Repairs: added that the lot was full, added it to the list, added, turning to me, that she was leaving. Requires something to add to: add cannot open a report. It means "and then this, from the same speaker," so there has to be a then. A paragraph that begins She added that the store was closed sends the reader hunting backward for the sentence it was added to. Reports: an extra piece appended to what the same speaker just said. Neutral about the content, but it does commit you to the claim that both statements came from one speaker in one stretch of talk.

"It's closed. And the lot is full," he said. → He said the store was closed and added that the lot was full.

  1. She said the flight was delayed, then added that nobody had told the gate agent.
  2. "And bring a jacket," he added.
  3. The senator added that she would release the full record next week.
  4. He added a line at the bottom of the form and initialed it.
  5. I should add that none of this is official yet.
  6. "We'll be late," she said, and then added, "very late."

See also: continue, go on, mention, note

advise#

Pronunciation: /ədˈvaɪz/ · uhd-VYZE Type: reporting verb — statement (the inform sense) · Register: formal — business, legal, and administrative writing Patterns: advise + sb + that-clause · advise + sb + of + noun phrase · advise + sb + wh-clause · advise + that-clause (impersonal, no listener named) · passive be advised that / be advised of · Please advise. (fixed, standing alone) Never: advise me the total — no double object. The message attaches with of: advise me of the total. Also never advise to me — like tell, this verb takes the person bare. And an infinitive after it silently switches verbs on you: advised me that the road was closed is information, advised me to take the freeway is a recommendation. Two different verbs, one spelling: the sense here is "inform." The much commoner sense — "recommend a course of action," advised us to leave early — behaves differently and lives at advise. Only context separates them: He advised me that the road was closed is information; He advised me to take the freeway is a recommendation. Reports: a formal notification, usually written and usually from an institution. In ordinary American speech it sounds stiff enough to be a small joke — nobody advises a friend that dinner is ready, they tell them.

"Your policy expires on June 1," the letter said. → The letter advised me that my policy would expire on June 1.

  1. Please be advised that the office will close at noon on Friday.
  2. Counsel advised the court that his client would not testify.
  3. The bank advised us of a change to the account terms.
  4. She advised the committee when the audit would begin.
  5. We will advise you as soon as the shipment leaves the warehouse.
  6. I've attached the invoice. Please advise.

See also: inform, notify, tell, advise (= recommend)

announce#

Pronunciation: /əˈnaʊns/ · uh-NOWNSS Type: reporting verb — public statement · Register: neutral, tilting formal Patterns: announce + that-clause · announce + noun phrase · announce + wh-clause · announce + direct quotation · announce + to + sb + that-clause · announce + noun phrase + to + sb · passive it was announced that Never: announce me that / announce us the results — no personal object without to. Also announce about the merger — the topic attaches directly or goes in a clause, never behind about. Repairs: announced the results to us, announced to us that the merger was off, announced the merger. Word order: this is one of the few verbs here where the to-phrase comfortably precedes the that-clause — announced to the staff that the office was moving is better English than announced that the office was moving to the staff, which briefly says the office is moving to the staff. Reports: a statement made publicly, formally, or at least to a group. It presupposes an audience, so it sounds oversized for one listener: He announced to me that he was hungry is a joke about self-importance.

"The merger closed this morning," the CEO said. → The CEO announced that the merger had closed that morning.

  1. The airline announced that all flights out of Denver were canceled.
  2. She announced her resignation in a two-line email.
  3. The school announced to parents that classes would resume Tuesday.
  4. Nobody had announced who was replacing him.
  5. They announced the winners in reverse order.
  6. He walked in and announced that dinner was ruined.

See also: report, state, say, notify

comment#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɑmɛnt/ · KAH-ment Type: reporting verb — a brief remark, often evaluative · Register: neutral, common in journalism Patterns: comment + that-clause · comment + on / upon + noun phrase · comment + on + wh-clause · comment + direct quotation · comment + to + sb + that-clause · No comment. (fixed noun use) Never: comment the change — the topic attaches with on: commented on the change. Also never comment me that; the listener, if you need one, goes behind to: commented to reporters that… Reports: a short opinion offered about something already in view. Unlike remark, which is neutral about content, comment leans slightly toward evaluation — you comment on how something is, not merely that it is.

"The wording is unusually vague," she said. → She commented that the wording was unusually vague.

  1. He commented that the second draft was much tighter than the first.
  2. Several neighbors commented on the noise.
  3. The mayor declined to comment on the investigation.
  4. "Nice of them to tell us," she commented.
  5. Readers commented under the article for three days.
  6. She commented to a colleague that the numbers looked padded.

See also: remark, observe, note, say

communicate#

Pronunciation: /kəˈmjunəˌkeɪt/ · kuh-MYOO-nuh-kayt Type: reporting verb — transmission of a message · Register: formal Patterns: communicate + that-clause · communicate + noun phrase · communicate + noun phrase + to + sb · communicate + to + sb + that-clause · communicate + wh-clause · communicate + with + sb (intransitive — "be in contact") · passive be communicated to sb Never: communicate me the results — no double object. Repair: communicate the results to me. Note that communicate with me is fine but means something else: it is contact, not content. Reports: the successful transfer of a message, by any channel — speech, writing, gesture, a look. It is the most abstract verb in this file and the one most likely to be corporate filler: We need to communicate that to the team almost always improves as We need to tell the team.

"The deadline has moved to the 15th," she told the group. → She communicated to the group that the deadline had moved to the 15th.

  1. The board communicated its decision to shareholders that afternoon.
  2. Somehow he communicated that he was in pain without saying a word.
  3. Please communicate any schedule changes to the front desk.
  4. The two departments barely communicate with each other.
  5. She communicated what the client wanted far more clearly than the brief did.
  6. Nothing about the layoffs was communicated until Friday.

See also: convey, inform, indicate, tell

continue#

Pronunciation: /kənˈtɪnju/ · kuhn-TIN-yoo Type: reporting verb — resumption after a pause · Register: neutral, mostly written narrative Patterns: continue + direct quotation (usually as an inverted tag) · continue + that-clause (journalistic) · continue + noun phrase · continue + to-infinitive · continue + -ing Never: continue me that — no personal object. And take care with the last two patterns: continued to speak and continued speaking report that the talking went on, not what was said. Only the first two patterns report a message. Reports: that the same speaker picked back up after a break — a breath, an interruption, a paragraph. Like add, it cannot start a report, because there has to be something to continue from.

"…and that's the whole story," she said. "Or most of it," she continued. → She said that was the whole story, then continued that it was really only most of it.

  1. "As I was saying," he continued, "the numbers don't add up."
  2. She paused for a sip of water and continued.
  3. The witness continued that he had never met the defendant before that night.
  4. He continued reading aloud even after everyone had left.
  5. "There's one more thing," she continued, quieter now.
  6. The report continues that no single factor explains the collapse.

See also: go on, add, repeat, say

convey#

Pronunciation: /kənˈveɪ/ · kuhn-VAY Type: reporting verb — transmission of content or feeling · Register: formal Patterns: convey + that-clause · convey + noun phrase · convey + noun phrase + to + sb · convey + to + sb + that-clause · convey + wh-clause Never: convey me the message / convey me that — no personal object of any kind without to. Repairs: convey the message to me, conveyed to me that… Reports: the getting-across of something, with emphasis on what arrived rather than on the words used. It is the verb of choice for content that was never quite stated: Her tone conveyed that the meeting was over works, while Her tone said that the meeting was over is a metaphor and Her tone told us needs the object. Nearest neighbor: communicate stresses the channel and the act; convey stresses the cargo. You communicate with a colleague; you convey sympathy.

"Please tell them how sorry I am," he said. → He asked me to convey his apologies to them.

  1. Her expression conveyed that she had already heard.
  2. Please convey our congratulations to the whole team.
  3. The letter conveyed the board's decision without explaining it.
  4. It's hard to convey how loud the room was.
  5. He conveyed to the family that there was nothing more to be done.
  6. The translation loses almost everything the original conveys.

See also: communicate, indicate, express, tell

describe#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈskraɪb/ · dih-SKRYBE Type: reporting verb — an account of something · Register: neutral Patterns: describe + noun phrase · describe + wh-clause · describe + how-clause · describe + noun phrase + to + sb · describe + to + sb + how / wh-clause · describe + noun phrase + as + adjective / noun phrase / -ing Never: describe me the room — the listener attaches with to: describe the room to me. And describe that the room was dark — describe takes no that-clause at all. And describe about the room — the object attaches directly, with no preposition. Repairs: described the room as dark, described how dark the room was, described the darkness. Reports: the giving of an account. Note the as-frame, which is where describe does its most useful reporting work: He described the deal as dead attributes the characterization to him and quietly declines to endorse it.

"It was maybe twenty feet long, and gray," she said. → She described the animal as about twenty feet long and gray.

  1. Can you describe what you saw?
  2. She described the apartment to me over the phone, room by room.
  3. The report describes the response as slow and poorly coordinated.
  4. He described how the machine had failed, one step at a time.
  5. Witnesses described a man in a red jacket.
  6. I don't know how to describe it — you had to be there.

See also: explain, recount, relate, report, discuss

discuss#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈskʌs/ · dih-SKUSS Type: verb of talk — names the topic, not the message · Register: neutral Patterns: discuss + noun phrase · discuss + wh-clause · discuss + -ing · discuss + noun phrase + with + sb · passive be discussed Never: discuss about the budget — the topic attaches with no preposition at all: discuss the budget. This is the most persistent preposition error in English and it survives well into advanced writing, because talk about, argue about, and complain about all take one. Also discuss that the budget was tight — discuss takes no that-clause, no to-infinitive, and no direct quotation. And discuss me the budget / discuss the budget to me — the other person attaches with with: discuss it with me. It reports that talking happened, not what was said. That is why it cannot do reported speech proper: there is no way to convert a quotation into discussed. "The budget is tight," she said becomes She said the budget was tight, never She discussed that the budget was tight. What discuss can report is the subject matter, and the reader is told nothing about who said what or how it came out. Reports: an exchange of talk on a topic, between two or more people, with no claim about the content, the conclusion, or who held which view.

"Let's go over the budget." — "Fine. Here's where we are." → They discussed the budget. (Not discussed that the budget was tight — the content is gone.)

  1. We discussed the budget for nearly two hours and settled nothing.
  2. I'd rather discuss this in person.
  3. She discussed the offer with her lawyer before signing anything.
  4. They never discussed what would happen if the funding fell through.
  5. The board discussed moving the deadline to March.
  6. Salaries are not discussed openly here.

See also: share, communicate, say, tell, describe

explain#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈspleɪn/ · ik-SPLAYN Type: reporting verb — statement that makes something understood · Register: neutral Patterns: explain + that-clause · explain + wh-clause · explain + how/why-clause · explain + noun phrase · explain + to + sb + that-clause · explain + to + sb + wh-clause · explain + noun phrase + to + sb · explain (intransitive) · passive it was explained that Never: explain me something / explain me that / explain me how — one of the three or four most common errors in English. Explain never takes a bare personal object. Nor does it take a person plus an infinitive: explained me to wait is impossible in every register, because that frame belongs to tell. Repairs: explain it to me, explain to me why, explained to the class that…, and for the infinitive, told me to wait. Word order: when both a listener and a that-clause are present, the listener comes first — explained to me that the machine was old. Putting the to-phrase at the end strands it: explained that the machine was old to me reads as though the machine's age belonged to me. Reports: a statement offered to remove confusion. It presupposes that something needed clearing up, which is why it is not fully interchangeable with say: He said the door was locked is information; He explained that the door was locked implies you had been wondering why you couldn't get in.

"The heater only runs at night," he said. → He explained to me that the heater only ran at night.

  1. She explained that the deposit wouldn't clear until Monday.
  2. Could you explain the difference to me?
  3. He never explained why he left.
  4. The manual doesn't explain how to reset the timer.
  5. I tried to explain, but nobody was listening.
  6. Let me explain it to you one more time.

See also: describe, tell, say, point out

go on#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊ ˈɑn/ · goh-ON Type: reporting verb — resumption · Register: neutral, conversational Patterns: go on + to say / to explain / to add / to argue + that-clause · go on + direct quotation · go on + -ing · go on + about + noun phrase · go on + with + noun phrase Never: go on me — no personal object. And go on + a bare that-clause (He went on that the budget was fixed) is marginal at best in American English; edited prose writes went on to say that. The two -ing / to-infinitive readings are opposites:

FrameMeansExample
went on to saysaid the next thingShe finished the summary and went on to say the budget was frozen.
went on sayingkept saying the same thingShe went on saying the budget was frozen long after it wasn't.
went on abouttalked at tedious lengthHe went on about the budget for twenty minutes.

Reports: the same speaker moving to the next thing. It is the spoken counterpart of continue and slightly warmer.

"That's the first problem. The second is money," he said. → He named the first problem and went on to say that the second was money.

  1. He apologized, then went on to explain what had happened.
  2. "Anyway," she went on, "we found the file."
  3. The article goes on to argue that the policy backfired.
  4. She went on talking as if nothing had happened.
  5. Don't get him started — he'll go on about it all night.
  6. Go on, I'm listening.

See also: continue, add, say

if you ask me#

Pronunciation: /ɪf jə ˈæsk mi/ · if yuh ASK mee Type: fixed phrase — opinion marker, not a reporting verb · Register: informal, spoken Patterns: If you ask me, + clause · clause + , if you ask me · parenthetical inside the clause (The whole thing was, if you ask me, a waste) Never: if you ask to me / if you ask me that — the phrase is frozen. It takes no preposition, no complement, and no tense change; if you asked me is a real conditional and a different sentence. It is not a conditional and it reports nothing: nobody has asked. The phrase flags the clause attached to it as the speaker's own unsolicited opinion, usually one that runs against what everyone else is saying, and it carries a faint note of grievance. Reports: nothing anyone else said — it marks the attached clause as the speaker's own opinion, offered without being asked. Where say attributes words to someone, this phrase claims them. It does not survive being reported. This is the point worth learning: the phrase belongs to whoever is speaking now, so reporting it forces a paraphrase.

"It's too expensive, if you ask me," she said. → She said that in her opinion it was too expensive. / She said that as far as she was concerned, it was too expensive.

  1. If you ask me, the second album was better.
  2. They repainted the whole thing, which was a waste of money if you ask me.
  3. If you ask me, nobody read the contract before signing it.
  4. He was, if you ask me, never really interested in the job.
  5. That's not a bug, if you ask me — that's a design decision.
  6. If you ask me, they should have canceled the game.

See also: let me tell you, put it, say

indicate#

Pronunciation: /ˈɪndəˌkeɪt/ · IN-duh-kayt Type: reporting verb — an indirect or evidential statement · Register: formal — academic, journalistic, corporate Patterns: indicate + that-clause · indicate + wh-clause · indicate + noun phrase · indicate + to + sb + that-clause Never: indicate me that — the listener attaches with to: indicated to us that… And indicated to step down — indicate takes no infinitive complement; the plan goes in a that-clause: indicated that he would step down. Two jobs at once: with a human subject it reports a statement made indirectly or non-verbally — She indicated that she was willing to settle means she signaled it, not that she announced it. With a non-human subject it reports evidence: The data indicate that costs are rising. The second use is the standard hedge of academic English, and it is deliberately weaker than show or prove. Number note: in formal American writing data takes a plural verb — the data indicate. In ordinary usage the data indicates is now common and unremarkable. Reports: a message delivered obliquely, or evidence pointing toward a conclusion. It is deliberately weaker than state and announce: indicate leaves room for the speaker to have meant something else.

"I might be open to an offer," she said. → She indicated that she might be open to an offer.

  1. The survey indicates that most respondents never opened the email.
  2. He indicated to the committee that he would step down in the spring.
  3. Her silence indicated what she thought of the proposal.
  4. Preliminary results indicate a much smaller effect than expected.
  5. Nothing in the file indicated that the payment had been made.
  6. Please indicate whether you plan to attend.

See also: convey, suggest, report, note

inform#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈfɔrm/ · in-FORM Type: reporting verb — statement delivered to a named listener · Register: formal Patterns: inform + sb · inform + sb + that-clause · inform + sb + of / about + noun phrase · inform + sb + wh-clause · passive be informed that / be informed of Never: inform that the meeting was canceled — like tell, this verb requires its person. Also inform me the change — no double object; the message attaches with of: inform me of the change. And never inform to me. of vs. about: of is the formal default and takes a fact (informed us of the delay); about is looser and takes a topic (informed us about the new procedure). Neither one can be dropped. Reports: the formal delivery of information to someone who is entitled to it. It is tell with a suit on, and it carries a faint institutional chill — I regret to inform you is a phrase people brace for.

"Your application was not successful," the email said. → The email informed me that my application had not been successful.

  1. Nobody informed the neighbors that the water would be shut off.
  2. Please inform your supervisor of any schedule change.
  3. We regret to inform you that the position has been filled.
  4. She was never properly informed about her options.
  5. He informed the officer where he had been that evening.
  6. Keep me informed.

See also: notify, advise, tell, announce

let me tell you#

Pronunciation: /lɛt mi ˈtɛl ju/ · let mee TELL yoo — usually reduced to LEM-ee tell yuh Type: fixed phrase — emphasis marker · Register: informal, spoken Patterns: Let me tell you, + clause · clause + , let me tell you · let me tell you + something · let me tell you + about + noun phrase · let me tell you + wh-clause Never: let me to tell you — let takes a bare infinitive, never to. Also let me tell to you — tell takes its person bare, which is the same rule as in the tell entry. It is not a request for permission. Nobody is being asked. The phrase is a pointer that says what comes next matters, and with something attached it usually introduces a complaint or a warning: Let me tell you something — that's not how this works. Reports: nothing that was previously said — it announces the speaker's own next statement and marks it as worth hearing. Compare tell, which reports a message that actually reached a listener. It does not survive being reported, for the same reason if you ask me doesn't: the emphasis belongs to the person speaking now. Reporting it means finding a different verb.

"Let me tell you, that was a long drive," he said. → He said emphatically that it had been a long drive. / He insisted it had been a long drive.

  1. Let me tell you, that was the coldest I have ever been.
  2. Let me tell you something: nobody reads the second page.
  3. It wasn't cheap, let me tell you.
  4. Let me tell you about the time our flight got canceled in Denver.
  5. And let me tell you why that matters.
  6. Let me tell you, I have never been so glad to see a rest stop.

See also: if you ask me, tell, put it

mention#

Pronunciation: /ˈmɛnʃən/ · MEN-shuhn Type: reporting verb — a passing statement · Register: neutral Patterns: mention + that-clause · mention + noun phrase · mention + sb (as a topic, never as a listener) · mention + wh-clause · mention + -ing · mention + noun phrase + to + sb · mention + to + sb + that-clause · impersonal it is worth mentioning that Never: mention about the meeting — mention takes its object directly, with no preposition: mentioned the meeting. This is a stubborn error, probably by analogy with talk about and tell someone about. Also mention me that — repair with mention it to me, or mentioned to me that… Note the trap in the bare-object frame: He mentioned you means you were the subject of the remark, not the person who heard it. Reports: something brought up briefly and without emphasis. The word itself is a claim about weight: He mentioned that he was leaving says it came up in passing, and if he actually made a speech about it, mention is the wrong verb. That is also why it works as an apology in advance — I should mention that I'll be late softens the news by pretending it is minor.

"Oh, and I'm out on Friday," she said. → She mentioned that she would be out on Friday.

  1. He mentioned that his sister lives about an hour from here.
  2. Did anyone mention the parking situation?
  3. She mentioned seeing him at the conference last spring.
  4. I don't think he ever mentioned what he did before this.
  5. Don't mention it to Dave — he'll worry.
  6. It's worth mentioning that the warranty expired in March.

See also: note, remark, add, say

note#

Pronunciation: /noʊt/ · NOHT Type: reporting verb — a statement of a fact taken as given · Register: formal, mostly written Patterns: note + that-clause · note + noun phrase · note + wh-clause · note + direct quotation (as a tag) · passive it should be noted that / it is worth noting that Never: note me that — no bare personal object. The to-form (noted to reporters that…) does turn up in journalism, but it is far rarer than with remark or comment; in most writing note assumes a reader rather than addressing one. It presupposes truth. This is the one place note is not neutral: He noted that attendance was down takes it as established that attendance was down, and reports him as calling attention to a fact. Compare He claimed that attendance was down, which does the opposite. If the point is contested, note quietly takes a side. Reports: the act of registering something for the record — observing it, and expecting the reader to accept it.

"Attendance is down about nine percent," the report said. → The report noted that attendance was down about nine percent.

  1. The judge noted that neither party had filed on time.
  2. It should be noted that these figures exclude part-time employees.
  3. She noted the discrepancy in the margin and moved on.
  4. Critics noted how little had changed since the first audit.
  5. Please note that the building has no visitor parking.
  6. He noted, correctly, that we had no budget for any of this.

See also: observe, remark, mention, indicate

notify#

Pronunciation: /ˈnoʊtəˌfaɪ/ · NOH-tuh-fye Type: reporting verb — formal notification · Register: formal — administrative, legal Patterns: notify + sb · notify + sb + that-clause · notify + sb + of + noun phrase · notify + sb + of + wh-clause · passive be notified that / be notified of Never: notify that the class was canceled — the person is required. And notify us the date — no double object; repair with notify us of the date. Also notify to me: like tell, this verb takes its person bare. One more line separates it from inform — notify will not take a bare wh-clause: notify us what you decide → notify us of what you decide, where inform us what you decide is perfectly good. (A when-clause after notify is a time adverbial, not the message: notifies you when a payment fails says when the notice arrives, not what it says.) Reports: an official act of informing, usually one that some rule or contract requires. It differs from inform in that notify often stands alone with no message at all — Notify the family is a complete instruction, while Inform the family invites the question "of what?"

"You'll hear from us within ten days," they said. → They said we would be notified within ten days.

  1. The school notified parents that the trip had been canceled.
  2. Tenants must notify the landlord of any change of address.
  3. We were never notified.
  4. Notify the on-call engineer immediately.
  5. She was notified by certified mail on the 12th.
  6. The system notifies you when a payment fails.

See also: inform, advise, announce, tell

observe#

Pronunciation: /əbˈzɚv/ · uhb-ZURV Type: reporting verb — a considered remark · Register: formal, literary Patterns: observe + that-clause · observe + direct quotation · observe + noun phrase · observe + wh-clause · observe + to + sb + that-clause (literary) · observe + sb + -ing / bare infinitive (the watch sense) · passive it has often been observed that Never: observe me that — no bare personal object in the speech sense. The literary to-form is available — observed to her sister that… — but the bare object never is. Two verbs, and the frames tell them apart: with a that-clause or a quotation it means "said, having noticed" — He observed that the door was open. With a person plus -ing it means "watched" — He observed her leaving. There is no frame where the two readings compete, which is why the ambiguity almost never causes trouble in practice. Reports: a remark presented as the product of noticing something. Drier and more deliberate than remark, and unlike note it is a spoken act, not a written one.

"Nobody here has read the contract," he said. → He observed that nobody there had read the contract.

  1. "You're up early," she observed.
  2. He observed that the two accounts contradicted each other on every important point.
  3. One reviewer observed how carefully the film avoids explaining itself.
  4. She observed the whole exchange from across the room.
  5. Visitors may observe the class but may not participate.
  6. It has often been observed that committees expand to fill the time available.

See also: remark, note, comment, say

put it#

Pronunciation: /ˈpʊt ɪt/ · PUUT it Type: reporting verb — attribution of wording · Register: neutral Patterns: as + sb + put(s) it · to put it + adverb (bluntly, mildly, simply, another way) · put it + this way / like this / another way · put it + that-clause (formal and rare) · put it + to + sb + that-clause (chiefly British, in questioning) Never: as she put — the it is not optional and never changes: not as she put them, not as she put the matter. The whole expression is frozen around that one pronoun. Reports: the wording, not the fact. As he put it, the project was "resting" attributes the phrasing to him and quietly declines to endorse it — the same move as scare quotes, done with a verb. That is why as he put it so often precedes a euphemism.

"We're pausing the rollout," she said. → The rollout was, as she put it, "paused."

  1. The building is, as the listing puts it, "full of character."
  2. To put it mildly, the presentation did not go well.
  3. Let me put it another way: there is no money.
  4. He was, as his coach put it, "not a natural."
  5. To put it bluntly, nobody wants the job.
  6. She put it better than I could have.

See also: quote, describe, say, if you ask me

quote#

Pronunciation: /kwoʊt/ · KWOHT Type: reporting verb — verbatim attribution · Register: neutral, journalistic Patterns: quote + sb · quote + noun phrase · quote + direct quotation · quote + from + noun phrase · quote + sb + as saying / as calling / as describing · quote + sb + on + topic · passive be quoted as saying that… Never: quote that the deal was dead — quote takes no that-clause on its own, and no personal object plus a clause either: quoted me that the deal was dead. Repairs: quoted him as saying that the deal was dead, quoted him: "The deal is dead." It is the one verb here that claims exactness. Every other verb in this file reports content; quote reports words. Getting them wrong is not a style problem — misquoting someone is a factual error and, in journalism, a correctable one. Use say or state when you are paraphrasing. The passive is the workhorse: was quoted as saying is the standard newspaper frame for a statement the reporter did not personally hear. Reports: someone's exact words, reproduced. The judgment it carries is a promise of accuracy rather than an opinion about the content — quote says nothing about whether the speaker was right, only that this is what came out of their mouth.

"This deal is dead," he told reporters. → He was quoted as saying the deal was dead.

  1. The article quotes two former employees who describe the same pattern.
  2. She was quoted as calling the decision "indefensible."
  3. Don't quote me on the exact number.
  4. He quoted his grandmother at least once an hour.
  5. The study is quoted far more often than it is read.
  6. Can I quote you on that?

See also: put it, report, state, say

recount#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈkaʊnt/ · rih-KOWNT Type: reporting verb — extended narrative · Register: formal, literary Patterns: recount + noun phrase · recount + how-clause · recount + wh-clause · recount + noun phrase + to + sb · recount + to + sb + how-clause Never: recount me the story — the listener attaches with to: recounted the story to me. And recount + a plain that-clause (He recounted that he had been fired) is stiff and rare; native writers use a how-clause or a noun phrase instead: recounted how he had been fired. Stress matters: /rɪˈkaʊnt/ with second-syllable stress is this verb. /ˈriːkaʊnt/ with first-syllable stress is the election noun — a recount — and the verb meaning "count again." Reports: the telling of a sequence of events at length and in order. Its object is an episode, not a proposition, which is exactly why the that-clause fits so badly.

"So I got there, and the office was already locked, and…" she said. → She recounted how she had arrived to find the office already locked.

  1. He recounted the entire trip, hour by hour.
  2. She recounted how the two of them had met.
  3. The memoir recounts his years in Chicago.
  4. Survivors recounted what they had seen to a federal panel.
  5. I've heard him recount that story a dozen times.
  6. The chapter recounts the strike from the union's side.

See also: relate, describe, report, tell

relate#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈleɪt/ · rih-LAYT Type: reporting verb — orderly telling · Register: formal, literary Patterns: relate + noun phrase · relate + that-clause · relate + how / wh-clause · relate + noun phrase + to + sb Never: relate me the story — no double object; repair with relate the story to me. Three unrelated verbs share this spelling, and only the first is a reporting verb: relate a story (tell), relate to something (be connected with — questions relating to the budget), and relate to someone (identify with — I can relate). The third is informal American English and now far more common in speech than the reporting sense, which is why He related the incident sounds distinctly bookish today. Reports: a full, orderly account given to someone. Slightly more formal than recount and slightly less committed to length.

"It started when the alarm didn't go off," he said. → He related how the whole day had started with an alarm that didn't go off.

  1. She related the events of that morning to the investigators.
  2. He related that the two companies had been in talks for a year.
  3. The book relates the founding of the town in some detail.
  4. Strange as it may seem, he related the whole thing without once raising his voice.
  5. The witness related what she had heard through the wall.
  6. Ask him to relate the story about the ferry.

See also: recount, describe, report, tell

remark#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈmɑrk/ · rih-MARK Type: reporting verb — a brief spoken observation · Register: neutral, tilting literary Patterns: remark + that-clause · remark + direct quotation · remark + on / upon + noun phrase · remark + on + wh-clause · remark + to + sb + that-clause Never: he remarked the change — the topic takes on: remarked on the change. (The bare-object use survives only in the archaic sense "notice," as in a change no one remarked.) Also remark me that. Reports: a short, unweighted comment made out loud, often in passing. Compared with its neighbors: observe is more deliberate, comment is more evaluative, note is written and presupposes truth, and remark is the plainest of the four — it reports that something was said aloud and stops there.

"It's colder than it was last week," she said. → She remarked that it was colder than it had been the week before.

  1. "You've been quiet," he remarked.
  2. She remarked that the parking lot was already half empty at four.
  3. Several people remarked on how much the neighborhood had changed.
  4. He remarked to a friend that he'd never seen the boss so cheerful.
  5. Somebody remarked that the coffee tasted like the pot it came in.
  6. It's rude to remark on what other people are eating.

See also: comment, observe, note, mention

repeat#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈpit/ · rih-PEET Type: reporting verb — restatement · Register: neutral Patterns: repeat + that-clause · repeat + noun phrase · repeat + direct quotation · repeat + wh-clause · repeat + noun phrase + to / for + sb · repeat + oneself Never: repeat me the question — no double object. Repairs: repeat the question for me, Could you repeat that? The bare Repeat that, please is the ordinary spoken form. Redundancy watch: repeat again is a double, since repeat already contains the "again." It is common in speech and universally cut in editing. Say repeat or say said it again, not both. Reports: a statement made a second time, which carries a small implication of insistence or of not having been heard the first time — She repeated that she wasn't going is a stronger sentence than She said again that she wasn't going.

"I'm not signing it. I am not signing it," he said. → He repeated that he was not going to sign it.

  1. She repeated that the deadline had not moved.
  2. Could you repeat the last part?
  3. He repeated the address back to the dispatcher.
  4. Don't repeat this to anyone.
  5. The recording repeats the same three instructions in a loop.
  6. I'll repeat it for the people in the back.

See also: continue, state, say, tell

report#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈpɔrt/ · rih-PORT Type: reporting verb — an account given to someone entitled to it · Register: neutral, journalistic and institutional Patterns: report + that-clause · report + noun phrase · report + -ing · report + wh-clause · report + to + sb + that-clause · report + sb + as + -ing / adjective · report + on + noun phrase · report + sb + to + sb (denunciation — see below) · passive it was reported that / sb is reported to have Never: report me that — the listener attaches with to: reported to the board that… And report about the findings — the topic takes on: reported on the findings. Watch the other senses. Report someone to someone is a denunciation, not a report of speech — She reported him to HR has nothing to do with what he said. And report to plus a person also means "have as a supervisor": He reports to the CFO. Reports: the passing along of information, and it is scrupulously neutral about truth — the reporter neither endorses the content nor doubts it. That neutrality is the reason it dominates journalism, where claimed would editorialize and revealed would concede the point.

"Sales fell about four percent last quarter," the company said. → The company reported that sales had fallen about four percent the previous quarter.

  1. Two papers reported that the talks had collapsed.
  2. She reported the outage to the utility just after midnight.
  3. Residents reported hearing a loud bang around two in the morning.
  4. He is reported to have left the country in June.
  5. The committee reported on its findings in November.
  6. Nobody ever reported what happened to the missing equipment.

See also: state, announce, say, quote, indicate

say#

Pronunciation: /seɪ/ · SAY — past and participle said /sɛd/ · SED, third person says /sɛz/ · SEZ Type: reporting verb — statement (the default) · Register: neutral Patterns: say + that-clause · say + direct quotation · say + noun phrase (short fixed list) · say + to + sb (+ that-clause) · say + wh-clause (mostly under negation or a modal: I couldn't say what he meant) · say + to-infinitive with no object (spoken American English only — see the footnote below) Never: say me / say someone something / say me that you're sorry — say takes no personal object, ever, in any register. Repairs: say to me, or switch verbs to tell me. This and its mirror image under tell are the two errors an American reader notices instantly. Pronunciation trap: said rhymes with bed, not with paid, and says rhymes with fez, not with days. The vowel changes even though the spelling doesn't. say to vs. tell: say to someone is perfectly grammatical, but when a listener is named, tell is far more idiomatic. Reserve say to for when the listener is contrasted or stressed: He didn't say a word to me. The fixed noun objects: say hello, say goodbye, say yes, say no, say thank you, say grace, say a few words, say nothing, say so, needless to say, say the word, that is to say. None of them needs quotation marks — She said hello is complete as it stands. The honest footnote: objectless say + to-infinitive — He said to wait. She said not to touch it. — is extremely common in spoken American English and is what most Americans would actually produce. Edited prose still prefers He told us to wait. What is never possible in any register is the personal object: He said me to wait. See Reported commands and requests. Reports: that words were produced. It is the unmarked, weightless default — the verb you use when you have no reason to use any other, and the one that adds nothing at all to the message.

"I'm not coming," she said. → She said (that) she wasn't coming. "I'll call you tomorrow," she said. → She said she would call me the next day.

  1. He said he'd be here by six.
  2. "That's not what I meant," she said.
  3. She didn't say a word to anyone all afternoon.
  4. What did the doctor say?
  5. The sign says the pool closes at nine.
  6. I couldn't say why he changed his mind.
  7. Just say the word and we'll leave.

See also: tell, state, mention, remark, report, The basics: say, tell, ask, speak, talk

share#

Pronunciation: /ʃɛr/ · SHAIR Type: reporting verb — statement offered to a group · Register: neutral, with a workplace and therapeutic flavor Patterns: share + that-clause · share + noun phrase · share + noun phrase + with + sb · share + with + sb + that-clause · share + wh-clause · share (intransitive — Would anyone like to share?) Never: share me the news — no double object. And share the news to me — this verb takes with, not to: share the news with me. The to version is spreading in some varieties of English and in social-media usage (share it to your story), but American edited prose still requires with for a listener. Register warning, said plainly: share that has become a soft corporate and therapeutic substitute for tell and say — I wanted to share that we're restructuring. It is completely grammatical, and plenty of readers find it evasive, because it dresses an announcement up as a gift. In neutral prose, tell and say are usually better. Reports: the offering of information to a person or group, framed as generous or voluntary.

"I've decided to take the job," she told the group. → She shared with the group that she had decided to take the job.

  1. He shared the news with his family before it went public.
  2. She shared that she'd been out of work for most of the year.
  3. Would anyone like to share before we wrap up?
  4. I'll share the document with you this afternoon.
  5. Nobody shared what had been discussed in the closed session.
  6. Thanks for sharing that with us.

state#

Pronunciation: /steɪt/ · STAYT Type: reporting verb — a deliberate, on-the-record statement · Register: formal Patterns: state + that-clause · state + noun phrase · state + wh-clause · state + direct quotation · state + to + sb + that-clause · passive it is stated that Never: state me that — no personal object; use state to the court that… Also state about the delay — state takes its object directly or takes a clause, never about. Reports: a statement made explicitly and for the record — heavier and more deliberate than say, and neutral about truth in a way claim is not. If someone writes He stated that he was home all evening, they are reporting a formal assertion without commenting on it; He claimed that he was home all evening invites you to doubt it. The weakness of overuse: in student writing state is often substituted for say to sound serious, and it backfires. My mother stated that dinner was ready is comic. Save it for occasions that genuinely have a record.

"I was at home all evening," he said. → He stated that he had been at home all evening.

  1. The contract states that either party may cancel with thirty days' notice.
  2. She stated her objection for the record.
  3. Please state your name and occupation.
  4. The memo stated clearly that no exceptions would be made.
  5. He stated to investigators that he had never seen the documents.
  6. It is stated nowhere in the policy that overtime must be pre-approved.

tell#

Pronunciation: /tɛl/ · TELL — past and participle told /toʊld/ · TOHLD Type: reporting verb — statement, command, or information delivered to a named listener · Register: neutral Patterns: tell + sb + that-clause · tell + sb + noun phrase (double object) · tell + sb + to-infinitive · tell + sb + wh-clause · tell + sb + about + noun phrase · tell + sb + of + noun phrase (formal) · tell + sb + direct quotation (as a tag) · tell + noun phrase (fixed objects only — see below) · passive be told that / be told to Never: tell that the road was closed — the person is required; repair with tell me that the road was closed. And tell to me — the person attaches bare, with no preposition. A direct quotation cannot rescue it either: "I'm late," he told. → "I'm late," he told me. The fixed objects — tell with no listener. A short, closed list where tell takes a non-person object and names nobody:

ExpressionExample
tell the truthJust tell the truth.
tell a lieHe's telling a lie and he knows it.
tell a story / a jokeShe told a story about her first job.
tell timeHe learned to tell time in kindergarten. (British: tell the time)
tell the differenceI can't tell the difference between the two files.
tell A from BCan you tell margarine from butter?

The other verb: in several of those, tell does not mean "say" at all — it means distinguish or know: I can't tell if it's working. You can tell she's nervous. That sense takes no listener and no message, and it never appears in reported speech. The passive is everywhere: I was told the office had moved. We were told to wait. Because English promotes the person object, the passive of tell is one of the commonest ways to report a statement whose source you can't or won't name. Reports: the delivery of a message to a specific person. Where say reports that words existed, tell reports that they reached someone — which is why it, and not say, carries commands: told me to sit down.

"The road is closed," he said to me. → He told me (that) the road was closed. "Sit down," she said. → She told me to sit down.

  1. She told me the meeting had moved to Thursday.
  2. Nobody told us where to park.
  3. He told the kids to stay off the ice.
  4. Tell me about the new place.
  5. I was told the check had already been mailed.
  6. You can tell she's done this before.
  7. To tell you the truth, I'd rather stay home.

<a id="see-also-note"></a>A note on cross-references#

Two verbs turn up in the See also lines above without entries of their own in this file, because they are not neutral. Express (expressed regret, expressed doubt) reports a feeling rather than a proposition, which puts it with the verbs of emotion and evaluation rather than here. Point out presupposes that what was said is true, which is a judgment — He pointed out that the form was late concedes that the form was late, while He said the form was late does not; it is catalogued at point out.

The same test sorts the whole dictionary. Ask what the verb commits you, the reporter, to. Say, state, report, and mention commit you to nothing but the fact that words were produced. Note and point out commit you to the truth of the content. Claim and allege commit you to doubting it. Admit and confess commit you to the premise that there was a fault to own. Only the first group belongs in this file.
