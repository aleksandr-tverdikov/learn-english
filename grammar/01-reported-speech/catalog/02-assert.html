Asserting, claiming, and insisting#

The verbs for putting a statement on the record — and for telling your reader, in the choice of verb alone, whether you believe it.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

Every verb in this file reports a statement: an act of putting something forward as true, and nearly all of them take a that-clause. What separates one from the next is not grammar but stance — confirm hands the reader a fact the writer accepts, say and state stay neutral, and claim, allege, purport, and profess quietly withhold the writer's endorsement. That is why a newspaper writes the suspect claimed he was home rather than said he was home: the verb is doing the work a disclaimer would otherwise have to do, and doing it in one word. The grammatical trap that runs through the whole group is the object slot — most of these verbs refuse a personal object outright (✗ She confirmed me that the meeting was on, ✗ He announced us the news), while assure refuses to go without one (✗ He assured that the parts would arrive), and the two verbs that straddle demanding and asserting, insist and stipulate, change the mood of their own that-clause depending on which act they name.

The doubt scale#

The single most useful thing in this file. Same content, seven verbs, seven different messages to the reader about the writer's own position:

VerbWhat it signals about the writer's stanceExample
confirm, point out, noteThe writer accepts it as true — factiveShe confirmed that the flight was canceled.
say, state, announce, declareNeutral. The writer takes no position at allShe said the flight was canceled.
assert, proclaimNeutral content, forceful manner — in news copy, a faint raised eyebrowShe asserted that the flight was canceled.
maintain, insist, contendSomeone disputes this, and the speaker is holding the lineShe insisted that the flight was canceled.
claim, professThe writer is not vouching for itShe claimed the flight was canceled.
allegeUnproven and accusatory — someone is charged with wrongdoingShe alleged that the airline had lied.
purportProbably not what it appears to be; usually of documents and objectsA memo purporting to show the cancellation.

Nothing on that scale is a hedge you can drop. Swapping said for claimed in a news story changes what the story asserts, and swapping claimed for confirmed can make the paper liable for the statement. Journalists and lawyers pick from this column deliberately; a learner who treats them as stylistic variants of say will write sentences that mean the opposite of what was intended.

affirm#

Pronunciation
/əˈfɜrm/ · uh-FURM
Русский
утверждать, подтверждать — официально повторять свою позицию; юр. заявлять без присяги
Type
reporting verb — statement, formally and deliberately restated
Register
formal
Patterns
affirm + that-clause · affirm + noun phrase · affirm + to sb + that-clause · affirm (intransitive, in court: he affirmed rather than swore)
Never
affirm someone that and affirm to do something. There is no personal object and no infinitive complement: ✗ He affirmed us that the funding was secure, ✗ She affirmed to support the measure. The addressee, if you need one, takes to. Repairs: affirmed that the funding was secure, affirmed to the committee that the funding was secure, affirmed her support for the measure, or — if the point is settling someone's doubt rather than stating for the record — assured us that the funding was secure.
Reports
a public, deliberate statement that something is true, usually restating a position already on record. Unlike claim, it carries no doubt; unlike confirm, it does not require a prior question. Two specialized senses live alongside it: to affirm in court is to make a solemn declaration in place of an oath (He affirmed rather than swore), and an appellate court affirms a lower court's ruling by letting it stand.

"We remain committed to the treaty," the ambassador said. → The ambassador affirmed that they remained committed to the treaty. "Yes — the policy has not changed." → She affirmed that the policy had not changed.

  1. The board affirmed that no layoffs were planned for the coming year.
  2. She quietly affirmed her belief that the study would hold up.
  3. The witness chose to affirm rather than swear on a Bible.
  4. The appeals court affirmed the conviction in a two-page order.
  5. The ambassador affirmed to reporters that the treaty remained in force.
  6. In his letter he affirmed that the money had been repaid in full.
  7. Nobody at the hearing affirmed that the deadline was still in force.
See also
reaffirm, confirm, assert, avow, swear

allege#

Pronunciation
/əˈlɛdʒ/ · uh-LEJ
Русский
утверждать без доказательств — юр./газетн. обвинять, не ручаясь за правдивость
Type
reporting verb — accusation asserted but unproven
Register
formal; journalistic and legal
Patterns
allege + that-clause · allege + noun phrase · be alleged + to-infinitive (passive) · allege + sb + to be (rare, legal)
Never
allege to do something in the active voice — ✗ He alleged to have seen the car. The infinitive frame exists only in the passive: ✓ He is alleged to have seen the car. Also ✗ allege someone something and ✗ allege sb that: ✗ She alleged the police that he had lied → ✓ She alleged that he had lied.
Reports
the strongest doubt marker in English, and the only one that also carries an accusation. Allege tells the reader two things at once: the statement is unproven, and it charges someone with wrongdoing. This is exactly why journalists reach for it — writing the company allegedly falsified the invoices reports the accusation without the newspaper adopting it. Use it for wrongdoing only; she alleged that the train was late is comic, because nothing is at stake.

"Their auditor destroyed the files," the plaintiff said. → The plaintiff alleged that their auditor had destroyed the files. "He took money from the account." → He is alleged to have taken money from the account.

  1. Prosecutors allege that the payments were routed through three shell companies.
  2. The suit alleges a pattern of retaliation going back four years.
  3. She is alleged to have signed the forms without reading them.
  4. None of the alleged victims has been named publicly.
  5. The article carefully alleged nothing it could not document.
  6. He allegedly left the building twenty minutes before the alarm sounded.
See also
claim, purport, assert, contend, testify

announce#

Pronunciation
/əˈnaʊns/ · uh-NOWNSS
Русский
объявлять, сообщать — публично оглашать новость, обычно впервые
Type
reporting verb — public statement of news
Register
neutral
Patterns
announce + that-clause · announce + noun phrase · announce + wh-clause · announce + to sb + that-clause · announce + noun phrase + to sb
Never
announce someone something and announce someone that. Announce is not ditransitive; the addressee needs to: ✗ She announced us the results → ✓ She announced the results to us / announced to us that the results were in. Also ✗ announce to do.
Reports
making something publicly known, usually for the first time. It presupposes both that the content is true and that it is news — which is why he announced that he was tired sounds like a joke. Compared with declare it is plainer and less ceremonial; compared with proclaim it lacks any grandeur.

"We're moving the office to Denver," she told the staff. → She announced to the staff that they were moving the office to Denver. "The winner is Maria Okonkwo." → He announced that the winner was Maria Okonkwo.

  1. The mayor announced that the pool would reopen in June.
  2. They announced the merger an hour before the market opened.
  3. She walked in and announced to nobody in particular that the elevator was broken.
  4. The airline has not yet announced which routes it will cut.
  5. He announced his resignation in a two-sentence email.
  6. Nothing was announced about the second round of funding.
See also
declare, proclaim, pronounce, confirm

argue#

Pronunciation
/ˈɑrɡju/ · AR-gyoo
Русский
доказывать, утверждать с доводами — отстаивать позицию, приводя основания
Type
reporting verb — a claim advanced with reasons
Register
neutral to formal
Patterns
argue + that-clause · argue + for/against + noun phrase / -ing · argue + noun phrase (argue a case, argue a point) · argue + sb + into/out of + -ing · argue + about/over + noun phrase / wh-clause (quarrel sense) · argue + with sb (+ about sth)
Never
argue someone that and argue to do something — ✗ He argued me that the plan was risky, ✗ She argued to raise the fee. Repairs: argued that the plan was risky, argued for raising the fee. And note that the persuasion frame requires a preposition: ✗ He argued me to stay → ✓ He argued me into staying.
Reports
asserting a position together with the reasons for it. The verb commits the speaker to a supporting case, so it presupposes that the point is contestable — which is why it is the default verb of academic writing (Ellison argues that…) but sounds combative in a report of casual conversation. It does not signal doubt the way claim does; it signals contested ground.

"The data don't support that conclusion, and here's why," she said. → She argued that the data did not support the conclusion. "We should hire two, not one." → He argued for hiring two rather than one.

  1. She argues that the second experiment was never properly controlled.
  2. The defense argued the case for four days.
  3. He argued against expanding the program until the audit was finished.
  4. They argued her into taking the job by promising a relocation budget.
  5. The two of them argued about whether to sell the house.
  6. It could be argued that the whole policy was obsolete by the time it passed.
See also
contend, maintain, assert, submit, posit

assert#

Pronunciation
/əˈsɜrt/ · uh-SURT
Русский
заявлять, утверждать — категорично и уверенно, обычно без доказательств
Type
reporting verb — statement made forcefully and without hedging
Register
formal
Patterns
assert + that-clause · assert + noun phrase (assert a right, assert control) · assert + oneself
Never
assert someone that, assert someone something, assert to do something, and assert someone to do something. ✗ He asserted the committee that the figures were sound, ✗ She asserted to know the answer, ✗ They asserted him to have signed the form. Repairs: asserted that the figures were sound, claimed to know the answer, asserted that he had signed the form.
Reports
stating something as fact, flatly and with confidence, and usually without offering support. That last part is what gives assert its faint edge in journalism: by drawing attention to the confidence rather than the evidence, it invites the reader to notice that nothing was proved. It is a milder doubt marker than claim — closer to neutral — but it is not fully neutral, and he asserted in a news story is rarely a compliment.

"The vote was rigged. Period," he said. → He asserted that the vote had been rigged. "This land is ours." → They asserted their claim to the land.

  1. The report asserts that the software was never tested on live data.
  2. She asserted her right to have a lawyer present.
  3. He asserted, without producing a single document, that the deal had been approved.
  4. The company has asserted control over three of the four subsidiaries.
  5. In meetings she has learned to assert herself early.
  6. Nobody in the room asserted anything they could not back up.
See also
claim, contend, maintain, aver, declare

assure#

Pronunciation
/əˈʃʊr/ · uh-SHOOR
Русский
заверять, уверять кого-л. — обязательно с адресатом: заверяют всегда кого-то
Type
reporting verb — statement made to remove someone's doubt
Register
neutral
Patterns
assure + sb + that-clause · assure + sb + of + noun phrase · assure + sb (alone)
Never
assure that with no object. This is the single commonest error with the verb: ✗ He assured that the parts would arrive Friday. Assure is obligatorily ditransitive — somebody has to be reassured. Repairs: assured us that the parts would arrive, or drop the verb for a neutral one: said that the parts would arrive. Also ✗ assure to sb that, ✗ assure sb to do (that is promise or urge).
Reports
telling someone directly that something is true, in order to settle their doubt. Where affirm is oriented toward the record, assure is oriented toward the addressee — the point is the listener's peace of mind, not the public statement. It carries no doubt of its own, but the writer's he assured us often invites the reader to notice that the assurance may not have held.

"Don't worry — the check went out Monday," she said. → She assured me that the check had gone out that Monday. "The building is completely safe." → He assured the tenants that the building was completely safe.

  1. The nurse assured him that the swelling would go down in a day or two.
  2. She assured us of her support before the vote was even scheduled.
  3. I can assure you that nobody here saw the email.
  4. He assured the landlord the rent would clear by Friday.
  5. Rest assured — the file was backed up before the crash.
  6. The contractor assured them repeatedly, and then missed the deadline anyway.
WordMeansExample
assureremove someone's doubt (needs a person as object)She assured me the room was booked.
ensuremake certain that something happensShe ensured the room was booked.
insurecover with an insurance policyShe insured the room against damage.
See also
affirm, confirm, swear, promise

attest#

Pronunciation
/əˈtɛst/ · uh-TEST
Русский
свидетельствовать, удостоверять — подтверждать по личному знанию; юр. заверять
Type
reporting verb — formal statement from firsthand knowledge
Register
formal; legal and ceremonial
Patterns
attest + that-clause · attest + to + noun phrase / -ing / wh-clause · attest + noun phrase (attest a signature, attest a will)
Never
attest to + a that-clause. The preposition takes a noun phrase, an -ing form, or a wh-clause — but never a bare that-clause: ✗ She attested to that the copy was genuine. Repairs: attested that the copy was genuine, or attested to the authenticity of the copy, or the idiom attested to the fact that the copy was genuine. Also ✗ attest someone that and ✗ attest to do.
Reports
bearing witness — asserting something on the strength of one's own knowledge, usually in a setting where the statement counts for something. It presupposes the speaker's credibility rather than the truth of the content, which is why an inanimate subject works so naturally: the wear on the steps attests to a century of use.

"I saw him sign it," she said. → She attested that she had seen him sign it. "The translation is accurate." → The translator attested to the accuracy of the translation.

  1. Two colleagues attested that he had been in the building all morning.
  2. Anyone who has waited in that line can attest to how slow it is.
  3. A notary must attest the signature before the deed is filed.
  4. The scorch marks on the ceiling attest to an earlier fire.
  5. She attested under penalty of perjury that the return was complete.
  6. Nothing in the file attests to a second inspection.
See also
testify, swear, affirm, confirm, aver

aver#

Pronunciation
/əˈvɜr/ · uh-VUR
Русский
книжн./юр. утверждать, заявлять — в обычной речи звучит устарело и вычурно
Type
reporting verb — statement made positively and on the record
Register
formal; legal, literary, rare in speech
Patterns
aver + that-clause · aver + noun phrase (legal: aver facts in a pleading)
Never
aver someone that and aver to do something — ✗ He averred the court that he had paid, ✗ She averred to have paid. Repairs: averred that he had paid, claimed to have paid.
Reports
stating something confidently and formally, and in legal pleading, alleging a fact one intends to prove at trial. Outside of court filings and self-consciously literary prose it reads as precious or archaic — he averred in ordinary American writing sounds like a nineteenth-century novel. Say said, insisted, or asserted instead unless the register genuinely calls for it.

"I never received the notice," he said. → He averred that he had never received the notice. "The defendant knew the account was overdrawn." → The complaint avers that the defendant knew the account was overdrawn.

  1. The complaint avers that the contract was signed under duress.
  2. He averred, with a straight face, that he had read every page.
  3. She averred her innocence in the same flat tone all afternoon.
  4. Counsel must aver the facts supporting jurisdiction in the opening paragraphs.
  5. In the memoir he avers that the letter was destroyed by accident.
  6. Nobody in the deposition averred anything about a second meeting.
See also
assert, allege, avow, maintain, submit

avow#

Pronunciation
/əˈvaʊ/ · uh-VOW
Русский
книжн. открыто признавать — заявлять о взглядах или верности, которые можно было бы скрыть
Type
reporting verb — open admission of a belief or allegiance
Register
formal, literary
Patterns
avow + that-clause · avow + noun phrase · avow + oneself + noun phrase / adjective (avowed himself satisfied)
Never
avow someone that and avow to do something — ✗ He avowed us that he had voted for it, ✗ She avowed to resign. Repairs: avowed that he had voted for it, vowed to resign (a different verb, and a different act).
Reports
declaring openly something one might reasonably be expected to conceal — a belief, a loyalty, a motive. It presupposes candor and a small cost to the speaker. The past participle is far commoner than the finite verb: an avowed socialist, her avowed aim. Its opposite, disavow, denies any connection: the campaign disavowed the flyer.

"Yes, I supported the strike, and I still do," she said. → She avowed that she had supported the strike and still did. "I am, and always have been, an anarchist." → He avowed himself an anarchist.

  1. She avowed that her only interest in the case was the principle.
  2. He publicly avowed his support for the boycott a week before the vote.
  3. An avowed skeptic, she read the whole report twice before commenting.
  4. The senator avowed himself satisfied with the compromise.
  5. The company disavowed the memo within hours of its leaking.
  6. Few of them would avow in writing what they said freely in the hallway.
See also
profess, declare, affirm, aver, proclaim

claim#

Pronunciation
/kleɪm/ · KLAYM
Русский
утверждать, заявлять — маркер сомнения: автор за сказанное не ручается («якобы»)
Type
reporting verb — statement the writer does not endorse
Register
neutral
Patterns
claim + that-clause · claim + to-infinitive (same subject) · claim + noun phrase · claim + sb/sth + to be (formal, rare)
Never
claim someone to do something and claim someone that. ✗ He claimed me to be lying, ✗ She claimed us that the package had shipped. Claim takes no indirect object and no object-controlled infinitive. Repairs: claimed that I was lying, told us that the package had shipped.
Control
claim + to-infinitive is same-subject only. He claimed to have paid means he paid. To report a claim about somebody else you need the that-clause: He claimed that his brother had paid — never ✗ He claimed his brother to have paid. The one apparent exception is the formal claim + object + to be frame in the Patterns line (the report claimed the signature to be a forgery): it is restricted to to be plus a complement, it is confined to legal and academic prose, and a learner is always safe rewriting it as a that-clause. No other infinitive is available after an object.
Reports
the everyday doubt marker of English. Writing he claimed he was home tells the reader, in one word, that the writer is not vouching for it. That is a real editorial act, not a stylistic flourish: said is neutral, pointed out endorses, claimed withholds. Unlike allege, it does not accuse anyone; unlike purport, it takes a human subject comfortably.

"I paid that invoice in March," he said. → He claimed that he had paid the invoice that March. "I speak four languages." → She claimed to speak four languages.

VerbThe reader is toldExample
saidnothing about the writer's viewHe said he was home.
pointed outthe writer accepts itHe pointed out that he was home.
claimedthe writer does not vouch for itHe claimed he was home.
allegedit is unproven and accusatoryHe alleged that she was home.
  1. He claims the fence was already down when he got there.
  2. She claimed to have no memory of the conversation.
  3. Two different groups have claimed responsibility for the outage.
  4. The label claims the bottle contains no added sugar.
  5. Nobody has claimed the bag left in the lobby.
  6. They claimed a deduction that the auditor immediately disallowed.

confirm#

Pronunciation
/kənˈfɜrm/ · kun-FURM
Русский
подтверждать — сообщать, что предполагавшееся верно; автор с этим согласен
Type
reporting verb — statement verifying something already in question
Register
neutral
Patterns
confirm + that-clause · confirm + noun phrase · confirm + wh-clause · confirm + to sb + that-clause · be confirmed + to-infinitive (passive)
Never
confirm someone that — ✗ She confirmed me that the meeting was on. The addressee needs to: ✓ She confirmed to me that the meeting was on, or simply She confirmed that the meeting was on. Also ✗ confirm to do.
Reports
stating that something already suggested, rumored, or asked about is in fact true. It is factive — He confirmed that the deal had collapsed commits the writer to the collapse, which is why a newspaper will not use it for a statement it cannot stand behind. It also presupposes a prior question; there is nothing to confirm if nobody wondered.

"Yes, the flight is canceled," the agent said. → The agent confirmed that the flight was canceled. "Has the check cleared?" — "It has." → She confirmed that the check had cleared.

  1. The lab confirmed that the sample was contaminated.
  2. Can you confirm whether the room is still available for Thursday?
  3. He confirmed the appointment by text an hour beforehand.
  4. Two sources have confirmed to the paper that the talks broke down.
  5. The species was later confirmed to be new to science.
  6. Nobody at the agency would confirm who had signed off on the waiver.
See also
affirm, reaffirm, attest, announce, claim

contend#

Pronunciation
/kənˈtɛnd/ · kun-TEND
Русский
утверждать, доказывать — отстаивать позицию в споре, где есть другая сторона
Type
reporting verb — a claim advanced inside a dispute
Register
formal
Patterns
contend + that-clause · contend + with + noun phrase (a different sense: cope with) · contend + for + noun phrase (a different sense: compete for)
Never
contend someone that, contend to do something, and — in the reporting sense — contend + noun phrase. ✗ He contended his innocence → ✓ He contended that he was innocent or asserted his innocence. ✗ The union contended the company to be at fault → ✓ contended that the company was at fault.
Reports
asserting a position in an argument that has another side. Contend presupposes an opponent, which is why it belongs to court reporting, academic disagreement, and news coverage of disputes. It carries a mild doubt tint — the writer is describing one party's position, not the facts — but it is more dignified than claim and does not accuse anyone.

"The clause never applied to seasonal staff," the union said. → The union contended that the clause had never applied to seasonal staff. "Their figures are three years out of date." → She contended that their figures were three years out of date.

  1. The defense contends that the search was unlawful from the start.
  2. Critics contend the model breaks down at low temperatures.
  3. He contended that no one had ever explained the fee to him.
  4. Both sides contend that the other walked away from the table.
  5. She has contended for years that the surveys undercount renters.
  6. It has been contended, though never demonstrated, that the two events are linked.

declare#

Pronunciation
/dɪˈklɛr/ · dih-KLAIR
Русский
объявлять, провозглашать — официально и авторитетно, нередко перформативно
Type
reporting verb — formal public statement made with authority
Register
formal
Patterns
declare + that-clause · declare + noun phrase · declare + sb/sth + (to be) + complement (declared the election invalid) · declare + oneself + adjective / noun phrase (declared herself satisfied, declared himself a candidate) · declare + to sb + that-clause · declare + for/against + noun phrase
Never
declare someone that and declare someone something — ✗ He declared us that the meeting was over, ✗ She declared me the results. Repairs: declared to us that the meeting was over, announced the results to me. Also ✗ declare to do.
Reports
stating something formally and with standing, often in a way that makes it so — I declare this meeting adjourned does not describe an adjournment, it performs one. On the doubt scale it is neutral as to truth but loud as to manner, and in ordinary narrative it lends a whiff of theater: she declared that she was finished with the whole thing.

"This election is void," the commission said. → The commission declared the election void. "I'm never doing that again," she said. → She declared that she was never doing that again.

  1. The governor declared a state of emergency before the storm made landfall.
  2. He declared that the numbers had been checked twice.
  3. The judge declared the contract unenforceable.
  4. She declared herself satisfied and went home.
  5. You have to declare anything over ten thousand dollars at customs.
  6. Two senators have declared against the bill in its current form.

emphasize#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛmfəsaɪz/ · EM-fuh-syze
Русский
подчёркивать, особо отмечать — придавать уже сказанному дополнительный вес
Type
reporting verb — statement given extra weight
Register
neutral
Patterns
emphasize + that-clause · emphasize + noun phrase · emphasize + wh-clause · emphasize + to sb + that-clause
Never
emphasize on — the commonest error with this verb, imported from the noun. The noun takes on (put emphasis on the deadline); the verb takes a plain direct object: ✗ He emphasized on the deadline → ✓ He emphasized the deadline. Also ✗ emphasize someone that (✗ She emphasized me that it was urgent → ✓ emphasized to me that it was urgent) and ✗ emphasize to do.
Spelling
American English writes -ize. Emphasise is British and has no place in American copy — see spelling the reporting verbs.
Reports
not a new claim but extra weight on part of one. It is factive: she emphasized that the deadline was firm commits the writer to the deadline being firm. Compared with insist it implies no resistance; the speaker is underlining, not defending.

"And this is the important part — the deposit is nonrefundable," she said. → She emphasized that the deposit was nonrefundable. "Remember, no exceptions." → He emphasized to the class that there would be no exceptions.

  1. She emphasized that the results were preliminary.
  2. The coach emphasized footwork all through the first week.
  3. He emphasized how little time was left before the filing date.
  4. The memo emphasized to managers that overtime had to be approved in advance.
  5. Every speaker emphasized the same three numbers.
  6. It cannot be emphasized enough that the backup runs only on weekdays.

hold#

Pronunciation
/hoʊld/ · HOHLD — irregular: hold, held, held
Русский
держаться мнения, считать; юр. постановлять — суд формулирует правовую позицию
Type
reporting verb — a settled belief, or a court's binding ruling
Register
formal
Patterns
hold + that-clause · hold + sb/sth + (to be) + complement (held the clause to be void) · hold + noun phrase (hold the view, hold an opinion) · it is held + that-clause (passive, very common)
Never
hold someone that and hold to do something — ✗ He held the jury that the statute applied, ✗ The court held to reverse the judgment. Repairs: held that the statute applied, voted to reverse the judgment.
Reports
maintaining a position one is settled in rather than arguing toward — a standing belief, a doctrine, or, in law, the part of a decision that binds later courts. Keep the legal pair straight: a court holds the law (the court held that the search was unlawful) and finds the facts (the court found that the officer had entered at 9 p.m.).

"In my view, the deadline was never binding," she said. → She held that the deadline had never been binding. "The clause is void." → The court held the clause to be void.

  1. Most economists hold that the effect is too small to measure.
  2. The Ninth Circuit held that the ordinance violated the First Amendment.
  3. She holds the view that the whole department should be restructured.
  4. It is widely held that the practice began in the 1930s.
  5. He held the study to be worthless and said so in print.
  6. Nobody now holds that the two species are closely related.
See also
maintain, contend, submit, pronounce, posit

insist#

Pronunciation
/ɪnˈsɪst/ · in-SIST
Русский
настаивать — упорно утверждать вопреки возражениям; либо требовать, не принимая отказа
Type
reporting verb — two acts under one word: a statement held against resistance, and a demand
Register
neutral
Patterns
insist + that-clause (indicative = claim; bare subjunctive = demand) · insist + on/upon + noun phrase · insist + on/upon + -ing · insist + on/upon + sb + -ing · insist (intransitive)
Never
insist someone to do something — ✗ She insisted me to stay. This is the classic error, and insist blocks every part of it: no personal object, no infinitive, no object-controlled complement. Repairs: insisted that I stay, insisted on my staying, insisted on me staying (both possessive and object forms are current; the possessive is the more formal).

this is the verb the mandative subjunctive was built for, and the mood you choose decides which speech act you are reporting. In the claim sense the clause is an ordinary tensed one and backshifts normally.

Two consequences worth memorizing. The negative is a bare not in front of the verb — no do, no agreement: ✓ insisted that she not be told, ✗ that she doesn't be told, ✗ that she not is told. testify + that-clause · testify + to + noun phrase / -ing · testify + about/against/for + noun phrase · testify + before + noun phrase (testified before the committee)
Never
testify someone that, testify to do something, and testify to + a that-clause. ✗ She testified the jury that the door was open, ✗ He testified to be present, ✗ He testified to that the door was open. Repairs: testified that the door was open, testified to the door's being open, testified that he had been present.
Reports
giving evidence on the record, under oath, in a court or a hearing. The verb carries no stance of its own — it neither endorses nor doubts, because its whole point is the forum: what matters is that the statement was made where perjury is a crime. That neutrality is exactly why reporters use it. An inanimate subject shifts the sense to attest: the wreckage testifies to the force of the impact.

"I was in the parking lot at the time," she said on the stand. → She testified that she had been in the parking lot at the time. "He handed me the envelope." → The witness testified that the defendant had handed him the envelope.

  1. Three former employees testified that the logs had been altered.
  2. She testified before the subcommittee for nearly five hours.
  3. He refused to testify against his own brother.
  4. The expert testified to the reliability of the test.
  5. She was subpoenaed but never actually testified.
  6. Nothing he testified to at trial matched his earlier statement to police.
See also
attest, swear, affirm, allege, maintain

underline#

Pronunciation
/ˌʌndərˈlaɪn/ · un-der-LYNE — the noun keeps the stress on the first syllable: /ˈʌndərlaɪn/, UN-der-lyne
Русский
подчёркивать — брит. вариант underscore; в амер. чаще о буквальном подчёркивании
Type
reporting verb — statement given extra weight
Register
neutral, but British-leaning; American writing prefers underscore
Patterns
underline + that-clause · underline + noun phrase · underline + wh-clause · underline + to sb + that-clause
Never
underline someone that and underline to do something — ✗ He underlined us that the funding was temporary, ✗ She underlined to check the totals. Repairs: underlined to us that the funding was temporary, underlined the need to check the totals.
Reports
drawing attention to something already said, exactly as emphasize does, by the metaphor of a pen under a line of text. Both underline and underscore are current in American English, but American editors reach for underscore by a wide margin in the figurative sense and keep underline for what you literally do with a pen. Like the rest of the emphasis group it is factive.

"And note that this is a one-time payment," she said. → She underlined that the payment was a one-time one. "The margins here are razor thin." → He underlined how thin the margins were.

  1. She underlined that the arrangement was temporary.
  2. The delay underlines how fragile the supply chain still is.
  3. He underlined the words no exceptions twice in red ink.
  4. Her resignation underlined the depth of the disagreement.
  5. The report underlines a problem the board has known about for years.
  6. Nothing in his answer underlined the urgency the memo had described.
See also
underscore, emphasize, stress, reiterate

underscore#

Pronunciation
/ˌʌndərˈskɔr/ · un-der-SKOR — the noun keeps the stress on the first syllable: /ˈʌndərskɔr/, UN-der-skor
Русский
подчёркивать, акцентировать — амер. норма в переносном смысле, часто с неодуш. подлежащим
Type
reporting verb — statement given extra weight
Register
neutral, journalistic; the standard American choice
Patterns
underscore + that-clause · underscore + noun phrase · underscore + wh-clause · underscore + to sb + that-clause
Never
underscore someone that and underscore to do something — ✗ She underscored the committee that time was short, ✗ He underscored to move quickly. Repairs: underscored to the committee that time was short, underscored the need to move quickly.
Reports
the American twin of underline, and the more idiomatic of the two in figurative use. It works with a speaker (she underscored that the vote was advisory) but is especially at home with an inanimate subject, where an event rather than a person does the emphasizing: the outage underscored how little redundancy the system had. Factive, like the rest of the group.

"This is exactly the kind of failure we warned about," she said. → She underscored that it was the kind of failure they had warned about. "The numbers are worse than last quarter." → He underscored that the numbers were worse than the previous quarter's.

  1. She underscored that the vote was advisory and nothing more.
  2. The outage underscored how little redundancy the network had.
  3. He underscored the importance of documenting every change.
  4. The two resignations underscore a problem the board has ignored.
  5. Her opening remarks underscored what the written report only implied.
  6. Nothing in the briefing underscored the cost, which is what everyone wanted to hear about.
See also
underline, emphasize, stress, reaffirm

vow#

Pronunciation
/vaʊ/ · VOW
Русский
клятвенно обещать, зарекаться — личное торжественное обещание; ср. avow «признавать»
Type
reporting verb — a solemn personal promise
Register
formal; a journalistic favorite
Patterns
vow + to-infinitive · vow + that-clause · vow + to sb + that-clause · vow + noun phrase (vowed revenge)
Never
vow someone to do something and vow someone that — ✗ He vowed us to return, ✗ She vowed me that she'd appeal. There is no indirect object: repairs are vowed to return, vowed to me that she would appeal, or promised me she would appeal.
Reports
a promise made with personal solemnity and, usually, some defiance. It is the headline writer's verb of choice because it is short and dramatic (Mayor vows to appeal), and it differs from pledge in being personal rather than institutional and from swear in requiring no oath. Do not confuse it with avow, which is an admission, not a promise.

"I will fight this to the last court there is," he said. → He vowed to fight it to the last court there was. "We'll be back next season." → They vowed that they would be back the following season.

  1. She vowed to appeal the ruling within the week.
  2. He vowed that no one would be evicted while he was in office.
  3. The company vowed revenge in the trade press and then quietly settled.
  4. They exchanged vows in front of about forty people.
  5. He vowed never to set foot in the building again.
  6. Nobody who vowed to read the whole report actually did.
See also
swear, pledge, avow, promise
