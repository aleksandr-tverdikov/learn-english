# Asserting, claiming, and insisting

> The verbs for putting a statement on the record — and for telling your reader, in the choice of verb alone, whether you believe it.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

Every verb in this file reports a **statement**: an act of putting something forward as true, and nearly all of them take a *that*-clause. What separates one from the next is not grammar but stance — *confirm* hands the reader a fact the writer accepts, *say* and *state* stay neutral, and *claim*, *allege*, *purport*, and *profess* quietly withhold the writer's endorsement. That is why a newspaper writes *the suspect claimed he was home* rather than *said he was home*: the verb is doing the work a disclaimer would otherwise have to do, and doing it in one word. The grammatical trap that runs through the whole group is the **object slot** — most of these verbs refuse a personal object outright (✗ *She confirmed me that the meeting was on*, ✗ *He announced us the news*), while *assure* refuses to go without one (✗ *He assured that the parts would arrive*), and the two verbs that straddle demanding and asserting, *insist* and *stipulate*, change the **mood** of their own *that*-clause depending on which act they name.

## The doubt scale

The single most useful thing in this file. Same content, seven verbs, seven different messages to the reader about the writer's own position:

| Verb | What it signals about the writer's stance | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [*confirm*](#confirm), [*point out*](08-ask-explain.md#point-out), [*note*](01-neutral.md#note) | The writer accepts it as true — **factive** | She **confirmed** that the flight was canceled. |
| [*say*](01-neutral.md#say), [*state*](01-neutral.md#state), [*announce*](#announce), [*declare*](#declare) | Neutral. The writer takes no position at all | She **said** the flight was canceled. |
| [*assert*](#assert), [*proclaim*](#proclaim) | Neutral content, forceful manner — in news copy, a faint raised eyebrow | She **asserted** that the flight was canceled. |
| [*maintain*](#maintain), [*insist*](#insist), [*contend*](#contend) | Someone disputes this, and the speaker is holding the line | She **insisted** that the flight was canceled. |
| [*claim*](#claim), [*profess*](#profess) | The writer is **not vouching** for it | She **claimed** the flight was canceled. |
| [*allege*](#allege) | Unproven **and** accusatory — someone is charged with wrongdoing | She **alleged** that the airline had lied. |
| [*purport*](#purport) | Probably not what it appears to be; usually of documents and objects | A memo **purporting** to show the cancellation. |

Nothing on that scale is a hedge you can drop. Swapping *said* for *claimed* in a news story changes what the story asserts, and swapping *claimed* for *confirmed* can make the paper liable for the statement. Journalists and lawyers pick from this column deliberately; a learner who treats them as stylistic variants of *say* will write sentences that mean the opposite of what was intended.

### affirm

**Pronunciation:** /əˈfɜrm/ &middot; *uh-FURM*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement, formally and deliberately restated &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *affirm* + **that**-clause &middot; *affirm* + noun phrase &middot; *affirm* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *affirm* (intransitive, in court: *he affirmed rather than swore*)
**Never:** ***affirm someone that*** and ***affirm to do something***. There is no personal object and no infinitive complement: ✗ *He affirmed us that the funding was secure*, ✗ *She affirmed to support the measure*. The addressee, if you need one, takes *to*. Repairs: *affirmed that the funding was secure*, *affirmed to the committee that the funding was secure*, *affirmed her support for the measure*, or — if the point is settling someone's doubt rather than stating for the record — *assured us that the funding was secure*.
**Reports:** a public, deliberate statement that something is true, usually restating a position already on record. Unlike *claim*, it carries no doubt; unlike *confirm*, it does not require a prior question. Two specialized senses live alongside it: to *affirm* in court is to make a solemn declaration **in place of** an oath (*He affirmed rather than swore*), and an appellate court *affirms* a lower court's ruling by letting it stand.

> "We remain committed to the treaty," the ambassador said.  →  The ambassador **affirmed that** they **remained** committed to the treaty.
> "Yes — the policy has not changed."  →  She **affirmed that** the policy **had** not **changed**.

1. The board *affirmed* that no layoffs were planned for the coming year.
2. She quietly *affirmed* her belief that the study would hold up.
3. The witness chose to *affirm* rather than swear on a Bible.
4. The appeals court *affirmed* the conviction in a two-page order.
5. The ambassador *affirmed* to reporters that the treaty remained in force.
6. In his letter he *affirmed* that the money had been repaid in full.
7. Nobody at the hearing *affirmed* that the deadline was still in force.

### allege

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɛdʒ/ &middot; *uh-LEJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — accusation asserted but unproven &middot; **Register:** formal; journalistic and legal
**Patterns:** *allege* + **that**-clause &middot; *allege* + noun phrase &middot; *be alleged* + **to-infinitive** (passive) &middot; *allege* + sb + **to be** (rare, legal)
**Never:** ***allege to do something*** in the active voice — ✗ *He alleged to have seen the car*. The infinitive frame exists only in the passive: ✓ *He is alleged to have seen the car*. Also ✗ *allege someone something* and ✗ *allege sb that*: ✗ *She alleged the police that he had lied* → ✓ *She alleged that he had lied*.
**Reports:** the strongest doubt marker in English, and the only one that also carries an accusation. *Allege* tells the reader two things at once: the statement is **unproven**, and it charges someone with wrongdoing. This is exactly why journalists reach for it — writing *the company allegedly falsified the invoices* reports the accusation without the newspaper adopting it. Use it for wrongdoing only; *she alleged that the train was late* is comic, because nothing is at stake.

> "Their auditor destroyed the files," the plaintiff said.  →  The plaintiff **alleged that** their auditor **had destroyed** the files.
> "He took money from the account."  →  He **is alleged to have taken** money from the account.

1. Prosecutors *allege* that the payments were routed through three shell companies.
2. The suit *alleges* a pattern of retaliation going back four years.
3. She is *alleged* to have signed the forms without reading them.
4. None of the *alleged* victims has been named publicly.
5. The article carefully *alleged* nothing it could not document.
6. He *allegedly* left the building twenty minutes before the alarm sounded.

**See also:** [claim](#claim), [purport](#purport), [assert](#assert), [contend](#contend), [testify](#testify)

### announce

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnaʊns/ &middot; *uh-NOWNSS*
**Type:** reporting verb — public statement of news &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *announce* + **that**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase &middot; *announce* + **wh**-clause &middot; *announce* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase + **to** sb
**Never:** ***announce someone something*** and ***announce someone that***. *Announce* is not ditransitive; the addressee needs *to*: ✗ *She announced us the results* → ✓ *She announced the results to us* / *announced to us that the results were in*. Also ✗ *announce to do*.
**Reports:** making something publicly known, usually for the first time. It presupposes both that the content is **true** and that it is **news** — which is why *he announced that he was tired* sounds like a joke. Compared with *declare* it is plainer and less ceremonial; compared with *proclaim* it lacks any grandeur.

> "We're moving the office to Denver," she told the staff.  →  She **announced to** the staff **that** they **were moving** the office to Denver.
> "The winner is Maria Okonkwo."  →  He **announced that** the winner **was** Maria Okonkwo.

1. The mayor *announced* that the pool would reopen in June.
2. They *announced* the merger an hour before the market opened.
3. She walked in and *announced* to nobody in particular that the elevator was broken.
4. The airline has not yet *announced* which routes it will cut.
5. He *announced* his resignation in a two-sentence email.
6. Nothing was *announced* about the second round of funding.

**See also:** [declare](#declare), [proclaim](#proclaim), [pronounce](#pronounce), [confirm](#confirm)

### argue

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑrɡju/ &middot; *AR-gyoo*
**Type:** reporting verb — a claim advanced with reasons &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *argue* + **that**-clause &middot; *argue* + **for/against** + noun phrase / **-ing** &middot; *argue* + noun phrase (*argue a case, argue a point*) &middot; *argue* + sb + **into/out of** + **-ing** &middot; *argue* + **about/over** + noun phrase / **wh**-clause (quarrel sense) &middot; *argue* + **with** sb (+ **about** sth)
**Never:** ***argue someone that*** and ***argue to do something*** — ✗ *He argued me that the plan was risky*, ✗ *She argued to raise the fee*. Repairs: *argued that the plan was risky*, *argued for raising the fee*. And note that the persuasion frame requires a preposition: ✗ *He argued me to stay* → ✓ *He argued me into staying*.
**Reports:** asserting a position **together with the reasons for it**. The verb commits the speaker to a supporting case, so it presupposes that the point is contestable — which is why it is the default verb of academic writing (*Ellison argues that…*) but sounds combative in a report of casual conversation. It does not signal doubt the way *claim* does; it signals contested ground.

> "The data don't support that conclusion, and here's why," she said.  →  She **argued that** the data **did** not **support** the conclusion.
> "We should hire two, not one."  →  He **argued for** hiring two rather than one.

1. She *argues* that the second experiment was never properly controlled.
2. The defense *argued* the case for four days.
3. He *argued* against expanding the program until the audit was finished.
4. They *argued* her into taking the job by promising a relocation budget.
5. The two of them *argued* about whether to sell the house.
6. It could be *argued* that the whole policy was obsolete by the time it passed.

**See also:** [contend](#contend), [maintain](#maintain), [assert](#assert), [submit](#submit), [posit](#posit)

### assert

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsɜrt/ &middot; *uh-SURT*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement made forcefully and without hedging &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *assert* + **that**-clause &middot; *assert* + noun phrase (*assert a right, assert control*) &middot; *assert* + **oneself**
**Never:** ***assert someone that***, ***assert someone something***, ***assert to do something***, and ***assert someone to do something***. ✗ *He asserted the committee that the figures were sound*, ✗ *She asserted to know the answer*, ✗ *They asserted him to have signed the form*. Repairs: *asserted that the figures were sound*, *claimed to know the answer*, *asserted that he had signed the form*.
**Reports:** stating something as fact, flatly and with confidence, and usually without offering support. That last part is what gives *assert* its faint edge in journalism: by drawing attention to the **confidence** rather than the evidence, it invites the reader to notice that nothing was proved. It is a milder doubt marker than *claim* — closer to neutral — but it is not fully neutral, and *he asserted* in a news story is rarely a compliment.

> "The vote was rigged. Period," he said.  →  He **asserted that** the vote **had been** rigged.
> "This land is ours."  →  They **asserted their** claim to the land.

1. The report *asserts* that the software was never tested on live data.
2. She *asserted* her right to have a lawyer present.
3. He *asserted*, without producing a single document, that the deal had been approved.
4. The company has *asserted* control over three of the four subsidiaries.
5. In meetings she has learned to *assert* herself early.
6. Nobody in the room *asserted* anything they could not back up.

**See also:** [claim](#claim), [contend](#contend), [maintain](#maintain), [aver](#aver), [declare](#declare)

### assure

**Pronunciation:** /əˈʃʊr/ &middot; *uh-SHOOR*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement made to remove someone's doubt &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *assure* + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *assure* + sb + **of** + noun phrase &middot; *assure* + sb (alone)
**Never:** ***assure that*** with no object. This is the single commonest error with the verb: ✗ *He assured that the parts would arrive Friday.* *Assure* is **obligatorily ditransitive** — somebody has to be reassured. Repairs: *assured us that the parts would arrive*, or drop the verb for a neutral one: *said that the parts would arrive*. Also ✗ *assure to sb that*, ✗ *assure sb to do* (that is *promise* or *urge*).
**Reports:** telling someone directly that something is true, in order to settle their doubt. Where *affirm* is oriented toward the record, *assure* is oriented toward the **addressee** — the point is the listener's peace of mind, not the public statement. It carries no doubt of its own, but the writer's *he assured us* often invites the reader to notice that the assurance may not have held.

> "Don't worry — the check went out Monday," she said.  →  She **assured me that** the check **had gone** out **that Monday**.
> "The building is completely safe."  →  He **assured** the tenants **that** the building **was** completely safe.

1. The nurse *assured* him that the swelling would go down in a day or two.
2. She *assured* us of her support before the vote was even scheduled.
3. I can *assure* you that nobody here saw the email.
4. He *assured* the landlord the rent would clear by Friday.
5. Rest *assured* — the file was backed up before the crash.
6. The contractor *assured* them repeatedly, and then missed the deadline anyway.

| Word | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *assure* | remove someone's doubt (needs a person as object) | She **assured** me the room was booked. |
| *ensure* | make certain that something happens | She **ensured** the room was booked. |
| *insure* | cover with an insurance policy | She **insured** the room against damage. |

**See also:** [affirm](#affirm), [confirm](#confirm), [swear](#swear), [promise](03-agree-refuse.md#promise)

### attest

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtɛst/ &middot; *uh-TEST*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal statement from firsthand knowledge &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and ceremonial
**Patterns:** *attest* + **that**-clause &middot; *attest* + **to** + noun phrase / **-ing** / **wh**-clause &middot; *attest* + noun phrase (*attest a signature, attest a will*)
**Never:** ***attest to*** + a **that**-clause. The preposition takes a noun phrase, an *-ing* form, or a *wh*-clause — but never a bare *that*-clause: ✗ *She attested to that the copy was genuine.* Repairs: *attested that the copy was genuine*, or *attested to the authenticity of the copy*, or the idiom *attested to the fact that the copy was genuine*. Also ✗ *attest someone that* and ✗ *attest to do*.
**Reports:** bearing witness — asserting something on the strength of one's own knowledge, usually in a setting where the statement counts for something. It presupposes the speaker's **credibility** rather than the truth of the content, which is why an inanimate subject works so naturally: *the wear on the steps attests to a century of use*.

> "I saw him sign it," she said.  →  She **attested that** she **had seen** him sign it.
> "The translation is accurate."  →  The translator **attested to** the accuracy of the translation.

1. Two colleagues *attested* that he had been in the building all morning.
2. Anyone who has waited in that line can *attest* to how slow it is.
3. A notary must *attest* the signature before the deed is filed.
4. The scorch marks on the ceiling *attest* to an earlier fire.
5. She *attested* under penalty of perjury that the return was complete.
6. Nothing in the file *attests* to a second inspection.

**See also:** [testify](#testify), [swear](#swear), [affirm](#affirm), [confirm](#confirm), [aver](#aver)

### aver

**Pronunciation:** /əˈvɜr/ &middot; *uh-VUR*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement made positively and on the record &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, literary, rare in speech
**Patterns:** *aver* + **that**-clause &middot; *aver* + noun phrase (legal: *aver facts in a pleading*)
**Never:** ***aver someone that*** and ***aver to do something*** — ✗ *He averred the court that he had paid*, ✗ *She averred to have paid*. Repairs: *averred that he had paid*, *claimed to have paid*.
**Reports:** stating something confidently and formally, and in legal pleading, alleging a fact one intends to prove at trial. Outside of court filings and self-consciously literary prose it reads as precious or archaic — *he averred* in ordinary American writing sounds like a nineteenth-century novel. Say *said*, *insisted*, or *asserted* instead unless the register genuinely calls for it.

> "I never received the notice," he said.  →  He **averred that** he **had** never **received** the notice.
> "The defendant knew the account was overdrawn."  →  The complaint **avers that** the defendant **knew** the account **was** overdrawn.

1. The complaint *avers* that the contract was signed under duress.
2. He *averred*, with a straight face, that he had read every page.
3. She *averred* her innocence in the same flat tone all afternoon.
4. Counsel must *aver* the facts supporting jurisdiction in the opening paragraphs.
5. In the memoir he *avers* that the letter was destroyed by accident.
6. Nobody in the deposition *averred* anything about a second meeting.

**See also:** [assert](#assert), [allege](#allege), [avow](#avow), [maintain](#maintain), [submit](#submit)

### avow

**Pronunciation:** /əˈvaʊ/ &middot; *uh-VOW*
**Type:** reporting verb — open admission of a belief or allegiance &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *avow* + **that**-clause &middot; *avow* + noun phrase &middot; *avow* + **oneself** + noun phrase / adjective (*avowed himself satisfied*)
**Never:** ***avow someone that*** and ***avow to do something*** — ✗ *He avowed us that he had voted for it*, ✗ *She avowed to resign*. Repairs: *avowed that he had voted for it*, *vowed to resign* (a different verb, and a different act).
**Reports:** declaring openly something one might reasonably be expected to conceal — a belief, a loyalty, a motive. It presupposes **candor** and a small cost to the speaker. The past participle is far commoner than the finite verb: *an avowed socialist*, *her avowed aim*. Its opposite, *disavow*, denies any connection: *the campaign disavowed the flyer*.

> "Yes, I supported the strike, and I still do," she said.  →  She **avowed that** she **had supported** the strike and still **did**.
> "I am, and always have been, an anarchist."  →  He **avowed himself** an anarchist.

1. She *avowed* that her only interest in the case was the principle.
2. He publicly *avowed* his support for the boycott a week before the vote.
3. An *avowed* skeptic, she read the whole report twice before commenting.
4. The senator *avowed* himself satisfied with the compromise.
5. The company *disavowed* the memo within hours of its leaking.
6. Few of them would *avow* in writing what they said freely in the hallway.

**See also:** [profess](#profess), [declare](#declare), [affirm](#affirm), [aver](#aver), [proclaim](#proclaim)

### claim

**Pronunciation:** /kleɪm/ &middot; *KLAYM*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement the writer does not endorse &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *claim* + **that**-clause &middot; *claim* + **to-infinitive** (same subject) &middot; *claim* + noun phrase &middot; *claim* + sb/sth + **to be** (formal, rare)
**Never:** ***claim someone to do something*** and ***claim someone that***. ✗ *He claimed me to be lying*, ✗ *She claimed us that the package had shipped*. *Claim* takes no indirect object and no object-controlled infinitive. Repairs: *claimed that I was lying*, *told us that the package had shipped*.
**Control:** *claim* + to-infinitive is **same-subject only**. *He claimed to have paid* means **he** paid. To report a claim about somebody else you need the *that*-clause: *He claimed that his brother had paid* — never ✗ *He claimed his brother to have paid*. The one apparent exception is the formal *claim* + object + ***to be*** frame in the Patterns line (*the report claimed the signature to be a forgery*): it is restricted to *to be* plus a complement, it is confined to legal and academic prose, and a learner is always safe rewriting it as a *that*-clause. No other infinitive is available after an object.
**Reports:** the everyday doubt marker of English. Writing *he claimed he was home* tells the reader, in one word, that the writer is **not vouching** for it. That is a real editorial act, not a stylistic flourish: *said* is neutral, *pointed out* endorses, *claimed* withholds. Unlike *allege*, it does not accuse anyone; unlike *purport*, it takes a human subject comfortably.

> "I paid that invoice in March," he said.  →  He **claimed that** he **had paid** the invoice **that** March.
> "I speak four languages."  →  She **claimed to speak** four languages.

| Verb | The reader is told | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *said* | nothing about the writer's view | He **said** he was home. |
| *pointed out* | the writer accepts it | He **pointed out** that he was home. |
| *claimed* | the writer does not vouch for it | He **claimed** he was home. |
| *alleged* | it is unproven **and** accusatory | He **alleged** that she was home. |

1. He *claims* the fence was already down when he got there.
2. She *claimed* to have no memory of the conversation.
3. Two different groups have *claimed* responsibility for the outage.
4. The label *claims* the bottle contains no added sugar.
5. Nobody has *claimed* the bag left in the lobby.
6. They *claimed* a deduction that the auditor immediately disallowed.

**See also:** [allege](#allege), [purport](#purport), [profess](#profess), [assert](#assert), [maintain](#maintain)

### confirm

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈfɜrm/ &middot; *kun-FURM*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement verifying something already in question &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *confirm* + **that**-clause &middot; *confirm* + noun phrase &middot; *confirm* + **wh**-clause &middot; *confirm* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *be confirmed* + **to-infinitive** (passive)
**Never:** ***confirm someone that*** — ✗ *She confirmed me that the meeting was on.* The addressee needs *to*: ✓ *She confirmed to me that the meeting was on*, or simply *She confirmed that the meeting was on*. Also ✗ *confirm to do*.
**Reports:** stating that something already suggested, rumored, or asked about is in fact true. It is **factive** — *He confirmed that the deal had collapsed* commits the writer to the collapse, which is why a newspaper will not use it for a statement it cannot stand behind. It also presupposes a prior question; there is nothing to confirm if nobody wondered.

> "Yes, the flight is canceled," the agent said.  →  The agent **confirmed that** the flight **was** canceled.
> "Has the check cleared?" — "It has."  →  She **confirmed that** the check **had cleared**.

1. The lab *confirmed* that the sample was contaminated.
2. Can you *confirm* whether the room is still available for Thursday?
3. He *confirmed* the appointment by text an hour beforehand.
4. Two sources have *confirmed* to the paper that the talks broke down.
5. The species was later *confirmed* to be new to science.
6. Nobody at the agency would *confirm* who had signed off on the waiver.

**See also:** [affirm](#affirm), [reaffirm](#reaffirm), [attest](#attest), [announce](#announce), [claim](#claim)

### contend

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈtɛnd/ &middot; *kun-TEND*
**Type:** reporting verb — a claim advanced inside a dispute &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *contend* + **that**-clause &middot; *contend* + **with** + noun phrase (a different sense: cope with) &middot; *contend* + **for** + noun phrase (a different sense: compete for)
**Never:** ***contend someone that***, ***contend to do something***, and — in the reporting sense — ***contend + noun phrase***. ✗ *He contended his innocence* → ✓ *He contended that he was innocent* or *asserted his innocence*. ✗ *The union contended the company to be at fault* → ✓ *contended that the company was at fault*.
**Reports:** asserting a position in an argument that has another side. *Contend* presupposes an **opponent**, which is why it belongs to court reporting, academic disagreement, and news coverage of disputes. It carries a mild doubt tint — the writer is describing one party's position, not the facts — but it is more dignified than *claim* and does not accuse anyone.

> "The clause never applied to seasonal staff," the union said.  →  The union **contended that** the clause **had** never **applied** to seasonal staff.
> "Their figures are three years out of date."  →  She **contended that** their figures **were** three years out of date.

1. The defense *contends* that the search was unlawful from the start.
2. Critics *contend* the model breaks down at low temperatures.
3. He *contended* that no one had ever explained the fee to him.
4. Both sides *contend* that the other walked away from the table.
5. She has *contended* for years that the surveys undercount renters.
6. It has been *contended*, though never demonstrated, that the two events are linked.

**See also:** [argue](#argue), [maintain](#maintain), [assert](#assert), [submit](#submit), [hold](#hold)

### declare

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈklɛr/ &middot; *dih-KLAIR*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal public statement made with authority &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *declare* + **that**-clause &middot; *declare* + noun phrase &middot; *declare* + sb/sth + (**to be**) + complement (*declared the election invalid*) &middot; *declare* + **oneself** + adjective / noun phrase (*declared herself satisfied*, *declared himself a candidate*) &middot; *declare* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *declare* + **for/against** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***declare someone that*** and ***declare someone something*** — ✗ *He declared us that the meeting was over*, ✗ *She declared me the results*. Repairs: *declared to us that the meeting was over*, *announced the results to me*. Also ✗ *declare to do*.
**Reports:** stating something formally and with standing, often in a way that **makes it so** — *I declare this meeting adjourned* does not describe an adjournment, it performs one. On the doubt scale it is neutral as to truth but loud as to manner, and in ordinary narrative it lends a whiff of theater: *she declared that she was finished with the whole thing*.

> "This election is void," the commission said.  →  The commission **declared** the election **void**.
> "I'm never doing that again," she said.  →  She **declared that** she **was** never **doing** that again.

1. The governor *declared* a state of emergency before the storm made landfall.
2. He *declared* that the numbers had been checked twice.
3. The judge *declared* the contract unenforceable.
4. She *declared* herself satisfied and went home.
5. You have to *declare* anything over ten thousand dollars at customs.
6. Two senators have *declared* against the bill in its current form.

**See also:** [announce](#announce), [proclaim](#proclaim), [pronounce](#pronounce), [affirm](#affirm), [assert](#assert)

### emphasize

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛmfəsaɪz/ &middot; *EM-fuh-syze*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement given extra weight &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *emphasize* + **that**-clause &middot; *emphasize* + noun phrase &middot; *emphasize* + **wh**-clause &middot; *emphasize* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***emphasize on*** — the commonest error with this verb, imported from the noun. The **noun** takes *on* (*put emphasis on the deadline*); the **verb** takes a plain direct object: ✗ *He emphasized on the deadline* → ✓ *He emphasized the deadline*. Also ✗ *emphasize someone that* (✗ *She emphasized me that it was urgent* → ✓ *emphasized to me that it was urgent*) and ✗ *emphasize to do*.
**Spelling:** American English writes **-ize**. *Emphasise* is British and has no place in American copy — see [spelling the reporting verbs](../03-no-backshift.md#spelling-the-reporting-verbs).
**Reports:** not a new claim but extra weight on part of one. It is **factive**: *she emphasized that the deadline was firm* commits the writer to the deadline being firm. Compared with *insist* it implies no resistance; the speaker is underlining, not defending.

> "And this is the important part — the deposit is nonrefundable," she said.  →  She **emphasized that** the deposit **was** nonrefundable.
> "Remember, no exceptions."  →  He **emphasized to** the class **that** there **would be** no exceptions.

1. She *emphasized* that the results were preliminary.
2. The coach *emphasized* footwork all through the first week.
3. He *emphasized* how little time was left before the filing date.
4. The memo *emphasized* to managers that overtime had to be approved in advance.
5. Every speaker *emphasized* the same three numbers.
6. It cannot be *emphasized* enough that the backup runs only on weekdays.

**See also:** [stress](#stress), [underscore](#underscore), [underline](#underline), [reiterate](#reiterate), [insist](#insist)

### hold

**Pronunciation:** /hoʊld/ &middot; *HOHLD* — irregular: *hold, held, held*
**Type:** reporting verb — a settled belief, or a court's binding ruling &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *hold* + **that**-clause &middot; *hold* + sb/sth + (**to be**) + complement (*held the clause to be void*) &middot; *hold* + noun phrase (*hold the view, hold an opinion*) &middot; *it is held* + **that**-clause (passive, very common)
**Never:** ***hold someone that*** and ***hold to do something*** — ✗ *He held the jury that the statute applied*, ✗ *The court held to reverse the judgment*. Repairs: *held that the statute applied*, *voted to reverse the judgment*.
**Reports:** maintaining a position one is settled in rather than arguing toward — a standing belief, a doctrine, or, in law, the part of a decision that binds later courts. Keep the legal pair straight: a court **holds** the law (*the court held that the search was unlawful*) and **finds** the facts (*the court found that the officer had entered at 9 p.m.*).

> "In my view, the deadline was never binding," she said.  →  She **held that** the deadline **had** never **been** binding.
> "The clause is void."  →  The court **held** the clause **to be** void.

1. Most economists *hold* that the effect is too small to measure.
2. The Ninth Circuit *held* that the ordinance violated the First Amendment.
3. She *holds* the view that the whole department should be restructured.
4. It is widely *held* that the practice began in the 1930s.
5. He *held* the study to be worthless and said so in print.
6. Nobody now *holds* that the two species are closely related.

**See also:** [maintain](#maintain), [contend](#contend), [submit](#submit), [pronounce](#pronounce), [posit](#posit)

### insist

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈsɪst/ &middot; *in-SIST*
**Type:** reporting verb — **two** acts under one word: a statement held against resistance, and a demand &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *insist* + **that**-clause (indicative = claim; bare subjunctive = demand) &middot; *insist* + **on/upon** + noun phrase &middot; *insist* + **on/upon** + **-ing** &middot; *insist* + **on/upon** + sb + **-ing** &middot; *insist* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***insist someone to do something*** — ✗ *She insisted me to stay*. This is the classic error, and *insist* blocks every part of it: no personal object, no infinitive, no object-controlled complement. Also ✗ *insist to do*, ✗ *insist sb that*, and ✗ *insist doing* — the *-ing* form is never bare, it always needs *on*: ✗ *She insisted paying* → ✓ *She insisted on paying*. Repairs: *insisted that I stay*, *insisted on my staying*, *insisted on me staying* (both possessive and object forms are current; the possessive is the more formal).
**Subjunctive:** this is the verb the mandative subjunctive was built for, and the mood you choose decides which speech act you are reporting. In the **demand** sense American English puts a **bare base form** in the *that*-clause — no *-s*, no *to*, no *should* — and because a bare form has no tense, reporting leaves it untouched. In the **claim** sense the clause is an ordinary tensed one and [backshifts](../02-backshift.md) normally.

| | Claim sense — "I'm telling you this is so" | Demand sense — "I require this" |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | "He **was** there, I'm telling you." | "He **must be** there." |
| Reported | She insisted that he **had been** there. | She insisted that he **be** there. |
| Mood | indicative, backshifts | bare subjunctive, no shift |
| Third person singular | …insisted that he **pays** his share *(a claim about what he does)* | …insisted that he **pay** his share *(a demand)* |
| Negative | …insisted that she **wasn't** told | …insisted that she **not be** told |
| British equivalent | same | …insisted that he **should be** there |

Two consequences worth memorizing. **The negative is a bare *not* in front of the verb** — no *do*, no agreement: ✓ *insisted that she not be told*, ✗ *that she doesn't be told*, ✗ *that she not is told*. And the subjunctive is **invisible** except in the third-person singular and with *be*, so *they insisted that we leave* gives no clue which reading is intended; only *that he leave* and *that it be filed* show their hand. The same pattern runs after *demand*, *require*, *request*, *recommend*, *propose*, *urge*, *ask*, and *stipulate*, and after *it is essential / vital / imperative that* — the full treatment is in [the mandative subjunctive](../08-special-cases.md#the-mandative-subjunctive-american-be-british-should-be).

> "I insist that he **be** present," she said.  →  She **insisted that** he **be** present. *(a demand — no shift, because the subjunctive has no tense to move)*
> "He **was** present, I'm telling you," she insisted.  →  She **insisted that** he **had been** present. *(a claim — ordinary backshift)*
> "We demand that the fee **be** waived," they wrote.  →  They **demanded that** the fee **be waived**. *(the same bare subjunctive after* demand*)*

**Reports:** in the claim sense, continuing to assert something against doubt or contradiction — a soft signal that the hearer may not be buying it. In the demand sense, refusing to accept any alternative. Decide which act the speaker performed **before** you choose the mood.

1. He *insisted* that the door had been locked when he left.
2. She *insisted* that the report be circulated before the meeting, not after.
3. They *insisted* on paying for dinner.
4. The landlord *insisted* on our signing a two-year lease.
5. I said no twice, and he *insisted* anyway.
6. The union *insisted* that no one be laid off before the arbitration was finished.
7. She *insists* she never received the second notice.

**See also:** [maintain](#maintain), [stress](#stress), [contend](#contend), [stipulate](#stipulate), [protest](#protest), [recommend](05-advise-suggest.md#recommend)

### maintain

**Pronunciation:** /meɪnˈteɪn/ &middot; *mayn-TAYN*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement held to under pressure &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *maintain* + **that**-clause &middot; *maintain* + noun phrase (*maintain one's innocence, maintain a position*)
**Never:** ***maintain someone that*** and ***maintain to do something*** — ✗ *He maintained the police that he was home*, ✗ *She maintained to be innocent*. Repairs: *maintained that he was home*, *maintained her innocence* / *claimed to be innocent*.
**Reports:** continuing to assert something in the face of contradiction. *Maintain* presupposes a **challenge already made** — the word itself says the position is under attack — so it is a soft doubt marker: the reader infers that somebody credible disagrees. Compare *insist*, which adds force and impatience, and *contend*, which places the statement inside a formal dispute.

> "I've said it before: I was never told," he said.  →  He **maintained that** he **had** never **been told**.
> "The figures are correct."  →  She **maintained that** the figures **were** correct.

1. He has *maintained* from the first day that the account was not his.
2. She *maintained* her innocence through two trials.
3. The company *maintains* that the recall was voluntary.
4. Both witnesses *maintained* that the light had been green.
5. He *maintained* this position long after his own lawyers gave up on it.
6. They *maintain*, against all the evidence, that the map is accurate.

**See also:** [insist](#insist), [contend](#contend), [claim](#claim), [hold](#hold), [assert](#assert)

### pledge

**Pronunciation:** /plɛdʒ/ &middot; *PLEJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal public commitment &middot; **Register:** formal; journalistic
**Patterns:** *pledge* + **to-infinitive** &middot; *pledge* + **that**-clause &middot; *pledge* + noun phrase &middot; *pledge* + sb + noun phrase (*pledged them his support*) &middot; *pledge* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *pledge* + **oneself** + **to** + noun phrase / **-ing**
**Never:** ***pledge someone to do something*** in the sense "promise them that you will" — ✗ *He pledged us to fix the road.* That frame exists but means the opposite: *pledge sb to sth* **binds the other person** (*she pledged them to secrecy*). Nor is there a ditransitive *that*-clause: ✗ *He pledged us that he would fix the road* — the addressee needs *to*. Repairs: *pledged to fix the road*, *pledged to us that he would fix the road*, or *promised us that he would fix the road*. The only noun the addressee can carry directly is a thing pledged, not a clause: ✓ *pledged them his support*.
**Reports:** a formal, usually public promise, generally with money, policy, or institutional weight attached. Weaker as a personal oath than *vow*, and less solemn than *swear* — a government pledges, a person vows. It presupposes an audience: pledges are made where they can be counted.

> "We will cut emissions by half," the minister said.  →  The minister **pledged to cut** emissions by half.
> "I'll match whatever the staff raises."  →  He **pledged that** he **would match** whatever the staff **raised**.

1. The foundation *pledged* four million dollars over three years.
2. Both candidates *pledged* to release ten years of tax returns.
3. She *pledged* that no employee would lose health coverage in the transition.
4. He *pledged* them his full support and then missed every meeting.
5. The signatories *pledged* themselves to a common set of standards.
6. Donors have *pledged* more than they have actually paid.

**See also:** [vow](#vow), [swear](#swear), [affirm](#affirm), [promise](03-agree-refuse.md#promise)

### posit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑzɪt/ &middot; *PAH-zit*
**Type:** reporting verb — a claim put forward as a starting assumption &middot; **Register:** formal, academic
**Patterns:** *posit* + **that**-clause &middot; *posit* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***posit someone that***, ***posit to do something***, and ***posit about***. ✗ *She posited us that the effect was real*, ✗ *He posited about a third mechanism*. Repairs: *posited that the effect was real*, *posited a third mechanism*.
**Reports:** advancing a proposition as a **premise**, for the sake of argument, without asserting that it is true. It is the lowest-commitment verb in this file: *let us posit that the market is efficient* concedes nothing about actual markets. This makes it useless for reporting ordinary speech — nobody posits anything at dinner — and indispensable in academic writing.

> "Suppose, for the moment, that the sample is representative," she said.  →  She **posited that** the sample **was** representative.
> "There must be a fourth force."  →  He **posited** a fourth force.

1. The paper *posits* that language learning and music share a single mechanism.
2. Let's *posit* a reader who has never seen the earlier draft.
3. She *posited* an intermediate stage that nobody has yet observed.
4. Critics say the model *posits* far more structure than the data support.
5. He *posited* that the two outbreaks had a common source, then set out to test it.
6. Nothing in the theory *posits* a hard limit on the number of participants.

**See also:** [postulate](#postulate), [submit](#submit), [hold](#hold), [argue](#argue), [contend](#contend)

### postulate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑstʃəleɪt/ &middot; *PAHSS-chuh-layt* — the noun shifts to /ˈpɑstʃələt/, *PAHSS-chuh-lit*
**Type:** reporting verb — a claim asserted without proof, as a basis for reasoning &middot; **Register:** formal, academic and scientific
**Patterns:** *postulate* + **that**-clause &middot; *postulate* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***postulate someone that*** and ***postulate to do something*** — ✗ *He postulated us that the gene was duplicated*, ✗ *She postulated to explain the gap*. Repairs: *postulated that the gene had been duplicated*, *postulated a mechanism to explain the gap*.
**Reports:** asserting something as true without evidence, in order to build on it. Slightly more committed than *posit* — a postulate is something you intend to keep, not merely to try out — and much more committed than *hypothesize*, which invites a test. The vowel of the last syllable is the only thing separating the verb from the noun: *scientists postulate* /leɪt/ a mechanism, and the *postulate* /lət/ is what they end up with.

> "Assume a fifth planet, and the orbits work," he said.  →  He **postulated** a fifth planet.
> "There has to be an enzyme doing this."  →  She **postulated that** an enzyme **was** responsible.

1. Mendel *postulated* particles of inheritance long before anyone saw a chromosome.
2. The theory *postulates* that the two populations were separated for ten thousand years.
3. She *postulated* an unobserved intermediate to make the numbers work.
4. Nothing in the argument requires us to *postulate* a designer.
5. He *postulated* that memory consolidation happens mostly during sleep.
6. Every one of the *postulates* was later shown to be unnecessary.

**See also:** [posit](#posit), [hold](#hold), [assert](#assert), [submit](#submit), [argue](#argue)

### proclaim

**Pronunciation:** /proʊˈkleɪm/ &middot; *proh-KLAYM*
**Type:** reporting verb — loud, public, ceremonial declaration &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *proclaim* + **that**-clause &middot; *proclaim* + noun phrase &middot; *proclaim* + sb/sth + (**to be**) + complement (*proclaimed himself president*) &middot; *proclaim* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***proclaim someone that*** — ✗ *He proclaimed us that the war was over.* Repairs: *proclaimed to us that the war was over*, or *announced that the war was over*. Also ✗ *proclaim to do*.
**Reports:** announcing publicly and grandly, with more ceremony than *declare* and far more than *announce*. In modern American prose it is very often **ironic**: *he proclaimed the redesign a triumph* signals that the writer does not agree. Used straight, it belongs to proclamations, coronations, and headlines.

> "The revolution has succeeded!"  →  They **proclaimed that** the revolution **had succeeded**.
> "I am the rightful heir."  →  He **proclaimed himself** the rightful heir.

1. The governor *proclaimed* the first Monday in May a day of remembrance.
2. She *proclaimed* that the era of cheap money was over.
3. He *proclaimed* himself an expert after reading two articles.
4. The banner *proclaimed* a grand reopening that never happened.
5. Every review *proclaimed* the album a masterpiece.
6. Nobody was ready to *proclaim* victory with a third of the votes uncounted.

### profess

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈfɛs/ &middot; *pruh-FESS*
**Type:** reporting verb — an open claim about one's own beliefs, feelings, or abilities &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *profess* + **to-infinitive** (same subject; especially *profess to be*) &middot; *profess* + **that**-clause &middot; *profess* + noun phrase (*professed his innocence*) &middot; *profess* + **oneself** + adjective / noun phrase (*professed himself satisfied*, *professed herself a convert*)
**Never:** ***profess someone to do something*** and ***profess someone that*** — ✗ *He professed her to be innocent*, ✗ *She professed us that she knew nothing*. The infinitive is same-subject only. Repairs: *professed that she was innocent*, *professed to know nothing*.
**Reports:** claiming openly to hold a belief, feel a feeling, or possess a skill — and with the *to*-infinitive, the writer is almost always signaling **skepticism**. *He professes to care about the neighborhood* invites the reader to doubt that he does; *he says he cares* does not. That built-in eyebrow is the difference between *profess* and *avow*, which presupposes candor.

> "I have no idea how the file got there," he said.  →  He **professed to have** no idea how the file **had gotten** there.
> "I'm perfectly happy with the outcome."  →  She **professed herself** perfectly happy with the outcome.

1. He *professes* to know nothing about how the money moved.
2. She *professed* a lifelong love of opera and then fell asleep in the second act.
3. Both candidates *profess* deep concern about the deficit.
4. The company *professes* that its supply chain is fully audited.
5. He *professed* himself shocked, which fooled nobody.
6. She *professed* her innocence in a two-page letter to the editor.

**See also:** [claim](#claim), [purport](#purport), [avow](#avow), [allege](#allege), [assert](#assert)

### pronounce

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈnaʊns/ &middot; *pruh-NOWNSS*
**Type:** reporting verb — an authoritative verdict &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *pronounce* + sb/sth + complement (*pronounced the patient dead*) &middot; *pronounce* + **that**-clause &middot; *pronounce* + **on/upon** + noun phrase &middot; *pronounce* + noun phrase (*pronounce sentence, pronounce judgment*)
**Never:** ***pronounce someone that*** and ***pronounce to do something*** — ✗ *The judge pronounced the jury that the trial was over*, ✗ *He pronounced to be satisfied*. Repairs: *told the jury that the trial was over*, *pronounced himself satisfied*.
**Reports:** delivering a judgment that is official because of who is delivering it. The verdict frame — *pronounce* + object + complement — is the core one, and it is **performative**: a doctor's *I pronounce him dead* creates the legal fact. Used of ordinary opinions it is deliberately grand, and usually a little arch: *she tasted it and pronounced it edible*. The other *pronounce* — saying a word aloud — is the same word and is covered in [Pronunciation](../../../10-pronunciation/README.md).

> "The defendant is guilty on both counts."  →  The jury **pronounced** him **guilty** on both counts.
> "In my judgment the treatment has failed."  →  The consultant **pronounced** the treatment **a failure**.

1. She was *pronounced* dead at the scene.
2. The critic tasted three of them and *pronounced* the second one best.
3. The court will *pronounce* sentence on Thursday morning.
4. He declined to *pronounce* on a case still under appeal.
5. The inspector *pronounced* the wiring safe and signed the form.
6. Nobody has *pronounced* the project finished, but nobody is working on it either.

**See also:** [declare](#declare), [hold](#hold), [proclaim](#proclaim), [announce](#announce)

### protest

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈtɛst/ &middot; *pruh-TEST* — the noun shifts the stress: /ˈproʊtɛst/, *PROH-test*
**Type:** reporting verb — an indignant assertion made against an accusation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *protest* + **that**-clause &middot; *protest* + noun phrase (*protest one's innocence*; American also *protest the ruling*) &middot; *protest* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *protest* + **against/at** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***protest someone that***, ***protest to do something***, and ***protest someone something*** — ✗ *He protested the guard that he had a pass*, ✗ *She protested to be innocent*. Repairs: *protested to the guard that he had a pass*, *protested that she was innocent*.
**Register note:** American English lets *protest* take a **direct object** in the demonstration sense — *students protested the tuition increase*. British English requires *protest against*. Both standards keep *against* available; only American writing drops it freely.
**Reports:** asserting something in objection, almost always against a charge, and almost always with the implication that **nobody is convinced**. *"I never touched it," he protested* is a sentence in which the reader already suspects he did. Shakespeare's *the lady doth protest too much* turns on exactly this: too much denial reads as guilt.

> "But I already sent it — twice!" she said.  →  She **protested that** she **had** already **sent** it twice.
> "This isn't what I ordered."  →  He **protested that** it **was** not what he **had ordered**.

1. He *protested* that the charge had never appeared on any statement he received.
2. She *protested* her innocence loudly enough for the whole floor to hear.
3. Hundreds *protested* the closure outside the district office.
4. "I'm not even on the schedule today," he *protested*.
5. The union *protested* against the new shift pattern in writing.
6. She *protested* that she had been given no warning, and the file showed she was right.

### purport

**Pronunciation:** /pərˈpɔrt/ &middot; *pur-PORT* — the noun shifts the stress: /ˈpɜrpɔrt/, *PUR-port*
**Type:** reporting verb — a claim the writer strongly doubts &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *purport* + **to-infinitive** (the main and almost the only frame) &middot; *purport* + **that**-clause (rare, formal)
**Never:** ***purport someone to do something***, ***purport + -ing***, and ***purport + noun phrase***. ✗ *The memo purports the deal to be closed*, ✗ *purporting showing the transfer*, ✗ *He purported a degree in physics*. Repairs: *purports to show that the deal is closed*, *purporting to show the transfer*, *claimed a degree in physics*.
**Reports:** what a thing **presents itself as being**, with the writer's clear suggestion that it may be nothing of the kind. It is the sharpest doubt marker available for documents, recordings, and objects: *a memo purporting to show the transfer* tells the reader the memo may well be a forgery. Human subjects are possible (*he purports to be a licensed inspector*) but the verb reaches its full force with inanimate ones. The adjective *purported* and adverb *purportedly* do the same job in a smaller space.

> "This recording is from the night of the fire."  →  A recording **purporting to be** from the night of the fire **was** entered into evidence.
> "I'm a certified appraiser."  →  He **purports to be** a certified appraiser.

1. The site posted a document *purporting* to be an internal audit.
2. She produced a letter *purporting* to authorize the withdrawal.
3. The study *purports* to measure attention span, but it measures reaction time.
4. His *purported* resignation was never signed.
5. The caller *purportedly* worked for the county assessor's office.
6. Nothing in the archive *purports* to be a complete record.

**See also:** [claim](#claim), [allege](#allege), [profess](#profess), [assert](#assert)

### reaffirm

**Pronunciation:** /ˌriəˈfɜrm/ &middot; *ree-uh-FURM*
**Type:** reporting verb — a commitment restated on the record &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *reaffirm* + **that**-clause &middot; *reaffirm* + noun phrase &middot; *reaffirm* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***reaffirm someone that*** and ***reaffirm to do something*** — ✗ *He reaffirmed the allies that the guarantee held*, ✗ *She reaffirmed to support the treaty*. Repairs: *reaffirmed to the allies that the guarantee held*, *reaffirmed her support for the treaty*.
**Reports:** stating again, formally, a position already taken — and the *re-* is doing real work, because the verb **presupposes the earlier statement**. Use it only where the reader knows or can infer what is being restated; *he reaffirmed that the office opens at nine* is odd unless somebody had raised a doubt. Compare *reiterate*, which carries impatience, and *confirm*, which answers a question rather than restating a promise.

> "Our commitment to the alliance has not changed," she said.  →  She **reaffirmed that** their commitment to the alliance **had** not **changed**.
> "We stand by the guarantee."  →  They **reaffirmed** the guarantee.

1. The board *reaffirmed* its commitment to the scholarship fund.
2. She *reaffirmed* that no data had left the building.
3. The court *reaffirmed* a rule it had announced twenty years earlier.
4. Both governments *reaffirmed* the borders set out in the 1997 treaty.
5. He *reaffirmed* the timeline without offering any new evidence for it.
6. Nothing in the statement *reaffirmed* the original spending figure.

### reiterate

**Pronunciation:** /riˈɪtəreɪt/ &middot; *ree-IT-uh-rayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — a statement repeated, usually because it was ignored &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *reiterate* + **that**-clause &middot; *reiterate* + noun phrase &middot; *reiterate* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***reiterate someone that*** and ***reiterate to do something*** — ✗ *He reiterated us that the deadline was Friday*, ✗ *She reiterated to check the figures*. Repairs: *reiterated to us that the deadline was Friday*, *repeated her instruction to check the figures*.
**Redundancy:** *reiterate again* and *reiterate once more* say the same thing twice. The repetition is already inside the word; write *reiterated* or *said again*, not both.
**Reports:** saying again what has already been said, with a distinct note of **impatience** — the verb implies the first time did not take. It presupposes the earlier statement, like *reaffirm*, but where *reaffirm* is ceremonial and forward-looking, *reiterate* is corrective.

> "As I said before, the account was closed in April," he said.  →  He **reiterated that** the account **had been** closed in April.
> "Once more: no personal devices on the network."  →  She **reiterated that** no personal devices **were** allowed on the network.

1. She *reiterated* that the offer would expire at midnight.
2. He *reiterated* the same three objections he had raised in June.
3. The chair *reiterated* to the room that questions would come at the end.
4. I'll *reiterate* it one last time so nobody can claim they weren't told.
5. The letter *reiterates* a demand the company has already refused twice.
6. Nothing was gained by *reiterating* a position everyone had already rejected.

**See also:** [reaffirm](#reaffirm), [stress](#stress), [emphasize](#emphasize), [insist](#insist), [maintain](#maintain)

### stipulate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈstɪpjəleɪt/ &middot; *STIP-yuh-layt*
**Type:** reporting verb — a condition laid down, or a fact agreed to &middot; **Register:** formal, legal and contractual
**Patterns:** *stipulate* + **that**-clause (subjunctive in the requirement sense, indicative in the agreement sense) &middot; *stipulate* + noun phrase &middot; *stipulate* + **to** + noun phrase (American legal: agree a fact need not be proved — *stipulated to the chain of custody*) &middot; *stipulate* + **for** + noun phrase (rare, formal)
**Never:** ***stipulate someone to do something*** and ***stipulate someone that*** — ✗ *The contract stipulated us to pay in advance*, ✗ *She stipulated the buyer that the roof was excluded*. Repairs: *stipulated that we pay in advance*, *stipulated that the roof was excluded*.
**Subjunctive:** when *stipulate* lays down a **requirement**, the *that*-clause takes the bare subjunctive, exactly as after [insist](#insist) and *demand* — *the lease stipulates that the deposit **be** paid before move-in*, not *that it is paid*. When it reports an **agreement about a fact**, the clause is an ordinary indicative one and backshifts normally: *the parties stipulated that the signature **was** genuine*.
**Reports:** two related acts. In contracts, setting out a term that binds someone. In American courtrooms, agreeing that a fact need not be proved, which saves everyone the trouble of proving it — *counsel stipulated to the chain of custody* means nobody will argue about it.

> "The deposit must be paid before move-in," the lease says.  →  The lease **stipulates that** the deposit **be paid** before move-in.
> "We agree the signature is genuine."  →  Both parties **stipulated that** the signature **was** genuine.

1. The grant *stipulates* that the funds be spent within eighteen months.
2. Her father's will *stipulated* a trust for each grandchild.
3. The defense *stipulated* that the video was authentic.
4. The agreement *stipulates* that neither side discuss the terms publicly.
5. He *stipulated* only one condition: that his name stay off the building.
6. Nothing in the policy *stipulates* a maximum number of claims per year.

**See also:** [insist](#insist), [submit](#submit), [declare](#declare), [hold](#hold), [propose](05-advise-suggest.md#propose)

### stress

**Pronunciation:** /strɛs/ &middot; *STRESS*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement given extra weight &middot; **Register:** neutral; very common in news writing
**Patterns:** *stress* + **that**-clause &middot; *stress* + noun phrase &middot; *stress* + **wh**-clause &middot; *stress* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***stress someone that*** and ***stress to do something*** — ✗ *She stressed me that the deadline was firm*, ✗ *He stressed to check the totals*. Repairs: *stressed to me that the deadline was firm*, *stressed the importance of checking the totals*. Note that ✗ *stress on* is wrong for the same reason ✗ *emphasize on* is: the verb takes a plain object, the noun takes *on* (*lay stress on the deadline*).
**Reports:** the same act as *emphasize*, one syllable shorter, and for that reason the newsroom default. It is **factive** — *he stressed that the vote was nonbinding* commits the writer to the vote being nonbinding — and it implies no resistance, which is what separates it from *insist*.

> "And I want to be clear about this: nothing has been decided," she said.  →  She **stressed that** nothing **had been** decided.
> "Above all, don't sign anything today."  →  He **stressed that** we **should** not **sign** anything **that day**.

1. The spokesman *stressed* that the recall covered only one production run.
2. She *stressed* how quickly the situation could change.
3. He *stressed* the importance of getting the paperwork in early.
4. The doctor *stressed* to both parents that the fever was not dangerous.
5. Every version of the memo *stressed* confidentiality.
6. She *stressed* that she was speaking for herself and not for the department.

**See also:** [emphasize](#emphasize), [underscore](#underscore), [underline](#underline), [insist](#insist), [reiterate](#reiterate)

### submit

**Pronunciation:** /səbˈmɪt/ &middot; *sub-MIT*
**Type:** reporting verb — a proposition advanced deferentially &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and academic
**Patterns:** *submit* + **that**-clause &middot; *submit* + **to** sb + **that**-clause (*submitted to the court that…*) &middot; *submit* + noun phrase (*submit a proposal, submit an explanation* — put forward for consideration; also the everyday sense, hand in) &middot; *submit* + **to** + noun phrase (a different sense: yield to)
**Never:** ***submit someone that*** and ***submit to do something*** in the assertion sense — ✗ *Counsel submitted the court that the statute applied*, ✗ *He submitted to reverse the ruling*. Repairs: *submitted to the court that the statute applied*, or simply *submitted that the statute applied*.
**Reports:** putting a proposition to somebody who has the authority to reject it. *I submit that…* is a hedge and a challenge at once: it defers in form while asserting in substance, which is why it belongs to advocates addressing a judge and scholars addressing a field. Outside those settings it sounds stiff or faintly pompous.

> "In our view, the clause has never applied here," counsel said.  →  Counsel **submitted that** the clause **had** never **applied**.
> "I'd argue the whole framework is misconceived."  →  She **submitted that** the framework **was** misconceived.

1. Counsel *submits* that the evidence was obtained without a warrant.
2. I *submit* that we have been solving the wrong problem for two years.
3. She *submitted* that the earlier reading of the statute could not survive.
4. He *submitted*, with respect, that the committee had exceeded its authority.
5. It is *submitted* that no other reading of the clause is available.
6. The paper *submits* a simpler explanation than the one currently accepted.

**See also:** [contend](#contend), [argue](#argue), [posit](#posit), [maintain](#maintain), [hold](#hold)

### swear

**Pronunciation:** /swɛr/ &middot; *SWAIR* — irregular: *swear, swore, sworn*
**Type:** reporting verb — a statement or promise made under oath, or with maximum personal warranty &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *swear* + **that**-clause &middot; *swear* + **to-infinitive** &middot; *swear* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *swear* + sb + **to** + noun phrase (*swore us to secrecy*) &middot; *swear* + sb + **in** (install by oath) &middot; *swear* + **to** + noun phrase (*swear to it*) &middot; *swear* + **on** + noun phrase &middot; *swear* + **at** sb (a different sense: curse)
**Never:** ***swear someone that*** — ✗ *He swore me that he'd been home*. The addressee needs *to*: ✓ *He swore to me that he'd been home*. Also ✗ *swear someone to do something* meaning "promise them" — *swear sb to* binds the other person, as in *sworn to secrecy*.
**Reports:** asserting with the strongest personal guarantee available, either formally under oath (*sworn testimony*) or informally as an emphatic denial (*I swear I didn't touch it*). In legal settings the alternative is [affirm](#affirm), for witnesses who decline to take an oath. The curse sense — *swear at someone* — is a separate verb sharing the same form; the preposition tells them apart.

> "I did not see the car at all," she said.  →  She **swore that** she **had** not **seen** the car.
> "I'll be there, whatever happens."  →  He **swore to be** there whatever **happened**.

1. He *swore* that the safe had been locked when he left.
2. She *swore* to tell the truth and then said almost nothing for an hour.
3. They *swore* us to secrecy before showing us the prototype.
4. I *swear* I put the keys on the hook.
5. He was *sworn* in as a judge on a Tuesday afternoon.
6. She'd *swear* to it in court if anyone asked her.

**See also:** [vow](#vow), [testify](#testify), [attest](#attest), [affirm](#affirm), [pledge](#pledge)

### testify

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtɛstəfaɪ/ &middot; *TESS-tuh-fye*
**Type:** reporting verb — sworn statement given in a proceeding &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Patterns:** *testify* + **that**-clause &middot; *testify* + **to** + noun phrase / **-ing** &middot; *testify* + **about/against/for** + noun phrase &middot; *testify* + **before** + noun phrase (*testified before the committee*)
**Never:** ***testify someone that***, ***testify to do something***, and ***testify to*** + a **that**-clause. ✗ *She testified the jury that the door was open*, ✗ *He testified to be present*, ✗ *He testified to that the door was open*. Repairs: *testified that the door was open*, *testified to the door's being open*, *testified that he had been present*.
**Reports:** giving evidence on the record, under oath, in a court or a hearing. The verb carries **no stance of its own** — it neither endorses nor doubts, because its whole point is the forum: what matters is that the statement was made where perjury is a crime. That neutrality is exactly why reporters use it. An inanimate subject shifts the sense to *attest*: *the wreckage testifies to the force of the impact*.

> "I was in the parking lot at the time," she said on the stand.  →  She **testified that** she **had been** in the parking lot at the time.
> "He handed me the envelope."  →  The witness **testified that** the defendant **had handed** him the envelope.

1. Three former employees *testified* that the logs had been altered.
2. She *testified* before the subcommittee for nearly five hours.
3. He refused to *testify* against his own brother.
4. The expert *testified* to the reliability of the test.
5. She was subpoenaed but never actually *testified*.
6. Nothing he *testified* to at trial matched his earlier statement to police.

**See also:** [attest](#attest), [swear](#swear), [affirm](#affirm), [allege](#allege), [maintain](#maintain)

### underline

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndərˈlaɪn/ &middot; *un-der-LYNE* — the noun keeps the stress on the first syllable: /ˈʌndərlaɪn/, *UN-der-lyne*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement given extra weight &middot; **Register:** neutral, but British-leaning; American writing prefers *underscore*
**Patterns:** *underline* + **that**-clause &middot; *underline* + noun phrase &middot; *underline* + **wh**-clause &middot; *underline* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***underline someone that*** and ***underline to do something*** — ✗ *He underlined us that the funding was temporary*, ✗ *She underlined to check the totals*. Repairs: *underlined to us that the funding was temporary*, *underlined the need to check the totals*.
**Reports:** drawing attention to something already said, exactly as *emphasize* does, by the metaphor of a pen under a line of text. Both *underline* and [underscore](#underscore) are current in American English, but American editors reach for *underscore* by a wide margin in the figurative sense and keep *underline* for what you literally do with a pen. Like the rest of the emphasis group it is **factive**.

> "And note that this is a one-time payment," she said.  →  She **underlined that** the payment **was** a one-time one.
> "The margins here are razor thin."  →  He **underlined** how thin the margins **were**.

1. She *underlined* that the arrangement was temporary.
2. The delay *underlines* how fragile the supply chain still is.
3. He *underlined* the words *no exceptions* twice in red ink.
4. Her resignation *underlined* the depth of the disagreement.
5. The report *underlines* a problem the board has known about for years.
6. Nothing in his answer *underlined* the urgency the memo had described.

**See also:** [underscore](#underscore), [emphasize](#emphasize), [stress](#stress), [reiterate](#reiterate)

### underscore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndərˈskɔr/ &middot; *un-der-SKOR* — the noun keeps the stress on the first syllable: /ˈʌndərskɔr/, *UN-der-skor*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement given extra weight &middot; **Register:** neutral, journalistic; the standard American choice
**Patterns:** *underscore* + **that**-clause &middot; *underscore* + noun phrase &middot; *underscore* + **wh**-clause &middot; *underscore* + **to** sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***underscore someone that*** and ***underscore to do something*** — ✗ *She underscored the committee that time was short*, ✗ *He underscored to move quickly*. Repairs: *underscored to the committee that time was short*, *underscored the need to move quickly*.
**Reports:** the American twin of *underline*, and the more idiomatic of the two in figurative use. It works with a speaker (*she underscored that the vote was advisory*) but is especially at home with an **inanimate subject**, where an event rather than a person does the emphasizing: *the outage underscored how little redundancy the system had*. Factive, like the rest of the group.

> "This is exactly the kind of failure we warned about," she said.  →  She **underscored that** it **was** the kind of failure they **had warned** about.
> "The numbers are worse than last quarter."  →  He **underscored that** the numbers **were** worse than the previous quarter's.

1. She *underscored* that the vote was advisory and nothing more.
2. The outage *underscored* how little redundancy the network had.
3. He *underscored* the importance of documenting every change.
4. The two resignations *underscore* a problem the board has ignored.
5. Her opening remarks *underscored* what the written report only implied.
6. Nothing in the briefing *underscored* the cost, which is what everyone wanted to hear about.

**See also:** [underline](#underline), [emphasize](#emphasize), [stress](#stress), [reaffirm](#reaffirm)

### vow

**Pronunciation:** /vaʊ/ &middot; *VOW*
**Type:** reporting verb — a solemn personal promise &middot; **Register:** formal; a journalistic favorite
**Patterns:** *vow* + **to-infinitive** &middot; *vow* + **that**-clause &middot; *vow* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *vow* + noun phrase (*vowed revenge*)
**Never:** ***vow someone to do something*** and ***vow someone that*** — ✗ *He vowed us to return*, ✗ *She vowed me that she'd appeal*. There is no indirect object: repairs are *vowed to return*, *vowed to me that she would appeal*, or *promised me she would appeal*.
**Reports:** a promise made with personal solemnity and, usually, some defiance. It is the headline writer's verb of choice because it is short and dramatic (*Mayor vows to appeal*), and it differs from [pledge](#pledge) in being personal rather than institutional and from [swear](#swear) in requiring no oath. Do not confuse it with *avow*, which is an admission, not a promise.

> "I will fight this to the last court there is," he said.  →  He **vowed to fight** it to the last court there **was**.
> "We'll be back next season."  →  They **vowed that** they **would be** back the **following** season.

1. She *vowed* to appeal the ruling within the week.
2. He *vowed* that no one would be evicted while he was in office.
3. The company *vowed* revenge in the trade press and then quietly settled.
4. They exchanged *vows* in front of about forty people.
5. He *vowed* never to set foot in the building again.
6. Nobody who *vowed* to read the whole report actually did.

**See also:** [swear](#swear), [pledge](#pledge), [avow](#avow), [promise](03-agree-refuse.md#promise)
