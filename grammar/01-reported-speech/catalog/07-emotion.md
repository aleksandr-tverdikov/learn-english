# Reporting with attitude: complaining, boasting, apologizing

> The reporting verbs that carry a feeling — the ones that tell you not just what was said but how the speaker felt about saying it, and which almost all demand a preposition before the person or the offense.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

Every verb in this file reports a speech act with an emotion welded on. *Say* is neutral; *complain*, *boast*, *apologize*, *accuse*, *sneer at* are not — choosing one commits the reporter to a judgment about the speaker, which is exactly why they are so useful in a summary and so dangerous in a neutral report. Two grammatical facts run through the whole group. First, most of these verbs reach the person and the offense through **a fixed preposition** (*accuse sb **of**, blame sb **for**, congratulate sb **on**, apologize **to** sb **for**, thank sb **for**, complain **to** sb **about**), and the preposition is not negotiable — it is memorized with the verb. Second, the manner-of-speaking verbs at the end (*whisper, mutter, snap, sigh*) split into those that can carry a full *that*-clause and those that really only introduce a direct quotation, and getting that split wrong produces sentences that are grammatical on paper and wrong in the ear.

A third pattern is worth naming up front: with almost none of these verbs can the person be a bare object of the verb plus a clause. ***He complained me that…***, ***She apologized me***, ***They boasted us that…*** are all impossible. The person is reached through *to* (*complained to me*, *apologized to me*), or the verb takes the person directly and the content through a preposition (*accused me of it*, *thanked me for it*) — never both as bare objects.

## The preposition map

This table is the file in miniature. Learn the frame, not the verb.

| Verb | Frame | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *accuse* | sb **of** + -ing / noun | accused her **of** taking the money |
| *apologize* | **to** sb **for** + -ing / noun | apologized **to** me **for** being late |
| *blame* | sb **for** + -ing &middot; sth **on** sb | blamed me **for** the delay &middot; blamed the delay **on** me |
| *boast*, *brag* | **about** + -ing / noun &middot; **of** + -ing / noun (formal) &middot; **that**-clause | bragged **about** winning &middot; boasted **of** a record year |
| *chide*, *rebuke*, *reproach*, *scold* | sb **for** + -ing | scolded him **for** slamming the door |
| *complain*, *gripe*, *grumble*, *moan*, *whine* | **to** sb **about** sth &middot; **that**-clause | complained **to** the manager **about** the noise |
| *compliment* | sb **on** + noun / -ing | complimented her **on** her presentation |
| *condemn*, *criticize* | sb **for** + -ing &middot; sth **as** + noun | criticized the mayor **for** stalling |
| *congratulate* | sb **on** + noun / -ing | congratulated me **on** the promotion |
| *crow*, *gloat* | **about** / **over** sth &middot; **that**-clause | gloated **over** the result &middot; crowed **that** they'd won |
| *joke* | **about** sth &middot; **with** sb &middot; **that**-clause | joked **with** me **about** the wait |
| *lament* | no preposition — plain object | lamented the closing of the plant |
| *laugh* | **at** sb (mockery) &middot; **about** sth (shared) | laughed **at** him &middot; laughed **about** it together |
| *mock* | sb (**for** + -ing) | mocked him **for** trying |
| *object* | **to** + noun / **-ing** | objected **to** being questioned |
| *praise* | sb **for** + -ing &middot; sb **as** + noun | praised her **for** speaking up |
| *protest* | sth (American) &middot; **against** sth (British) &middot; **to** sb | protested the ruling &middot; protested **against** the war |
| *taunt* | sb **about** / **with** sth | taunted him **with** the score |
| *tease* | sb **about** / **for** sth | teased him **about** his accent |
| *thank* | sb **for** + -ing / noun | thanked me **for** waiting |

Four traps live in that table. *Object to* is a **preposition**, so the verb after it is *-ing*, never an infinitive: *objected to **being** searched*, never ***objected to be searched***. *Blame* runs both directions with different prepositions — *blame the person **for** the thing*, *blame the thing **on** the person* — while *accuse* only ever takes **of**: ***accused him for lying*** and ***blamed him of the delay*** are the two halves of the same mistake. *Gloat* and *crow* take **over**, not **of** — ***gloated of the result*** is not English, though *boasted of the result* is. And *lament* is on the list precisely because it takes **no** preposition: ***lamented about the closing*** is the error the table is there to prevent.

## Which manner-of-speaking verbs take a *that*-clause

A verb that names a **noise** rather than a **saying** resists a *that*-clause, because the noise is not literally a way of producing words. Fiction ignores this freely; careful nonfiction does not. Here is where each verb actually stands in American English.

| Verb | Direct quote | *that*-clause | *at* sb | *to* sb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *shout* | yes | yes | yes — angry | yes — across distance |
| *yell* | yes | yes | yes — angry | yes — across distance |
| *whisper* | yes | yes | no | yes |
| *mutter* | yes | yes | marginal | yes (*to himself*) |
| *mumble* | yes | yes | no | yes |
| *murmur* | yes | yes | no | yes |
| *snap* | yes | yes | yes | no |
| *growl* | yes | yes | yes | marginal |
| *exclaim* | yes | yes | *at / over* = react to | yes |
| *cry* | yes | literary only | no | *cry out to* |
| *groan* | yes | yes | *at* = react to | yes |
| *moan* | yes | yes | British only | yes |
| *whine* | yes | yes | yes — of dogs and children | yes |
| *sigh* | yes | fiction only | no | marginal |
| *laugh* | yes | disputed | yes — mocking | no |
| *sneer* | yes | yes | yes — contempt | no |

The *cry*, *sigh*, and *laugh* rows are the honest part. *"I forgot," she sighed* and *She sighed that she had forgotten* are both printed constantly, and both are also flagged by copy editors on the grounds that you cannot sigh a sentence. In a novel, use them. In a report, write *she said with a sigh*.

Note what the *at* column is doing, because it is not one thing. For *shout*, *yell*, *snap*, and *growl*, *at* marks the **target of anger**. For *laugh* and *sneer* it marks the **target of contempt**. For *exclaim* and *groan* it marks the **thing reacted to** — *groaned at the price*, not at a person. For *moan* (British) it marks the person nagged at, and for *whine* it is mostly literal noise directed at a door or a parent. Only the first two kinds report a speech act aimed at a hearer, which is why *shouted at me* and *groaned at the price* cannot be reported the same way.

## What these verbs presuppose

A reporting verb can smuggle in a claim the speaker never made. The grammar of the sentence is about the speech act; the **presupposition** is about the reporter's own position, and it survives negation — *He did not gloat that he had won* still concedes that he won. Choose from this column deliberately, because a reader will hold you to it.

| Verb | What the reporter commits to |
|---|---|
| *accuse* | that the accuser believed the offense real — the reporter does not vouch for it |
| *apologize* | **fault**: you can only apologize for something treated as yours and wrong |
| *blame* | that the outcome was bad and that someone is answerable for it |
| *boast*, *brag*, *crow* | that the claim was made with self-satisfaction; *brag* and *crow* add disapproval |
| *gloat* | that someone **lost** — there is no gloating without a defeat to enjoy |
| *condemn* | a moral verdict, the reporter's or the speaker's, depending on the subject |
| *joke* | that the words were **not meant literally** — *he said he'd quit* and *he joked that he'd quit* report the same words and opposite situations |
| *lament* | that the thing is lost and beyond remedy |
| *mock*, *taunt* | hostility; *tease* instead presupposes affection |
| *protest* | a hint that the objection may not be believed — *he protested his innocence* is never quite neutral |
| *scold* | an authority gap; using it of two adults infantilizes one of them |
| *sneer* | contempt on the speaker's part, and the reporter's decision to show it |
| *whine* | that the complaint is **unwarranted** — there is no neutral use |

The same trap runs through the neighboring files, and it is worth carrying the list across: [claim](02-assert.md#claim) and [allege](02-assert.md#allege) signal the *writer's* doubt about what was said; [admit](04-admit-deny.md#admit) and [confess](04-admit-deny.md#confess) presuppose fault the way *apologize* does; and [point out](08-ask-explain.md#point-out) presupposes that the thing pointed out is **true**, so *she pointed out that the numbers were wrong* endorses her. When a report must stay neutral, the safe verbs are [say](01-neutral.md#say), [tell](01-neutral.md#tell), and *state* — every verb in this file editorializes by design.

## The dictionary

### accuse

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkjuz/ &middot; *uh-KYOOZ*
**Русский:** обвинять — вменять кому-то проступок; accuse sb of sth = обвинять кого-то в чём-то
**Type:** reporting verb — accusation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *accuse* + **sb** &middot; *accuse* + **sb** + *of* + **-ing** &middot; *accuse* + **sb** + *of* + **noun phrase** &middot; *accuse* + **sb** + *of* + **having done sth** &middot; passive *be accused of*
**Never:** ***accuse someone that they did something***, ***accuse someone for doing something***, and ***accuse someone to have done something***. *Accuse* takes no *that*-clause at all, no infinitive, and never *for* — the preposition is *of*, and what follows it is a gerund or a noun. Repairs: *accused him **of** stealing it*, *accused him **of** theft*. To get a clause in, change the verb: *alleged that he stole it*, *said he had stolen it*.
**Perfect gerund:** for an offense clearly finished before the accusation, *of having done* is available and slightly more formal — *accused her of having falsified the records*. Plain *-ing* covers the same ground and is commoner.
**Reports:** a charge of wrongdoing laid at a named person, and it presupposes that the accuser believes the offense real. Compare *blame*, which assigns responsibility for an outcome without necessarily alleging misconduct, and *criticize*, which faults a choice rather than charging an offense.

> "You took the money," she said to him.  →  She **accused him of taking** the money.
> "You've been lying to me for months."  →  She **accused him of lying** to her for months.

1. She *accused* her landlord of ignoring three written repair requests.
2. He *accused* me of leaking the memo, which I hadn't.
3. Two former employees have *accused* the company of retaliation.
4. Nobody was *accusing* anyone of anything — they just wanted the numbers explained.
5. "Don't *accuse* me of something you can't prove," he said.
6. The report stops short of *accusing* the department of misconduct.

**See also:** [blame](#blame), [criticize](#criticize), [condemn](#condemn), [reproach](#reproach)

### apologise

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑləˌdʒaɪz/ &middot; *uh-PAH-luh-jyze*
**Русский:** извиняться — брит. написание apologize; в американском тексте только apologize
**Type:** reporting verb — apology &middot; **Register:** neutral — **British spelling**
**Patterns:** identical to [apologize](#apologize) — *apologise* (intransitive) &middot; *apologise* + *to* **sb** &middot; *apologise* + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *apologise* + *for* + **noun phrase** &middot; *apologise* + *to* **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase**
**British spelling:** *apologise* is the standard form in British, Irish, and Australian writing; *apologize* is required in American English and is also perfectly standard in Britain, where Oxford house style prefers it. The two are pronounced exactly alike — the *s* is /z/ either way, so nothing in the sound tells you which spelling a speaker has in mind. The same *-ise/-ize* split runs through [criticise](#criticise)/[criticize](#criticize), *recognise/recognize*, and *organise/organize*.
**Never:** ***apologise me*** or ***apologise him for the delay*** — the grammar is *apologize*'s in every detail, so the person is reached only through *to*. And in an American text, never ***apologise*** at all: this library is American throughout, so every other example in this file uses *apologize*. If you are writing for a British publication, flip all of them.
**Reports:** the same speech act as *apologize* — nothing about the meaning or the grammar changes with the spelling.

> "Sorry I'm late," he said.  →  He **apologised for being** late. *(British)*
> "Sorry I'm late," he said.  →  He **apologized for being** late. *(American)*

1. The minister *apologised* to the families for the delay in the inquiry. *(British press)*
2. Our style guide uses *apologise*, so the American draft had to be respelled throughout.
3. She *apologised* for the confusion and reissued the invoice. *(British)*
4. Both *apologise* and *apologize* appear in the OED; only one appears in Merriam-Webster's main entry.
5. He never learned which spelling his company used, so he wrote *I'm sorry* instead.
6. The autocorrect kept changing *apologise* to *apologize*, which told him what dictionary it was using.

**See also:** [apologize](#apologize), [criticise](#criticise), [criticize](#criticize)

### apologize

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑləˌdʒaɪz/ &middot; *uh-PAH-luh-jyze*
**Русский:** извиняться, приносить извинения — сожалеть о своём поступке: to sb for sth
**Type:** reporting verb — apology &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *apologize* (intransitive) &middot; *apologize* + *to* **sb** &middot; *apologize* + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *apologize* + *for* + **noun phrase** &middot; *apologize* + *to* **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; *apologize* + **that**-clause (institutional only, see below)
**Never:** ***apologize me***, ***apologize him for the delay***, and ***apologize about the delay***. The person apologized to is reached only through *to*, and *to* comes before *for*: *apologized **to** the customer **for** the delay*, never ***apologized the customer for the delay***. The order is fixed — ***apologized for the delay to the customer*** is grammatical but sounds like an afterthought. The offense takes *for* and only *for*; *about* belongs to [complain](#complain), not here.
**The *that*-clause:** *We apologize that your order was delayed* is now everywhere in corporate and customer-service English. It is not an error, but it is a register marker: no one says it in conversation. In careful writing use *apologize for* + noun (*apologize for the delay*) or drop the verb entirely (*we're sorry your order was delayed*).
**Reports:** an expression of regret for one's own act, which presupposes fault the way *admit* and *confess* do. It is not the same as expressing sympathy — you apologize for what you did, and you say *I'm sorry* for what merely happened.

> "I'm sorry I snapped at you," he said.  →  He **apologized for snapping** at me.
> "We're sorry, the flight is overbooked."  →  They **apologized to us for overbooking** the flight.

1. He *apologized* to the whole team for missing the deadline.
2. She *apologized* for the confusion and sent a corrected schedule.
3. I'm not going to *apologize* for wanting the contract in writing.
4. The airline *apologized* profusely and gave everyone a voucher.
5. "You could at least *apologize*," she said, and he did.
6. He *apologized* to her, not to me, which was the whole problem.

**See also:** [apologise](#apologise), [thank](#thank), [blame](#blame), [reproach](#reproach)

### blame

**Pronunciation:** /bleɪm/ &middot; *BLAYM*
**Русский:** винить, возлагать вину — за плохой исход: blame sb for sth / blame sth on sb
**Type:** reporting verb — accusation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *blame* + **sb** &middot; *blame* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *blame* + **sb** + *for* + **noun phrase** &middot; *blame* + **oneself** + *for* &middot; *blame* + **sth** + *on* + **sb** &middot; *blame* + **noun phrase** (*blame the weather*) &middot; *be to blame (for)*
**Never:** ***blame someone of something*** — that is *accuse*'s preposition, not *blame*'s — and ***blame someone about something***, which is nobody's. And no *that*-clause: ***He blamed that the driver was speeding*** is impossible. Repairs: *blamed the driver **for** speeding*, *blamed the crash **on** the driver*, *said the driver was speeding*.
**Two directions, two prepositions:** the person takes *for*, the outcome takes *on*. *She blamed **me for** the leak* and *She blamed **the leak on** me* say the same thing with the arguments reversed. Mixing them — ***blamed me on the leak*** — reverses the meaning into nonsense.
**Reports:** the assignment of responsibility for something bad. Unlike [accuse](#accuse), it need allege no misconduct — you can blame the weather, the software, or bad luck — and unlike [criticize](#criticize), it targets an outcome rather than a judgment call.

> "It's your fault the server went down," she said.  →  She **blamed me for** the outage. / She **blamed the outage on me**.
> "The vendor missed the deadline, not us."  →  He **blamed the delay on** the vendor.

1. Don't *blame* the intern — nobody told her the file was live.
2. He *blamed* the loss on a single bad call in the third quarter.
3. She *blamed* herself for not catching the typo before it printed.
4. I don't *blame* you for walking out of that meeting.
5. The report *blames* poor maintenance for the failure.
6. Everyone knew who was to *blame*, and nobody said it out loud.

**See also:** [accuse](#accuse), [criticize](#criticize), [condemn](#condemn), [reproach](#reproach)

### boast

**Pronunciation:** /boʊst/ &middot; *BOHST*
**Русский:** хвастаться, похваляться — заявлять с гордостью; о предмете — «располагать чем-л.»
**Type:** reporting verb — self-praise &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *boast* + **that**-clause &middot; *boast* + *about* + **-ing** / noun &middot; *boast* + *of* + **-ing** / noun &middot; *boast* + *to* **sb** + *that* / *about* &middot; *boast* + **noun phrase** (only in the "possess proudly" sense) &middot; + direct quotation
**Never:** ***boast someone that*** and ***boast to do something***. The listener needs *to*: *boasted **to** his brother that he'd won*. There is no infinitive frame — ***He boasted to have won*** is not English.
**The inanimate use:** *boast* alone among this group takes a plain noun-phrase object when the subject is a thing, and there it means "possess as a point of pride," with no speech at all: *The hotel boasts three restaurants and a rooftop pool*. This is a real and common sense; it just is not a reporting verb.
**Reports:** a claim advanced with self-satisfaction. *Boast* is the least loaded of the four self-praise verbs — it can be neutral or even admiring (*boasts a spotless record*) — where [brag](#brag) is informal and disapproving, [crow](#crow) is loud, and [gloat](#gloat) requires someone else's loss.

> "I've never lost a case," he said.  →  He **boasted that** he had never lost a case.
> "My kid got into Stanford."  →  She **boasted about** her son **getting** into Stanford.

1. He *boasted* that he could fix the transmission in an afternoon.
2. She never *boasts* about the awards; you have to find out from someone else.
3. They *boasted* of a turnaround time no competitor could match.
4. The résumé *boasted* fifteen years of experience she did not have.
5. "I *boast*," he admitted, "but only about things I've actually done."
6. The new terminal *boasts* the shortest security lines in the region.

**See also:** [brag](#brag), [crow](#crow), [gloat](#gloat), [praise](#praise)

### brag

**Pronunciation:** /bræɡ/ &middot; *BRAG*
**Русский:** хвастаться, бахвалиться — разг., неодобр.; то же, что boast, но с осуждением
**Type:** reporting verb — self-praise &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *brag* + **that**-clause &middot; *brag* + *about* + **-ing** / noun &middot; *brag* + *of* + **-ing** / noun (much rarer than with [boast](#boast)) &middot; *brag* + *to* **sb** + *about* / *that* &middot; + direct quotation
**Never:** ***brag something*** — no plain noun-phrase object, so no ***He bragged his salary***. And no inanimate subject in the "possess" sense: a hotel *boasts* three pools, it does not ***brag*** them. Repairs: *bragged **about** his salary*, *bragged **that** he made six figures*.
**Spelling:** doubles the *g* — *bragged*, *bragging*.
**Reports:** the same act as [boast](#boast) but with the disapproval built in. Calling it bragging is itself a judgment; no one describes their own speech that way except jokingly (*not to brag, but…*), which is the standard American hedge for bragging anyway.

> "I closed four deals this week," he said.  →  He **bragged that** he had closed four deals that week.
> "Nobody here can beat my time."  →  He **bragged about** his time.

1. He *bragged* that he'd never once been late to work.
2. Stop *bragging* about the truck — everyone has seen it.
3. She *bragged* to her sister about getting the corner office.
4. Not to *brag*, but I fixed the sink myself.
5. The kids *bragged* all afternoon about who caught the bigger fish.
6. He *brags* less now that people know what the numbers actually were.

**See also:** [boast](#boast), [crow](#crow), [gloat](#gloat)

### chide

**Pronunciation:** /tʃaɪd/ &middot; *CHYDE*
**Русский:** журить, попрекать — книжн., устар. оттенок; мягкий, часто ласковый упрёк
**Type:** reporting verb — reproof &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *chide* + **sb** &middot; *chide* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; + direct quotation (*"You promised," she chided*) &middot; passive *be chided for*
**Never:** ***chide someone that they did something***, ***chide someone to do something***, and ***chide at someone***. No *that*-clause, no infinitive, no preposition before the person. Repairs: *chided him **for** forgetting*, *told him he had forgotten*.
**Past tense:** *chided* in modern American English. The old forms *chid* and *chidden* survive only in older texts and are not usable now.
**Reports:** a mild, often affectionate rebuke — the gentlest verb in the [scold](#scold)–[rebuke](#rebuke)–[reproach](#reproach) family, and the only one that can be teasing. It reads as bookish; in speech an American would say *gave him a hard time* or *ribbed him*.

> "You really should have called," she said.  →  She **chided him for** not calling.
> "Manners, please."  →  She **chided** the children gently.

1. She *chided* him for showing up empty-handed, but she was smiling.
2. The columnist *chided* the mayor for reading a statement instead of answering.
3. "You're impossible," she *chided*, and handed him the umbrella anyway.
4. He was *chided* more than punished, which suited him fine.
5. Her grandmother *chided* her for speaking English at the dinner table.
6. The editorial *chided* both parties for walking away from the table.

**See also:** [scold](#scold), [rebuke](#rebuke), [reproach](#reproach), [tease](#tease)

### complain

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈpleɪn/ &middot; *kuhm-PLAYN*
**Русский:** жаловаться — на неудобство: to sb about sth; complain of — о симптомах, недомогании
**Type:** reporting verb — complaint &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *complain* + **that**-clause &middot; *complain* + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *complain* + *of* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *complain* + *to* **sb** + *about* / *of* / *that* &middot; *complain* (intransitive) &middot; + direct quotation
**Never:** ***complain me*** or ***complain the manager about the noise***. There is no personal object — the hearer is reached through *to*, and *to* comes first: *complained **to** the manager **about** the noise*. Also ***complain something***: the content never appears as a bare noun phrase, so no ***She complained the noise***. And never both markers at once — ***complained about that the food was cold***; it is either *complained **about** the cold food* or *complained **that** the food was cold*.
**of vs. about:** **complain of** is for symptoms, ailments, and formal reports of a condition — *complained of chest pains*, *complained of dizziness*. **Complain about** is the everyday grievance — *complained about the noise, the food, her boss*. Using *of* for a grievance sounds archaic; using *about* for a symptom sounds casual but is not wrong.
**Reports:** a statement of dissatisfaction, usually addressed to someone who might fix it — which is what separates it from [grumble](#grumble) and [gripe](#gripe), where nobody expects redress.

> "The heat hasn't worked in three days," she said.  →  She **complained that** the heat **hadn't** worked in three days.
> "This coffee is cold," he said to the server.  →  He **complained to** the server **about** the cold coffee.

1. Three tenants *complained* that the elevator had been out since Friday.
2. He *complained* to HR about the scheduling, and nothing changed.
3. She came in *complaining* of a headache and a stiff neck.
4. Passengers *complained* about being kept on the tarmac for two hours.
5. "I'm not *complaining*," he said, complaining.
6. Nobody *complained*, which the manager took as approval.

**See also:** [gripe](#gripe), [grumble](#grumble), [moan](#moan), [whine](#whine), [object](#object), [protest](#protest)

### compliment

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑmpləˌmɛnt/ &middot; *KAHM-pluh-ment*
**Русский:** делать комплимент, хвалить в глаза — compliment sb on sth; не путать с complement
**Type:** reporting verb — praise &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *compliment* + **sb** &middot; *compliment* + **sb** + *on* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; passive *be complimented on*
**Never:** ***compliment someone that they did well*** — no *that*-clause. And the preposition is *on*, not *for*: ***complimented her for her presentation*** is heard, but *on* is the standard frame and *for* belongs to [praise](#praise) and [thank](#thank). Repair: *complimented her **on** her presentation*.
**Homophone:** *compliment* (praise) and *complement* (complete, go well with) are pronounced identically and mean nothing alike. *The wine complements the fish*; *the chef complimented the sommelier*. This is one of the most common spelling errors in American English.
**Reports:** a short, direct expression of admiration said to the person's face. [Praise](#praise) can be public and about a third party; a compliment is addressed to its target.

> "That was a great presentation," he said to her.  →  He **complimented her on** her presentation.
> "I love your jacket."  →  She **complimented** me **on** my jacket.

1. He *complimented* her on the way she handled the hostile question.
2. Nobody had ever *complimented* his handwriting before.
3. She *complimented* the cook and asked for the recipe.
4. I was *complimented* on my Spanish by someone who had heard four words of it.
5. "Was that a *compliment*?" "It was supposed to be."
6. He *complimented* the team publicly and criticized them in private.

### condemn

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈdɛm/ &middot; *kuhn-DEM* — the final *n* is silent
**Русский:** осуждать, клеймить — публично объявлять недопустимым; отд. знач. «приговаривать»
**Type:** reporting verb — denunciation &middot; **Register:** formal, journalistic
**Patterns:** *condemn* + **noun phrase** &middot; *condemn* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; *condemn* + **sb** / **sth** + *as* + **noun phrase** / adjective &middot; passive *be condemned (as / for)* &middot; *condemn* + **sb** + *to* + **noun phrase** (= sentence — a separate sense)
**Never:** ***condemn that the policy was unfair*** — no *that*-clause — and ***condemn someone of something***, which is [accuse](#accuse)'s preposition. Repairs: *condemned the policy **as** unfair*, *condemned the policy*, *said the policy was unfair*. Also note that *condemn sb to* + noun means "sentence" (*condemned to death*), a different verb sense entirely, so ***condemned him to lie*** does not mean he was denounced for lying — and there is no infinitive frame in the denouncing sense at all.
**Spelling:** *condemned*, *condemning* — the silent *n* stays in every form.
**Reports:** the strongest public denunciation in this group. Where [criticize](#criticize) faults a decision and [accuse](#accuse) charges an offense, *condemn* declares the thing morally unacceptable, which is why it is the standard verb of diplomatic statements.

> "This attack is unacceptable and we reject it," the statement said.  →  The government **condemned** the attack.
> "What the company did was indefensible."  →  She **condemned** the company's conduct **as** indefensible.

1. Both senators *condemned* the ruling within the hour.
2. The union *condemned* the layoffs as a breach of the agreement.
3. She *condemned* the practice for putting drivers at risk.
4. The report stops at describing the failures; it does not *condemn* anyone.
5. "I *condemn* it in the strongest possible terms," he said, which meant nothing would happen.
6. The building was *condemned*, in the other sense, six months later.

**Русский:** поздравлять — поздравляют человека (sb on sth), а не событие
**Type:** reporting verb — congratulation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *congratulate* + **sb** &middot; *congratulate* + **sb** + *on* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *congratulate* + **oneself** + *on* &middot; passive *be congratulated on*
**Never:** ***congratulate your promotion*** — you congratulate a **person**, never the event. The event goes after *on*: *congratulated her **on** her promotion*. And no *that*-clause: ***congratulated me that I had passed*** is impossible; write *congratulated me **on** passing*.
**on vs. for:** *on* is the standard preposition and the only safe one. *Congratulate sb for* + -ing occurs in American speech and is not exactly wrong, but it shades toward praise for an effort rather than acknowledgment of an outcome, and editors change it to *on*.

**Reports:** an acknowledgment of someone's good fortune or achievement. It does not evaluate the person's work, which is what separates it from [praise](#praise) and [compliment](#compliment) — you can congratulate someone on a lottery win.

> "Congratulations on the new job!" she said.  →  She **congratulated me on** the new job.
> "You did it — you passed the bar."  →  He **congratulated her on** passing the bar.

1. She *congratulated* the whole team on shipping two weeks early.
2. He *congratulated* his opponent and left the stage without another word.
3. I *congratulated* her on the baby before I realized she hadn't announced it.
4. They *congratulated* themselves on a decision that had mostly been luck.
5. "Am I supposed to *congratulate* you?" she asked, not warmly.
6. The email *congratulated* everyone on numbers that three people had actually produced.

**Never:** ***criticize about the delay*** and ***criticize that the plan was rushed***. ### cry

### exclaim

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskleɪm/ &middot; *ik-SKLAYM*
**Русский:** восклицать — книжн.; внезапный возглас от удивления или восторга, не обязательно громкий
**Type:** reporting verb — exclamation &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *exclaim* + direct quotation &middot; *exclaim* + **that**-clause &middot; *exclaim* + *to* **sb** + *that* &middot; *exclaim* + *over* / *at* + **noun phrase** / **wh**-clause (= react admiringly or with dismay)
**Never:** ***exclaim someone that*** and ***exclaim something***. The listener needs *to*; there is no noun-phrase object, so no ***She exclaimed her surprise***. Repair: *exclaimed **that** she was surprised*.
**Register warning:** almost nobody says *exclaim* out loud. 4. "*Exclaimed*" appears eleven times in the first chapter, which is nine too many.
5. She *exclaimed* at the price and bought it anyway.
6. "At last!" *exclaimed* the letter, in capitals, with three exclamation points.

> "I only married him for the parking spot."  →  She **joked about** marrying him for the parking spot.

1. She *joked* that the coffee machine had a better attendance record than she did.
2. He *joked* about his accent before anyone else could.
3. They *joked* with the security guard while the alarm was being reset.
4. I was *joking*, and about half the room knew it.
5. You can't *joke* about that in a deposition.
6. He *joked* that he'd be fine, which is how everyone missed how bad it was.

**See also:** [tease](#tease), [laugh](#laugh), [mock](#mock)

### lament

**Pronunciation:** /ləˈmɛnt/ &middot; *luh-MENT*
**Русский:** сетовать, оплакивать — книжн.; горевать о непоправимом; переходный, без about
**Type:** reporting verb — complaint, grief &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *lament* + **that**-clause &middot; *lament* + **noun phrase** &middot; *lament* + *the fact that* &middot; *lament* + *over* / *for* + **noun phrase** (literary, = mourn) &middot; *lament* (intransitive) &middot; + direct quotation
**Never:** ***lament about something*** — the verb is transitive and *about* is redundant. Repairs: *lamented the closing of the plant*, *lamented **that** the plant was closing*. (*Lament over the ruins* is a genuine literary variant; *lament about* is not one.) Also no personal object — ***lamented me that*** is impossible — and no infinitive: ***lamented to have lost the house*** should be *lamented **that** he had lost the house*.
**Reports:** grief or regret expressed publicly, usually about something already lost and beyond fixing. That is the difference from [complain](#complain): a complaint asks for a remedy, a lament concedes there is none. It is a written verb — in speech Americans say *was upset about* or *kept saying how sad it was*.

> "There's nowhere left in this town to buy a book," she said.  →  She **lamented that** there was nowhere left in town to buy a book.
> "We've lost something we can't get back."  →  He **lamented** the loss.

1. The editorial *laments* the disappearance of the local paper it competes with.
2. She *lamented* that none of her students had read a newspaper that week.
3. He *lamented* the state of the roads to anyone who would listen.
4. The song *laments* a river that no longer runs.
5. Critics *lamented* the ending and praised everything before it.
6. There is no point *lamenting* a deadline you already missed.

**See also:** [moan](#moan), [complain](#complain), [sigh](#sigh), [groan](#groan)

### laugh

**Pronunciation:** /læf/ &middot; *LAF*
**Русский:** смеяться — laugh at sb = насмехаться над кем-то, laugh about sth = смеяться вместе
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *laugh* (intransitive) &middot; *laugh* + direct quotation &middot; *laugh* + *at* + **sb** / **sth** &middot; *laugh* + *about* / *over* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *laugh* + *off* + **noun phrase** &middot; *laugh* + **that**-clause (disputed — see below)
**Never:** ***laugh someone*** and ***laugh someone for doing something***. There is no ordinary transitive frame: the person laughed at takes *at*, and the only objects the verb allows are fixed idioms (*laugh it off*, *laughed him off the stage*, *laughed herself sick*). Repairs: *laughed **at** him*, *mocked him **for** trying*.
**at vs. about:** **laugh at** someone is mockery, with a victim; **laugh about** something is shared amusement, with none. *They laughed at her* and *They laughed about it with her* are opposite social events.
**The *that*-clause is contested:** *He laughed that the whole thing had been a misunderstanding* appears regularly in journalism and is regularly flagged, on the grounds that laughing is not a way of producing words. It is common enough that you will not be misunderstood, but *he said, laughing,* is unarguable and no longer.
**Reports:** amusement, real or performed. As a reporting verb it usually says how something was said rather than what act it was.

> "It's fine, honestly," she said, laughing.  →  She **laughed** and said it was fine.
> "You actually believed that?"  →  He **laughed at** me **for** believing it.

1. She *laughed* when she saw the size of the invoice.
2. They *laughed* about the wrong turn for the rest of the drive.
3. Don't *laugh* — I got the last two parking spots on the block.
4. He *laughed* off a question he should have answered.
5. Nobody *laughed*, which told him the joke had landed wrong.
6. "You'll *laugh*," she said, and we did not.

**See also:** [joke](#joke), [tease](#tease), [mock](#mock), [sneer](#sneer), [sigh](#sigh)

### moan

**Pronunciation:** /moʊn/ &middot; *MOHN*
**Русский:** стонать; ныть, жаловаться — разг.; в амер. чаще о звуке, в брит. — о жалобах
**Type:** reporting verb — complaint, manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *moan* + **that**-clause &middot; *moan* + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *moan* + *to* **sb** + *about* / *that* &middot; *moan* + direct quotation &middot; *moan* (intransitive) &middot; *moan* + *at* **sb** (British)
**Never:** ***moan someone*** and ***moan something***. No object without a preposition, and no personal object: ***moaned me about the heat*** should be *moaned **to** me **about** the heat*.
**American vs. British:** in **British** English *moan* is a top-tier complaining verb — *stop moaning*, *having a moan* — and it competes with *complain* in ordinary conversation. In **American** English the complaining sense exists and is understood, but the dominant reading is the **sound**: pain, exhaustion, or pleasure. *She moaned about the heat* is fine in American English; *stop your moaning* sounds imported. Americans reach for [gripe](#gripe), [whine](#whine), or [complain](#complain) first.
**Reports:** a long low sound, or the complaint delivered in that tone. Softer and more drawn out than [groan](#groan), which is shorter and lower.

> "It's so hot in here I can't think," she said.  →  She **moaned about** the heat.
> "Nobody ever tells me anything."  →  He **moaned that** nobody ever told him anything.

1. He *moaned* about the commute until he moved four blocks from the office.
2. She *moaned* that she'd been on hold for forty minutes.
3. The wind *moaned* in the chimney all night.
4. Stop *moaning* — you picked the restaurant.
5. He *moaned* softly and asked for more ice.
6. There's no point *moaning* about a rule you voted for.

**See also:** [groan](#groan), [complain](#complain), [gripe](#gripe), [whine](#whine), [grumble](#grumble)

### mock

**Pronunciation:** /mɑk/ &middot; *MAHK*
**Русский:** высмеивать, передразнивать — злая насмешка, обычно через подражание; переходный
**Type:** reporting verb — ridicule &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *mock* + **sb** / **sth** &middot; *mock* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; *mock* + **sb's** accent / voice / walk &middot; + direct quotation delivered in imitation &middot; passive *be mocked for*
**Never:** ***mock at someone***. The verb is transitive — *mocked him*, not ***mocked at him***. (*Mock at* was standard in older English and survives in some British writing; in American English it reads as an error.) Also no *that*-clause: ***mocked that he was scared*** should be *mocked him **for** being scared*.
**Reports:** ridicule aimed at making someone look foolish, usually by imitation. Unlike [tease](#tease) it is not affectionate, and unlike [criticize](#criticize) it attacks the person rather than the choice. *Mock-* as a prefix (*mock trial*, *mock-serious*) means "imitation," which is the same root doing honest work.

> "Ooh, look at me, I read the *manual*," he said in a high voice.  →  He **mocked** me **for** reading the manual.
> "Nice parallel parking," she said, watching him take six tries.  →  She **mocked** his parking.

1. He *mocked* the accent for a full minute before noticing whose family had it.
2. She was *mocked* online for a typo in a two-line post.
3. Don't *mock* the checklist — the checklist is why the plane lands.
4. They *mocked* his handwriting and copied his answers.
5. The bill was *mocked* by both parties and passed unanimously.
6. "Are you *mocking* me?" "A little."

**See also:** [tease](#tease), [taunt](#taunt), [sneer](#sneer), [laugh](#laugh), [gloat](#gloat), [criticize](#criticize)

### mumble

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌmbəl/ &middot; *MUM-buhl*
**Русский:** бормотать невнятно — о нечёткой дикции, без оттенка недовольства
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *mumble* + direct quotation &middot; *mumble* + **that**-clause &middot; *mumble* + **noun phrase** (*mumbled an apology*) &middot; *mumble* + *to* **sb** (+ *that*) &middot; *mumble* + *something about* + noun &middot; *mumble* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***mumble me something*** — no ditransitive frame. The hearer needs *to*: *mumbled something **to** me*, not ***mumbled me something***.
**Reports:** speech produced so indistinctly that the words are hard to make out — a fact about articulation, not about mood. That is what separates it from [mutter](#mutter), which is clear enough but resentful, and [murmur](#murmur), which is soft but usually warm. You can mumble a compliment.

> "Sorry, I didn't see it," he said, barely audibly.  →  He **mumbled that** he **hadn't** seen it.
> "…something about the parking…"  →  She **mumbled something about** the parking.

1. He *mumbled* an apology and went back to his desk.
2. She *mumbled* that she'd finish it by Friday, and she did.
3. Stop *mumbling* — the microphone is right there.
4. The witness *mumbled* through the oath and had to repeat it.
5. He *mumbled* something to the cashier that neither of them understood.
6. Teenagers *mumble*; it is not a moral failing.

**See also:** [mutter](#mutter), [murmur](#murmur), [whisper](#whisper), [groan](#groan)

### murmur

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɜrmər/ &middot; *MUR-mur*
**Русский:** тихо говорить, шептать ласково — книжн.; мягко и внятно, в отличие от mutter
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** literary
**Patterns:** *murmur* + direct quotation &middot; *murmur* + **that**-clause &middot; *murmur* + **noun phrase** (*murmured her name*) &middot; *murmur* + *to* **sb** / *to oneself* (+ *that*) &middot; *murmur* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***murmur someone something*** and ***murmur at someone***. The hearer takes *to*: *murmured **to** him that it was time to go*. (*Murmur against* — *the people murmured against Moses* — is a real but archaic complaining sense, biblical in flavor; do not use it for ordinary quiet speech.)
**Reports:** soft, low speech, usually gentle, intimate, or approving — the warm member of the quiet-speech family. Where [mutter](#mutter) is resentful and [mumble](#mumble) is unclear, a murmur is deliberately quiet and perfectly articulate. There is also a collective sense: *a murmur ran through the crowd*, meaning a low general sound of reaction.

> "It's going to be all right," she said very softly.  →  She **murmured that** it **was** going to be all right.
> "Thank you," he said, barely above a breath.  →  He **murmured** his thanks.

1. She *murmured* something to the nurse and squeezed his hand.
2. The audience *murmured* when the lights came back up.
3. He *murmured* that he'd rather not discuss it in the hallway.
4. A *murmur* of agreement went around the table.
5. "Stay," she *murmured*, and he stayed.
6. The stream *murmured* through the whole recording, which ruined the take.

**See also:** [mumble](#mumble), [mutter](#mutter), [whisper](#whisper)

### mutter

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌtər/ &middot; *MUT-er*
**Русский:** бормотать себе под нос, бурчать — нарочно вполголоса и с недовольством
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking, complaint &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *mutter* + direct quotation &middot; *mutter* + **that**-clause &middot; *mutter* + **noun phrase** (*muttered a curse*) &middot; *mutter* + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *mutter* + *to* **sb** / *to oneself* (+ *that*) &middot; *mutter* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***mutter someone that*** — the hearer needs *to*. And be careful with **mutter at** someone: it occurs, but it is marginal, and *muttered **to** himself* is the idiom you want for talking under your breath.
**Reports:** low, half-audible speech carrying resentment — words the speaker wants heard just enough to register and not enough to answer. That resentment is the whole difference from [mumble](#mumble): a mumble is unclear by accident, a mutter is quiet on purpose.

> "This is a complete waste of time," he said under his breath.  →  He **muttered that** it **was** a complete waste of time.
> "…never listens to anybody…"  →  She **muttered about** him never listening.

1. He *muttered* something about the budget and left the room.
2. She *muttered* that she'd been asking for that data since March.
3. He walks the halls *muttering* to himself and writes the best code in the building.
4. "Say it out loud or don't *mutter* it," his father said.
5. There was *muttering* in the back row for the rest of the presentation.
6. She *muttered* a word her grandmother would not have approved of.

**See also:** [mumble](#mumble), [murmur](#murmur), [grumble](#grumble), [snap](#snap), [whisper](#whisper)

### object

**Pronunciation:** /əbˈdʒɛkt/ &middot; *uhb-JEKT* — the verb stresses the second syllable; the noun *object* /ˈɑbdʒɪkt/ (*AHB-jikt*) stresses the first
**Русский:** возражать — object to + существительное или -ing (не инфинитив!); формальное несогласие
**Type:** reporting verb — objection &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *object* + **that**-clause &middot; *object* + *to* + **noun phrase** &middot; *object* + *to* + **-ing** &middot; *object* + *to* + **sb('s)** + **-ing** (*objected to my leaving early*) &middot; *object* (intransitive) &middot; + direct quotation
**Never:** ***object to be treated that way***. The *to* in *object to* is a **preposition**, not part of an infinitive, so what follows is an *-ing* form or a noun: *objected to **being** treated that way*, *objected to **the** treatment*. This is the single most common error with this verb. Also never ***object something*** and never ***object against*** — the latter is a direct import from other languages and is not English. And *to* here never introduces the **hearer**: *she objected to the chairman* means she found the chairman objectionable, not that she said something to him. To name the person addressed, rewrite — *told the chairman she objected*.
**Reports:** a stated disagreement, usually procedural or principled rather than emotional. It is the formal end of the complaining family — the courtroom verb, the meeting verb — where [complain](#complain) is domestic and [protest](#protest) is louder and more self-defensive.

> "I don't think we should vote on this today," she said.  →  She **objected to voting** on it that day.
> "But the numbers haven't been audited."  →  He **objected that** the numbers **had not** been audited.

1. Two members *objected* to the wording and one *objected* to the whole idea.
2. She *objected* that no one had seen the contract before the meeting.
3. If nobody *objects*, we'll move to the next item.
4. He *objected* to being asked the same question a third time.
5. "*Objection*," said the attorney, and the judge sustained it.
6. I don't *object* in principle; I *object* to the timing.

**See also:** [protest](#protest), [complain](#complain), [criticize](#criticize), [insist](03-agree-refuse.md#insist)

### praise

**Pronunciation:** /preɪz/ &middot; *PRAYZ*
**Русский:** хвалить, хвалебно отзываться — praise sb for sth; можно и заочно, в отличие от compliment
**Type:** reporting verb — praise &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *praise* + **sb** / **sth** &middot; *praise* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; *praise* + **sb** / **sth** + *as* + **noun phrase** / adjective &middot; passive *be praised for* / *be praised as*
**Never:** ***praise someone that they did well*** and ***praise about something***. No *that*-clause, no *about*. Also not **praise sb on** — that is [compliment](#compliment)'s and [congratulate](#congratulate)'s preposition. Repairs: *praised her **for** her handling of the crisis*, *praised her handling of the crisis*.
**Reports:** approval expressed publicly, and unlike [compliment](#compliment) it need not be said to the person's face or even in their hearing: *the review praised the second act*. It evaluates the work, where [congratulate](#congratulate) merely acknowledges the outcome.

> "She handled that better than anyone could have," he said.  →  He **praised her for** her handling of it.

1. The inspector *praised* the crew for stopping work when they noticed the crack.
2. She was *praised* in public and passed over in private.
3. The review *praised* the cinematography and said nothing about the plot.
4. He *praises* people specifically, which is why it means something.
5. Faint *praise* is worse than none.
6. Both papers *praised* the settlement as a reasonable compromise.

### protest

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈtɛst/ &middot; *pruh-TEST* — the verb stresses the second syllable; the noun *protest* /ˈproʊtɛst/ (*PROH-test*) stresses the first
**Русский:** протестовать; возражать, оправдываясь — амер. protest sth, брит. protest against sth
**Type:** reporting verb — objection &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *protest* + **that**-clause &middot; *protest* + **noun phrase** (American) &middot; *protest* + *against* / *at* + **noun phrase** (British) &middot; *protest* + *to* **sb** (+ *that*) &middot; *protest* + direct quotation &middot; *protest* (intransitive) &middot; *protest* + **noun phrase** (= assert: *protested his innocence*)
**Never:** ***protest to do something*** and ***protest someone that***. No infinitive, and no personal object before the clause — the person addressed takes *to*: *protested **to** the referee that the ball was out*, never ***protested the referee that***.
**American vs. British:** American English protests things **transitively** — *protest the war*, *protest the ruling*, *protest the layoffs*. British English uses *against* or *at* — *protest against the war*, *protested at the decision*. Both are correct in their own varieties; in American writing, *protest against* is not wrong but is markedly less common, and *protest at* reads as British. The transitive use is also the standard American legal one: *protested the assessment*, *protest a ruling*.
**Reports:** a stated objection to something perceived as unjust, often self-defensive and often after the fact. *"But I didn't touch it!" he protested* is the characteristic use — the verb carries a hint that the objection may not be believed, which is why *he protested his innocence* is always faintly skeptical.

> "But I turned it in on Tuesday!" he said.  →  He **protested that** he **had** turned it in on Tuesday.
> "This ruling is unjust and we won't accept it."  →  They **protested** the ruling.

1. Students *protested* the tuition increase for three weeks.
2. "I never agreed to that," she *protested*.
3. He *protested* that he had asked twice and gotten no answer.
4. She *protested* her innocence right up to the plea deal.
5. Thousands *protested* outside the statehouse in the rain.
6. He signed it under *protest* and said so in writing.

**See also:** [object](#object), [complain](#complain), [insist](03-agree-refuse.md#insist), [refuse](03-agree-refuse.md#refuse)

### rebuke

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈbjuk/ &middot; *ri-BYOOK*
**Русский:** делать выговор, порицать — книжн., офиц.; резкое замечание от вышестоящего
**Type:** reporting verb — reproof &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *rebuke* + **sb** &middot; *rebuke* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; passive *be rebuked for* &middot; noun *a rebuke* (*to* sb)
**Never:** ***rebuke someone that they were late***, ***rebuke someone to do something***, and ***rebuke at someone***. No clause, no infinitive, no preposition before the person. Repair: *rebuked him **for** being late*.
**Reports:** a sharp, formal reprimand delivered by someone with standing to deliver it — a court, a board, a superior. Stronger and more official than [chide](#chide), less domestic than [scold](#scold), and unlike [criticize](#criticize) it is an act of authority rather than an opinion. In American news writing it most often appears in the passive or as a noun: *a sharp rebuke from the bench*.

> "This filing is unacceptable and you know better," the judge said.  →  The judge **rebuked** the attorney **for** the filing.
> "You do not speak to the press without clearing it first."  →  She **rebuked him for** speaking to the press.

2. He was publicly *rebuked* for comments he made at a fundraiser.
3. The ruling is a *rebuke* to every agency that assumed the rule was optional.
4. She *rebuked* the staff for a leak nobody could trace.
5. The letter *rebuked* the department in language unusual for an inspector general.
6. A quiet *rebuke* from her did more than an hour of shouting from him.

### reproach

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈproʊtʃ/ &middot; *ri-PROHCH*
**Русский:** упрекать, укорять — книжн.; обиженный упрёк тому, от кого ждали большего
**Type:** reporting verb — reproof &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *reproach* + **sb** &middot; *reproach* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; *reproach* + **sb** + *with* + **noun phrase** (formal) &middot; *reproach* + **oneself** + *for* &middot; passive *be reproached for* &middot; noun *a reproach*, *above reproach*
**Never:** ***reproach someone that they forgot*** and ***reproach at someone***. No *that*-clause, no preposition before the person. Repair: *reproached him **for** forgetting*.
**for vs. with:** American English uses *for* + *-ing* almost exclusively. **Reproach sb with** + noun (*reproached him with his own words*) is formal and mostly British; it survives in American legal and literary prose.
**Reports:** disappointed criticism delivered to someone you had expectations of — the hurt cousin of [rebuke](#rebuke). Where a rebuke asserts authority, a reproach asserts a relationship, which is why *she reproached him* implies they knew each other and *she rebuked him* does not.

> "I thought you of all people would have said something," she said.  →  She **reproached him for** staying quiet.
> "After everything we did for you."  →  They **reproached** her **for** leaving without notice.

1. She *reproached* him for missing the funeral and never mentioned it again.
2. He *reproached* himself for years over a decision that was never his to make.
3. Her record is above *reproach*, which is why the accusation landed so oddly.
4. The letter *reproached* the board with its own published standards.
5. There was more sorrow than anger in the *reproach*.
6. Nobody *reproached* the intern; everyone *reproached* the manager.

**See also:** [rebuke](#rebuke), [chide](#chide), [scold](#scold), [blame](#blame)

### scold

**Pronunciation:** /skoʊld/ &middot; *SKOHLD*
**Русский:** ругать, отчитывать — сердитая выволочка, обычно взрослого ребёнку
**Type:** reporting verb — reproof &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Patterns:** *scold* + **sb** &middot; *scold* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** / **noun phrase** &middot; + direct quotation (*"Not on the couch," she scolded*) &middot; passive *be scolded for*
**Never:** ***scold someone that they did it***, ***scold someone to do something***, and ***scold at someone***. Repairs: *scolded him **for** doing it*, *told him not to do it*.
**Reports:** an angry telling-off, prototypically an adult to a child. Using it of two adults is a deliberate move — *she scolded her colleague* makes the colleague sound infantilized, which is sometimes exactly the point and is a well-known trap in reporting on women in public life.

> "How many times have I told you not to slam that door?"  →  She **scolded him for** slamming the door.
> "You know better than to leave that out."  →  He **scolded** the kids **for** leaving the milk out.

1. She *scolded* the dog, who was unmoved.
2. He was *scolded* by a stranger for parking across two spaces.
3. "Inside voices," she *scolded*, without looking up.
4. The op-ed *scolds* readers for a problem it spends no words explaining.
5. My grandmother *scolded* me in two languages, for emphasis.
6. Nobody likes being *scolded* by a machine, which is why the warning gets ignored.

**See also:** [chide](#chide), [rebuke](#rebuke), [reproach](#reproach), [snap](#snap), [yell](#yell)

### shout

**Pronunciation:** /ʃaʊt/ &middot; *SHOWT*
**Русский:** кричать — shout at sb = сердито на кого-то, shout to sb = чтобы услышали издалека
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *shout* + direct quotation &middot; *shout* + **that**-clause &middot; *shout* + **wh**-clause &middot; *shout* + **noun phrase** (*shouted an order*) &middot; *shout* + *at* **sb** &middot; *shout* + *to* **sb** &middot; *shout* + *at/to* **sb** + **to-infinitive** &middot; *shout* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***shout someone that*** and ***shout someone something***. The hearer always takes a preposition: *shouted **to** me that the gate was closing*, not ***shouted me that***.
**at vs. to — this is the whole verb:**

| Frame | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *shout **at** sb* | anger, aggression | He **shouted at** the referee. |
| *shout **to** sb* | distance, being heard | He **shouted to** her from the dock. |

*Don't shout at me* is about tone; *I shouted to him across the lot* is about acoustics. Learners who use *at* for both come across as describing a fight.
**Commands:** *shout at/to sb + to-infinitive* is the standard way to report a shouted order — *shouted at us to get back*, and see [Reported commands, requests, and advice](../06-commands-requests.md).
**Reports:** loud speech. Unlike [yell](#yell) it is not necessarily angry, and unlike [cry](#cry) it carries no literary flavor.

> "The bridge is out!" he said at the top of his voice.  →  He **shouted that** the bridge **was** out.
> "Get back from the edge!"  →  She **shouted at** us **to get** back from the edge.

1. Someone *shouted* that the elevator was stuck between floors.
2. He *shouted* to her across the parking lot and she didn't hear him.
3. Please stop *shouting* at the umpire; he has a hard enough job.
4. She *shouted* over the music to ask where the bathroom was.
5. He *shouted* a warning about half a second before the shelf came down.
6. They *shouted* until they were hoarse and the vote went the other way anyway.

**See also:** [yell](#yell), [cry](#cry), [exclaim](#exclaim), [snap](#snap), [whisper](#whisper)

### sigh

**Pronunciation:** /saɪ/ &middot; *SY* — the *gh* is silent
**Русский:** вздыхать, со вздохом произнести — о смирении, усталости или облегчении
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *sigh* (intransitive) &middot; *sigh* + direct quotation &middot; *sigh* + **that**-clause (fiction) &middot; *sigh* + *over* / *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *sigh* + *for* + **noun phrase** (literary, = long for) &middot; noun *a sigh*, *with a sigh*
**Never:** ***sigh someone*** and ***sigh someone that***. There is no object frame at all beyond the cognate object (*sighed a long sigh*), which reports no speech, and no personal object: if the hearer matters, write *said to me with a sigh*.
**The honest note:** you cannot physically produce a sentence while sighing, and copy editors have been striking *"Fine," she sighed* out of manuscripts for a century. It has not worked. The construction is standard in published fiction and perfectly clear, and *she sighed that she'd try again tomorrow* appears in reputable journalism. In formal nonfiction, use *said with a sigh*; everywhere else, use it and stop worrying.
**Reports:** resignation, weariness, or relief expressed as breath. As a reporting verb it tells you the speaker had given up, not what act was performed.

> "All right, I'll do it myself," she said wearily.  →  She **sighed that** she **would** do it herself. / She said **with a sigh** that she would do it herself.
> "Well. That's that."  →  He **sighed** and let it go.

1. She *sighed*, looked at the clock, and opened the file again.
2. "There's nothing else to try," he *sighed*.
3. He *sighed* over the invoice for a while and then paid it.
4. There was a long *sigh* on the other end of the line.
5. The whole room *sighed* with relief when the numbers came up green.
6. She *sighed* that she'd been through three of these reorganizations already.

**See also:** [groan](#groan), [moan](#moan), [laugh](#laugh), [grumble](#grumble)

### snap

**Pronunciation:** /snæp/ &middot; *SNAP*
**Русский:** огрызнуться, резко бросить — разг.; короткая раздражённая реплика, snap at sb
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking, irritation &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Patterns:** *snap* + direct quotation &middot; *snap* + **that**-clause &middot; *snap* + *at* **sb** &middot; *snap* + *at* **sb** + **to-infinitive** (*snapped at him to sit down*) &middot; *snap* (intransitive, = lose one's temper)
**Never:** ***snap someone***, ***snap someone that***, and ***snap to someone*** in this sense. The target takes *at* and nothing else: *snapped **at** me*. (*Snap to it* exists, but that is a different idiom meaning "hurry up.")
**Spelling:** doubles the *p* — *snapped*, *snapping*.
**Reports:** a short, sharp, irritated reply — brevity is the whole point. A snapped remark is one sentence, delivered in a tone that ends the exchange. Compare [mutter](#mutter), which is resentful but quiet, and [yell](#yell), which is loud but not necessarily short.

> "I said I'd handle it," she said sharply.  →  She **snapped that** she **had** said she'd handle it.
> "Do you mind?"  →  He **snapped at** the man behind him in line.

1. "I'm not asking again," he *snapped*.
2. She *snapped* that she'd sent the file twice already.
3. Sorry I *snapped* at you — it's been a long week.
4. He *snapped* at a waiter and the whole table went quiet.
5. She almost never *snaps*, which is why it registered.
6. He *snapped* after the third schedule change and walked out.

**See also:** [yell](#yell), [growl](#growl), [mutter](#mutter), [scold](#scold), [shout](#shout)

### sneer

**Pronunciation:** /snɪr/ &middot; *SNEER*
**Русский:** презрительно усмехаться, глумиться — книжн., неодобр.; только sneer at, без to
**Type:** reporting verb — contempt, manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** literary, journalistic — disapproving
**Patterns:** *sneer* (intransitive) &middot; *sneer* + *at* + **sb** / **sth** &middot; *sneer* + **that**-clause &middot; + direct quotation (*"Nice try," he sneered*) &middot; noun *a sneer*, adjective *sneering*
**Never:** ***sneer someone***, ***sneer someone that***, ***sneer to someone***, and ***sneer about something***. The verb reaches its target only through *at*, and that target is the thing despised, not the hearer: *sneered **at** the suggestion*, *sneered **at** me*. Unlike [whisper](#whisper) and [mutter](#mutter), it has no *to* frame at all, so the person spoken to cannot be named inside the verb — write *said **to** me with a sneer*.
**Reports:** contempt carried in the voice or the face — a remark delivered as though the thing said were beneath the speaker. Like [whine](#whine), it is the reporter's verdict rather than a neutral description: *he sneered that the plan wouldn't work* instructs the reader to dislike him, and a fair report writes *said dismissively* instead. Compare [mock](#mock), which works by imitation, and [taunt](#taunt), which works by provocation; a sneer needs no audience beyond the person it dismisses.

> "That's the best you could do?" he said, with his lip curled.  →  He **sneered at** my draft.
> "Nobody serious believes that."  →  He **sneered that** nobody serious **believed** it.

1. He *sneered* at the suggestion that anyone had asked his opinion.
2. She *sneered* that the report had been written by someone who had never seen the site.
3. "Amateurs," he *sneered*, and went back to his phone.
4. The column *sneers* at the readers it is trying to persuade, which is why it persuades nobody.
5. There was a *sneer* in his voice that the transcript does not record.
6. Don't *sneer* — half of that code is still running in production.

**See also:** [mock](#mock), [taunt](#taunt), [gloat](#gloat), [laugh](#laugh), [snap](#snap)

### taunt

**Pronunciation:** /tɔnt/ &middot; *TAWNT*
**Русский:** дразнить, задирать — насмешки, чтобы вывести из себя; переходный, без предлога
**Type:** reporting verb — ridicule &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *taunt* + **sb** &middot; *taunt* + **sb** + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *taunt* + **sb** + *with* + **noun phrase** &middot; *taunt* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** (less common than *about*) &middot; + direct quotation (*"Can't catch me," he taunted*) &middot; passive *be taunted about* / *with* &middot; noun *a taunt*
**Never:** ***taunt at someone*** and ***taunt someone that***. The verb is transitive. Repair: *taunted him **about** the score*.
**Reports:** repeated, provoking mockery meant to get a reaction — the playground and the sideline verb. [Mock](#mock) makes someone look foolish; a taunt is designed to make them respond, which is why it is the word used for what starts fights.

> "Zero for four! Zero for four!" the fans said, over and over.  →  The fans **taunted** him **about** going zero for four.
> "You'll never finish it and we both know it."  →  She **taunted him with** his own unfinished projects.

1. He *taunted* the goalie for the entire third period.
2. She *taunted* him about the bet until he doubled it.
3. The *taunts* from the away section got the game stopped for four minutes.
4. Don't *taunt* a system administrator two days before your renewal.
5. They *taunted* each other cheerfully for an hour and left as friends.
6. He was *taunted* with a nickname he'd been given in the seventh grade.

**See also:** [mock](#mock), [tease](#tease), [sneer](#sneer), [gloat](#gloat), [crow](#crow)

### tease

**Pronunciation:** /tiz/ &middot; *TEEZ*
**Русский:** поддразнивать, подшучивать — обычно беззлобно, между своими; tease sb about sth
**Type:** reporting verb — light ridicule &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Patterns:** *tease* + **sb** &middot; *tease* + **sb** + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *tease* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *tease* + **sb** + *into* / *out of* + **-ing** &middot; + direct quotation &middot; passive *be teased about*
**Never:** ***tease at someone*** and ***tease someone that***. Repair: *teased him **about** his tie*.
**about vs. for:** *about* is the default and the friendlier one; *for* leans toward blame — *teased him **about** his accent* is affectionate, *teased him **for** losing again* has an edge.
**Reports:** joking at someone's expense, usually affectionately and usually between people who like each other. That affection is the whole difference from [mock](#mock) and [taunt](#taunt); *I was only teasing* is the standard American repair when the affection did not come through.

> "Nice haircut. Did you lose a bet?"  →  She **teased him about** his haircut.
> "You've told that story four times tonight."  →  They **teased** him **for** repeating himself.

1. His sisters *teased* him about the truck for twenty years.
2. She *teased* me for alphabetizing the spice rack.
3. "I'm *teasing*," he said, a beat too late.
4. He was *teased* mercilessly and gave as good as he got.
5. She *teased* him into trying the karaoke machine.
6. There's a line between *teasing* and something else, and it moves depending on who's laughing.

**See also:** [joke](#joke), [mock](#mock), [taunt](#taunt), [laugh](#laugh), [chide](#chide)

### thank

**Pronunciation:** /θæŋk/ &middot; *THANK* — voiceless *th*, as in *thin*
**Русский:** благодарить — thank sb for sth: человек прямым дополнением, повод через for
**Type:** reporting verb — thanks &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *thank* + **sb** &middot; *thank* + **sb** + *for* + **noun phrase** &middot; *thank* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *thank* + **sb** + *for* + **having done sth** &middot; passive *be thanked for* &middot; fixed idiom *I'll thank you to* + **verb** (= a sharp request, not a thanks)
**Never:** ***thank to someone***, ***thank someone that they came***, and ***thank someone of something***. The verb takes the person directly and the reason through *for*: *thanked me **for** coming*, not ***thanked to me*** and not ***thanked me that I came***. (*Thanks to* exists as a prepositional phrase meaning "because of" — *thanks to the rain, we canceled* — and is a different construction entirely.)
**Two look-alikes that are not counterexamples:** *Thank God that nobody was hurt* is a fixed exclamation, not the reporting verb, and the *that*-clause belongs to that idiom alone and licenses nothing else, so still never ***thanked her that she came***. And *I'll thank you to mind your own business* is an infinitive after a frozen idiom meaning "kindly do it," not a *thank + to*-infinitive frame; you cannot say ***thanked him to help***.
**Perfect for a past favor:** *thanked her for having covered his shift* is available but unnecessary; plain *-ing* already covers finished action — *thanked her for covering his shift*.
**Reports:** an expression of gratitude. This is the verb that turns *"Thank you so much for coming"* into a single reported clause, and the *for* + *-ing* frame is the most reliably useful pattern in this whole file.

> "Thank you for covering my shift," he said.  →  He **thanked** me **for covering** his shift.
> "Thanks — I really appreciate it."  →  She **thanked** me.

1. She *thanked* every member of the crew by name.
2. He *thanked* me for the ride and I hadn't even offered yet.
3. I want to *thank* whoever refilled the coffee at 6 a.m.
4. They were *thanked* in the acknowledgments and paid in nothing else.
5. Don't *thank* me — *thank* the person who caught it in review.
6. She *thanked* him for his honesty in a tone that suggested otherwise.

**See also:** [apologize](#apologize), [congratulate](#congratulate), [compliment](#compliment), [praise](#praise)

### whine

**Pronunciation:** /waɪn/ &middot; *WYNE* — some American speakers keep an /hw/ onset, *HWYNE*
**Русский:** ныть, канючить — разг., неодобр.; жаловаться плаксиво; всегда оценка рассказчика
**Type:** reporting verb — complaint &middot; **Register:** informal, disapproving
**Patterns:** *whine* + **that**-clause &middot; *whine* + *about* + **noun phrase** / **-ing** &middot; *whine* + *to* **sb** + *about* / *that* &middot; *whine* + *at* + **sb** / **sth** (literal noise: *whined at the door*) &middot; *whine* + direct quotation &middot; *whine* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***whine someone*** and ***whine something***. No object without a preposition, and no personal object: ***whined me about the seat*** should be *whined **to** me **about** the seat*.
**Reports:** complaining in a high, drawn-out, childish tone — and, crucially, the reporter's verdict that the complaint is unwarranted. There is no neutral use. *He complained that the shift was unsafe* takes the complaint seriously; *He whined that the shift was unsafe* dismisses it, which makes this a verb to choose deliberately and never by accident in a report you want read as fair.
**Homophone:** identical to *wine* for most Americans.

> "Why do I always have to be the one to close?"  →  He **whined about** always having to close.
> "It's too far to walk."  →  She **whined that** it **was** too far to walk.

1. He *whined* about the seating chart until they moved him.
2. She *whined* that nobody had told her, though the email was in her inbox.
3. Stop *whining* and get in the car.
4. The dog *whined* at the door for ten minutes.
5. Calling it *whining* was how the complaint got buried.
6. There's a difference between raising an issue and *whining*, and it is mostly tone.

**See also:** [complain](#complain), [gripe](#gripe), [grumble](#grumble), [moan](#moan)

### whisper

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪspər/ &middot; *WIS-per* — the *h* is silent
**Русский:** шептать, шепнуть — whisper to sb that…; пассив it is whispered that = ходят слухи
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *whisper* + direct quotation &middot; *whisper* + **that**-clause &middot; *whisper* + **wh**-clause &middot; *whisper* + **noun phrase** (*whispered a name*) &middot; *whisper* + *to* **sb** + *that* &middot; *whisper* + *to* **sb** + **to-infinitive** (*whispered to me to wait*) &middot; *whisper* (intransitive) &middot; passive *it is whispered that* (= rumor)
**Never:** ***whisper someone that*** and ***whisper someone something***. The hearer takes *to*: *whispered **to** me that the boss was behind us*, not ***whispered me that***.
**The rumor passive:** *It was whispered that the deal had already been signed* is a live and useful construction — the passive with no agent is exactly how you report something everyone was saying and nobody would say on the record. See [Special cases](../08-special-cases.md) for the impersonal passive report generally.
**Reports:** speech with no voicing behind it, produced quietly on purpose — for secrecy, for intimacy, or because the room requires it. It is the manner verb that most freely takes a *that*-clause; nobody objects to *she whispered that she'd be right back*.

> "Don't look now, but he's right behind you," she said very quietly.  →  She **whispered that** he **was** right behind me.
> "Meet me outside in five."  →  He **whispered to** me **to** meet him outside in five.

1. She *whispered* something to the bailiff and the judge called a recess.
2. He *whispered* that the second door was unlocked.
3. "Not here," she *whispered*.
4. It was *whispered* around the building that the merger had been signed weeks earlier.
5. The kids *whispered* through the entire ceremony.
6. He *whispered* a name I didn't catch and everyone else clearly did.

**See also:** [murmur](#murmur), [mutter](#mutter), [mumble](#mumble), [shout](#shout)

### yell

**Pronunciation:** /jɛl/ &middot; *YEL*
**Русский:** орать, вопить — разг.; yell at sb = сердито на кого-то, yell to sb = издалека
**Type:** reporting verb — manner of speaking &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *yell* + direct quotation &middot; *yell* + **that**-clause &middot; *yell* + **wh**-clause &middot; *yell* + **noun phrase** (*yelled a warning*) &middot; *yell* + *at* **sb** &middot; *yell* + *to* **sb** &middot; *yell* + *at* / *to* **sb** + **to-infinitive** &middot; *yell* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***yell someone that*** and ***yell someone something***. As with [shout](#shout), the hearer needs a preposition: *yelled **at** me to slow down*.
**at vs. to:** the same split as *shout* — **yell at** someone is anger, **yell to** someone is distance. *He yelled at me* means I was in trouble; *he yelled to me* means he was far away. Note that *get yelled at* is the ordinary American way to say you were reprimanded, and it says nothing about actual volume: *I got yelled at for the expense report* may describe a quiet conversation.
**Reports:** loud, usually angry speech. More informal and more emotional than [shout](#shout) — a coach shouts instructions and yells at a referee.

> "Slow down! You're going to miss the turn!"  →  She **yelled at** me **to** slow down.
> "The oven's been on for three hours!"  →  He **yelled that** the oven **had** been on for three hours.

1. Somebody *yelled* that the last train was already on the platform.
2. She *yelled* to him from the top of the stairs and he never heard her.
3. Don't *yell* at the kid; he's four.
4. I got *yelled* at for something I hadn't done and apologized anyway.
5. He *yelled* until his voice gave out at the third quarter.
6. "Nobody's *yelling*," she said, yelling slightly.

**See also:** [shout](#shout), [scold](#scold), [snap](#snap), [cry](#cry)
