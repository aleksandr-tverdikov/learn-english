# Neutral reporting verbs

> The workhorses of reported speech — the verbs that tell you someone said something without telling you what to think about it.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

A neutral reporting verb makes no claim about whether the message was true, whether the speaker had any right to say it, or how the speaker felt while saying it. Substitute *claimed*, *admitted*, or *insisted* into any sentence in this file and you have added a judgment that was not there before; substitute *stated* or *mentioned* or *noted* and you have only changed the weight and the register. What actually varies inside this group is not attitude but **grammar** — specifically, how each verb handles the listener: some refuse one outright (*say*, *mention*, *announce*), some demand one with no preposition (*tell me*, *inform me*, *notify me*), and some accept one only behind a preposition (*explain **to** me*, *describe **to** me*, *share **with** me*, *discuss it **with** me*). That one distinction produces nearly every error learners make with these verbs, and it is why *say* and *tell*, which mean almost the same thing, can never be swapped for each other.

---

## The anchor: *say* vs. *tell*

These two carry more reported speech than every other verb in English combined, and they split on exactly one point: **the person you are talking to**.

| | *say* | *tell* |
|---|---|---|
| Personal object | **Never** — *say me* is impossible | **Required** — *tell that* is impossible |
| How the listener attaches | Behind *to*: *said **to** me* | Bare, immediately after the verb: *told **me*** |
| With a *that*-clause | *She said (that) she was tired.* | *She told me (that) she was tired.* |
| With a direct quotation | *"I'm tired," she said.* | *"I'm tired," she told me.* — never *she told.* |
| With a *to*-infinitive | Not in edited prose (see the entry) | *She told me to wait.* |
| With a bare noun phrase | Short fixed list: *say hello, say nothing, say so* | Short fixed list: *tell the truth, tell a story, tell time* |
| The fatal error | ~~She said me she was tired.~~ | ~~She told to me she was tired.~~ |

Two sentences that mean the same thing, correctly built each way:

> "I'm not coming," she said.  →  She **said (to me) that** she wasn't coming.
> "I'm not coming," she said.  →  She **told me that** she wasn't coming.

## The three listener patterns

Almost every verb in this file falls into one of three groups. Learn the group and you have learned the verb.

| Group | The listener is… | Verbs | Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No object** | impossible, or only behind *to* | say, mention, announce, state, remark, comment, note, observe, add, continue, report, indicate, repeat, relate, recount, convey, communicate, describe, explain, write | *explained **to** me that…* | ~~*explained me that…*~~ |
| **Bare object required** | mandatory, with no preposition | tell, inform, notify, advise — and, catalogued in other files, [remind](06-order-request.md#remind), [assure](02-assert.md#assure), [convince](05-advise-suggest.md#convince) | *informed **me** that…* | ~~*informed that…*~~ |
| **Prepositional object** | attaches with *with*, not *to* | share, discuss | *shared the news **with** me* | ~~*shared me the news*~~ |

Four entries sit outside the three groups because they never name a listener at all: *quote*, *put it*, *go on*, and the two fixed phrases. *Quote* is the one to watch, because it does take a bare personal object — but that object is the person **quoted**, not the person spoken to: *Don't quote me* means "don't attribute this to me." *Mention* has the same trap in reverse: *He mentioned you* makes you the subject of the remark, never its audience.

The one thing almost no verb here allows is the double-object pattern with an abstract message: ~~*informed me the change*~~, ~~*notified us the date*~~, ~~*announced them the results*~~, ~~*explained me the rule*~~. English gives that frame to *tell* — *told me the answer* — and, for something written, to *write*: *wrote me a long letter*. Nothing else in this file takes it.

## What each verb lets you put after it

The listener is one axis; the **complement** is the other. Most errors that survive the *say/tell* rule are errors of complement — a **that**-clause after a verb that refuses one, or a person plus a **to**-infinitive after a verb that has no such frame.

| Complement | Verbs here that take it | Verbs here that refuse it |
|---|---|---|
| **that**-clause | say, tell (+ sb), advise (+ sb), add, announce, comment, communicate, continue, convey, explain, indicate, inform (+ sb), mention, note, notify (+ sb), observe, relate, remark, repeat, report, share, state, write | **describe**, **discuss**, **quote** outright; **recount**, **put it**, and **go on** only stiffly or not at all in American prose |
| **wh**-clause | announce, communicate, convey, describe, discuss, explain, indicate, inform (+ sb), mention, note, observe, recount, relate, repeat, report, share, state, tell (+ sb), write, advise (+ sb); *say* only under negation or a modal (*I couldn't say what he meant*) | comment and remark, which need *on* first — *remarked **on** how much it had changed*; notify, which needs *of* — *notify us **of** what you decide* |
| **-ing** | continue, discuss, go on, mention, report; and behind *as*, describe and quote | say, tell, state, announce, explain, inform — no **-ing** complement at all |
| person + **to**-infinitive | **tell**, and nothing else in this file | every other verb here: ~~*said me to wait*~~, ~~*explained me to wait*~~, ~~*informed me to wait*~~, ~~*mentioned me to wait*~~, ~~*wrote me to come*~~ |

The last row is the one to memorize. *Advise* looks like an exception — *advised us to leave early* — but that is the other *advise*, the one that recommends, and it lives in [advise, suggest](05-advise-suggest.md#advise). In the notification sense catalogued here, *advise* takes a **that**-clause, not an infinitive.

## The verbs, A–Z

### add

**Pronunciation:** /æd/ &middot; *AD*
**Русский:** добавить — сказать ещё что-то к только что сказанному тем же говорящим
**Type:** reporting verb — a further statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *add* + **that**-clause &middot; *add* + direct quotation &middot; *add* + noun phrase &middot; *add* + noun phrase + **to** + noun phrase &middot; impersonal *it should be added that*
**Never:** ***add me that*** / ***add someone something*** — *add* takes no personal object at all, and unlike [explain](#explain) or [announce](#announce) it cannot rescue one with a preposition either: ***added to me that*** is not the repair. The *to* after *add* names what the thing was added **to**, never who heard it. Repairs: *added that the lot was full*, *added it to the list*, *added, turning to me, that she was leaving*.
**Requires something to add to:** *add* cannot open a report. It means "and then this, from the same speaker," so there has to be a *then*. A paragraph that begins *She added that the store was closed* sends the reader hunting backward for the sentence it was added to.
**Reports:** an extra piece appended to what the same speaker just said. Neutral about the content, but it does commit you to the claim that both statements came from one speaker in one stretch of talk.

> "It's closed. And the lot is full," he said.  →  He said the store was closed and **added that** the lot **was** full.

1. She said the flight was delayed, then *added* that nobody had told the gate agent.
2. "And bring a jacket," he *added*.
3. The senator *added* that she would release the full record next week.
4. He *added* a line at the bottom of the form and initialed it.
5. I should *add* that none of this is official yet.
6. "We'll be late," she said, and then *added*, "very late."

**See also:** [continue](#continue), [go on](#go-on), [mention](#mention), [note](#note)

### advise

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈvaɪz/ &middot; *uhd-VYZE*
**Русский:** уведомить, известить — офиц.; здесь не «советовать», а формально сообщить
**Type:** reporting verb — statement (the *inform* sense) &middot; **Register:** formal — business, legal, and administrative writing
**Patterns:** *advise* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *advise* + **sb** + **of** + noun phrase &middot; *advise* + **sb** + **wh**-clause &middot; *advise* + **that**-clause (impersonal, no listener named) &middot; passive *be advised that* / *be advised of* &middot; *Please advise.* (fixed, standing alone)
**Never:** ***advise me the total*** — no double object. The message attaches with *of*: *advise me **of** the total*. Also never ***advise to me*** — like *tell*, this verb takes the person bare. And an infinitive after it silently switches verbs on you: *advised me **that** the road was closed* is information, *advised me **to take** the freeway* is a recommendation.
**Two different verbs, one spelling:** the sense here is "inform." The much commoner sense — "recommend a course of action," *advised us to leave early* — behaves differently and lives at [advise](05-advise-suggest.md#advise). Only context separates them: *He advised me that the road was closed* is information; *He advised me to take the freeway* is a recommendation.
**Reports:** a formal notification, usually written and usually from an institution. In ordinary American speech it sounds stiff enough to be a small joke — nobody advises a friend that dinner is ready, they tell them.

> "Your policy expires on June 1," the letter said.  →  The letter **advised me that** my policy **would expire** on June 1.

1. Please be *advised* that the office will close at noon on Friday.
2. Counsel *advised* the court that his client would not testify.
3. The bank *advised* us of a change to the account terms.
4. She *advised* the committee when the audit would begin.
5. We will *advise* you as soon as the shipment leaves the warehouse.
6. I've attached the invoice. Please *advise*.

**See also:** [inform](#inform), [notify](#notify), [tell](#tell), [advise (= recommend)](05-advise-suggest.md#advise)

### announce

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnaʊns/ &middot; *uh-NOWNSS*
**Русский:** объявить, огласить — публично или для группы, не одному слушателю
**Type:** reporting verb — public statement &middot; **Register:** neutral, tilting formal
**Patterns:** *announce* + **that**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase &middot; *announce* + **wh**-clause &middot; *announce* + direct quotation &middot; *announce* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *announce* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; passive *it was announced that*
**Never:** ***announce me that*** / ***announce us the results*** — no personal object without *to*. Also ***announce about the merger*** — the topic attaches directly or goes in a clause, never behind *about*. Repairs: *announced the results to us*, *announced to us that the merger was off*, *announced the merger*.
**Word order:** this is one of the few verbs here where the *to*-phrase comfortably precedes the *that*-clause — *announced **to the staff that** the office was moving* is better English than *announced that the office was moving to the staff*, which briefly says the office is moving to the staff.
**Reports:** a statement made publicly, formally, or at least to a group. It presupposes an audience, so it sounds oversized for one listener: *He announced to me that he was hungry* is a joke about self-importance.

> "The merger closed this morning," the CEO said.  →  The CEO **announced that** the merger **had closed that morning**.

1. The airline *announced* that all flights out of Denver were canceled.
2. She *announced* her resignation in a two-line email.
3. The school *announced* to parents that classes would resume Tuesday.
4. Nobody had *announced* who was replacing him.
5. They *announced* the winners in reverse order.
6. He walked in and *announced* that dinner was ruined.

**See also:** [report](#report), [state](#state), [say](#say), [notify](#notify)

### comment

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑmɛnt/ &middot; *KAH-ment*
**Русский:** высказаться, отозваться (о чём-л.) — краткое замечание с оттенком оценки
**Type:** reporting verb — a brief remark, often evaluative &middot; **Register:** neutral, common in journalism
**Patterns:** *comment* + **that**-clause &middot; *comment* + **on** / **upon** + noun phrase &middot; *comment* + **on** + **wh**-clause &middot; *comment* + direct quotation &middot; *comment* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *No comment.* (fixed noun use)
**Never:** ***comment the change*** — the topic attaches with *on*: *commented **on** the change*. Also never ***comment me that***; the listener, if you need one, goes behind *to*: *commented to reporters that…*
**Reports:** a short opinion offered about something already in view. Unlike [remark](#remark), which is neutral about content, *comment* leans slightly toward evaluation — you comment on how something is, not merely that it is.

> "The wording is unusually vague," she said.  →  She **commented that** the wording **was** unusually vague.

1. He *commented* that the second draft was much tighter than the first.
2. Several neighbors *commented* on the noise.
3. The mayor declined to *comment* on the investigation.
4. "Nice of them to tell us," she *commented*.
5. Readers *commented* under the article for three days.
6. She *commented* to a colleague that the numbers looked padded.

**See also:** [remark](#remark), [observe](#observe), [note](#note), [say](#say)

### communicate

**Pronunciation:** /kəˈmjunəˌkeɪt/ &middot; *kuh-MYOO-nuh-kayt*
**Русский:** передать, донести (сообщение) — книжн.; communicate with — общаться, быть на связи
**Type:** reporting verb — transmission of a message &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *communicate* + **that**-clause &middot; *communicate* + noun phrase &middot; *communicate* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *communicate* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *communicate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *communicate* + **with** + sb (intransitive — "be in contact") &middot; passive *be communicated to sb*
**Never:** ***communicate me the results*** — no double object. Repair: *communicate the results **to** me*. Note that *communicate **with** me* is fine but means something else: it is contact, not content.
**Reports:** the successful transfer of a message, by any channel — speech, writing, gesture, a look. It is the most abstract verb in this file and the one most likely to be corporate filler: *We need to communicate that to the team* almost always improves as *We need to tell the team*.

> "The deadline has moved to the 15th," she told the group.  →  She **communicated to the group that** the deadline **had moved** to the 15th.

1. The board *communicated* its decision to shareholders that afternoon.
2. Somehow he *communicated* that he was in pain without saying a word.
3. Please *communicate* any schedule changes to the front desk.
4. The two departments barely *communicate* with each other.
5. She *communicated* what the client wanted far more clearly than the brief did.
6. Nothing about the layoffs was *communicated* until Friday.

**See also:** [convey](#convey), [inform](#inform), [indicate](#indicate), [tell](#tell)

### continue

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈtɪnju/ &middot; *kuhn-TIN-yoo*
**Русский:** продолжить (речь) — заговорить снова после паузы или перерыва
**Type:** reporting verb — resumption after a pause &middot; **Register:** neutral, mostly written narrative
**Patterns:** *continue* + direct quotation (usually as an inverted tag) &middot; *continue* + **that**-clause (journalistic) &middot; *continue* + noun phrase &middot; *continue* + **to**-infinitive &middot; *continue* + **-ing**
**Never:** ***continue me that*** — no personal object. And take care with the last two patterns: *continued to speak* and *continued speaking* report that the talking went on, not what was said. Only the first two patterns report a message.
**Reports:** that the same speaker picked back up after a break — a breath, an interruption, a paragraph. Like [add](#add), it cannot start a report, because there has to be something to continue from.

> "…and that's the whole story," she said. "Or most of it," she continued.  →  She said that **was** the whole story, then **continued that** it **was** really only most of it.

1. "As I was saying," he *continued*, "the numbers don't add up."
2. She paused for a sip of water and *continued*.
3. The witness *continued* that he had never met the defendant before that night.
4. He *continued* reading aloud even after everyone had left.
5. "There's one more thing," she *continued*, quieter now.
6. The report *continues* that no single factor explains the collapse.

**See also:** [go on](#go-on), [add](#add), [repeat](#repeat), [say](#say)

### convey

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈveɪ/ &middot; *kuhn-VAY*
**Русский:** передать, донести (мысль, чувство) — книжн.; важно, что дошло, а не какими словами
**Type:** reporting verb — transmission of content or feeling &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *convey* + **that**-clause &middot; *convey* + noun phrase &middot; *convey* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *convey* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *convey* + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***convey me the message*** / ***convey me that*** — no personal object of any kind without *to*. Repairs: *convey the message **to** me*, *conveyed **to** me that…*
**Reports:** the getting-across of something, with emphasis on what arrived rather than on the words used. It is the verb of choice for content that was never quite stated: *Her tone conveyed that the meeting was over* works, while *Her tone said that the meeting was over* is a metaphor and *Her tone told us* needs the object.
**Nearest neighbor:** [communicate](#communicate) stresses the channel and the act; *convey* stresses the cargo. You communicate with a colleague; you convey sympathy.

> "Please tell them how sorry I am," he said.  →  He asked me to **convey** his apologies **to** them.

1. Her expression *conveyed* that she had already heard.
2. Please *convey* our congratulations to the whole team.
3. The letter *conveyed* the board's decision without explaining it.
4. It's hard to *convey* how loud the room was.
5. He *conveyed* to the family that there was nothing more to be done.
6. The translation loses almost everything the original *conveys*.

**See also:** [communicate](#communicate), [indicate](#indicate), [express](#see-also-note), [tell](#tell)

### describe

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈskraɪb/ &middot; *dih-SKRYBE*
**Русский:** описать, обрисовать — дать словесную картину; describe as — охарактеризовать как
**Type:** reporting verb — an account of something &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *describe* + noun phrase &middot; *describe* + **wh**-clause &middot; *describe* + **how**-clause &middot; *describe* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *describe* + **to** + sb + **how** / **wh**-clause &middot; *describe* + noun phrase + **as** + adjective / noun phrase / **-ing**
**Never:** ***describe me the room*** — the listener attaches with *to*: *describe the room **to** me*. And ***describe that the room was dark*** — *describe* takes no **that**-clause at all. And ***describe about the room*** — the object attaches directly, with no preposition. Repairs: *described the room as dark*, *described how dark the room was*, *described the darkness*.
**Reports:** the giving of an account. Note the **as**-frame, which is where *describe* does its most useful reporting work: *He described the deal as dead* attributes the characterization to him and quietly declines to endorse it.

> "It was maybe twenty feet long, and gray," she said.  →  She **described** the animal **as** about twenty feet long and gray.

1. Can you *describe* what you saw?
2. She *described* the apartment to me over the phone, room by room.
3. The report *describes* the response as slow and poorly coordinated.
4. He *described* how the machine had failed, one step at a time.
5. Witnesses *described* a man in a red jacket.
6. I don't know how to *describe* it — you had to be there.

**See also:** [explain](#explain), [recount](#recount), [relate](#relate), [report](#report), [discuss](#discuss)

### discuss

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈskʌs/ &middot; *dih-SKUSS*
**Русский:** обсуждать (что-л., без предлога) — называет тему разговора, но не его содержание
**Type:** verb of talk — names the topic, not the message &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *discuss* + noun phrase &middot; *discuss* + **wh**-clause &middot; *discuss* + **-ing** &middot; *discuss* + noun phrase + **with** + sb &middot; passive *be discussed*
**Never:** ***discuss about the budget*** — the topic attaches with no preposition at all: *discuss the budget*. This is the most persistent preposition error in English and it survives well into advanced writing, because *talk about*, *argue about*, and *complain about* all take one. Also ***discuss that the budget was tight*** — *discuss* takes no **that**-clause, no **to**-infinitive, and no direct quotation. And ***discuss me the budget*** / ***discuss the budget to me*** — the other person attaches with *with*: *discuss it with me*.
**It reports that talking happened, not what was said.** That is why it cannot do reported speech proper: there is no way to convert a quotation into *discussed*. *"The budget is tight," she said* becomes *She said the budget was tight*, never ~~*She discussed that the budget was tight*~~. What *discuss* can report is the subject matter, and the reader is told nothing about who said what or how it came out.
**Reports:** an exchange of talk on a topic, between two or more people, with no claim about the content, the conclusion, or who held which view.

> "Let's go over the budget." — "Fine. Here's where we are."  →  They **discussed the budget**. (Not ~~*discussed that the budget was tight*~~ — the content is gone.)

1. We *discussed* the budget for nearly two hours and settled nothing.
2. I'd rather *discuss* this in person.
3. She *discussed* the offer with her lawyer before signing anything.
4. They never *discussed* what would happen if the funding fell through.
5. The board *discussed* moving the deadline to March.
6. Salaries are not *discussed* openly here.

**See also:** [share](#share), [communicate](#communicate), [say](#say), [tell](#tell), [describe](#describe)

### explain

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈspleɪn/ &middot; *ik-SPLAYN*
**Русский:** объяснить, растолковать — предполагает, что что-то было неясно
**Type:** reporting verb — statement that makes something understood &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *explain* + **that**-clause &middot; *explain* + **wh**-clause &middot; *explain* + **how**/**why**-clause &middot; *explain* + noun phrase &middot; *explain* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *explain* + **to** + sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *explain* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *explain* (intransitive) &middot; passive *it was explained that*
**Never:** ***explain me something*** / ***explain me that*** / ***explain me how*** — one of the three or four most common errors in English. *Explain* never takes a bare personal object. Nor does it take a person plus an infinitive: ***explained me to wait*** is impossible in every register, because that frame belongs to [tell](#tell). Repairs: *explain it **to** me*, *explain **to** me why*, *explained **to** the class that…*, and for the infinitive, *told me to wait*.
**Word order:** when both a listener and a *that*-clause are present, the listener comes first — *explained **to me that** the machine was old*. Putting the *to*-phrase at the end strands it: *explained that the machine was old to me* reads as though the machine's age belonged to me.
**Reports:** a statement offered to remove confusion. It presupposes that something needed clearing up, which is why it is not fully interchangeable with *say*: *He said the door was locked* is information; *He explained that the door was locked* implies you had been wondering why you couldn't get in.

> "The heater only runs at night," he said.  →  He **explained to me that** the heater only **ran** at night.

1. She *explained* that the deposit wouldn't clear until Monday.
2. Could you *explain* the difference to me?
3. He never *explained* why he left.
4. The manual doesn't *explain* how to reset the timer.
5. I tried to *explain*, but nobody was listening.
6. Let me *explain* it to you one more time.

**See also:** [describe](#describe), [tell](#tell), [say](#say), [point out](#see-also-note)

### go on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ ˈɑn/ &middot; *goh-ON*
**Русский:** продолжить, перейти к следующему — разг. соответствие continue
**Type:** reporting verb — resumption &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational
**Patterns:** *go on* + **to say / to explain / to add / to argue** + **that**-clause &middot; *go on* + direct quotation &middot; *go on* + **-ing** &middot; *go on* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *go on* + **with** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***go on me*** — no personal object. And *go on* + a bare **that**-clause (*He went on that the budget was fixed*) is marginal at best in American English; edited prose writes *went on **to say** that*.
**The two -ing / to-infinitive readings are opposites:**

| Frame | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *went on **to say*** | said the next thing | She finished the summary and *went on to say* the budget was frozen. |
| *went on **saying*** | kept saying the same thing | She *went on saying* the budget was frozen long after it wasn't. |
| *went on **about*** | talked at tedious length | He *went on about* the budget for twenty minutes. |

**Reports:** the same speaker moving to the next thing. It is the spoken counterpart of [continue](#continue) and slightly warmer.

> "That's the first problem. The second is money," he said.  →  He named the first problem and **went on to say that** the second **was** money.

1. He apologized, then *went on* to explain what had happened.
2. "Anyway," she *went on*, "we found the file."
3. The article *goes on* to argue that the policy backfired.
4. She *went on* talking as if nothing had happened.
5. Don't get him started — he'll *go on* about it all night.
6. *Go on*, I'm listening.

**See also:** [continue](#continue), [add](#add), [say](#say)

### if you ask me

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf jə ˈæsk mi/ &middot; *if yuh ASK mee*
**Русский:** по-моему, если хотите знать моё мнение — разг., маркер собственного мнения
**Type:** fixed phrase — opinion marker, not a reporting verb &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken
**Patterns:** *If you ask me*, + clause &middot; clause + , *if you ask me* &middot; parenthetical inside the clause (*The whole thing was, if you ask me, a waste*)
**Never:** ***if you ask to me*** / ***if you ask me that*** — the phrase is frozen. It takes no preposition, no complement, and no tense change; *if you asked me* is a real conditional and a different sentence.
**It is not a conditional and it reports nothing:** nobody has asked. The phrase flags the clause attached to it as the speaker's own unsolicited opinion, usually one that runs against what everyone else is saying, and it carries a faint note of grievance.
**Reports:** nothing anyone else said — it marks the attached clause as the speaker's own opinion, offered without being asked. Where [say](#say) attributes words to someone, this phrase claims them.
**It does not survive being reported.** This is the point worth learning: the phrase belongs to whoever is speaking now, so reporting it forces a paraphrase.

> "It's too expensive, if you ask me," she said.  →  She said **that in her opinion** it was too expensive. / She said **that as far as she was concerned**, it was too expensive.

1. *If you ask me*, the second album was better.
2. They repainted the whole thing, which was a waste of money *if you ask me*.
3. *If you ask me*, nobody read the contract before signing it.
4. He was, *if you ask me*, never really interested in the job.
5. That's not a bug, *if you ask me* — that's a design decision.
6. *If you ask me*, they should have canceled the game.

**See also:** [let me tell you](#let-me-tell-you), [put it](#put-it), [say](#say)

### indicate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪndəˌkeɪt/ &middot; *IN-duh-kayt*
**Русский:** дать понять, указать; свидетельствовать о — книжн., осторожнее, чем «заявить»
**Type:** reporting verb — an indirect or evidential statement &middot; **Register:** formal — academic, journalistic, corporate
**Patterns:** *indicate* + **that**-clause &middot; *indicate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *indicate* + noun phrase &middot; *indicate* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***indicate me that*** — the listener attaches with *to*: *indicated **to** us that…* And ***indicated to step down*** — *indicate* takes no infinitive complement; the plan goes in a **that**-clause: *indicated that he would step down*.
**Two jobs at once:** with a human subject it reports a statement made indirectly or non-verbally — *She indicated that she was willing to settle* means she signaled it, not that she announced it. With a non-human subject it reports evidence: *The data indicate that costs are rising*. The second use is the standard hedge of academic English, and it is deliberately weaker than *show* or *prove*.
**Number note:** in formal American writing *data* takes a plural verb — *the data **indicate***. In ordinary usage *the data indicates* is now common and unremarkable.
**Reports:** a message delivered obliquely, or evidence pointing toward a conclusion. It is deliberately weaker than [state](#state) and [announce](#announce): *indicate* leaves room for the speaker to have meant something else.

> "I might be open to an offer," she said.  →  She **indicated that** she **might** be open to an offer.

1. The survey *indicates* that most respondents never opened the email.
2. He *indicated* to the committee that he would step down in the spring.
3. Her silence *indicated* what she thought of the proposal.
4. Preliminary results *indicate* a much smaller effect than expected.
5. Nothing in the file *indicated* that the payment had been made.
6. Please *indicate* whether you plan to attend.

**See also:** [convey](#convey), [suggest](05-advise-suggest.md#suggest), [report](#report), [note](#note)

### inform

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈfɔrm/ &middot; *in-FORM*
**Русский:** известить, поставить в известность — офиц.; обязателен адресат: inform me of
**Type:** reporting verb — statement delivered to a named listener &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *inform* + **sb** &middot; *inform* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *inform* + **sb** + **of** / **about** + noun phrase &middot; *inform* + **sb** + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *be informed that* / *be informed of*
**Never:** ***inform that the meeting was canceled*** — like *tell*, this verb requires its person. Also ***inform me the change*** — no double object; the message attaches with *of*: *inform me **of** the change*. And never ***inform to me***.
**of vs. about:** *of* is the formal default and takes a fact (*informed us of the delay*); *about* is looser and takes a topic (*informed us about the new procedure*). Neither one can be dropped.
**Reports:** the formal delivery of information to someone who is entitled to it. It is *tell* with a suit on, and it carries a faint institutional chill — *I regret to inform you* is a phrase people brace for.

> "Your application was not successful," the email said.  →  The email **informed me that** my application **had not been** successful.

1. Nobody *informed* the neighbors that the water would be shut off.
2. Please *inform* your supervisor of any schedule change.
3. We regret to *inform* you that the position has been filled.
4. She was never properly *informed* about her options.
5. He *informed* the officer where he had been that evening.
6. Keep me *informed*.

**See also:** [notify](#notify), [advise](#advise), [tell](#tell), [announce](#announce)

### let me tell you

**Pronunciation:** /lɛt mi ˈtɛl ju/ &middot; *let mee TELL yoo* — usually reduced to *LEM-ee tell yuh*
**Русский:** уж поверьте, вот что я скажу — разг. усилитель, а не просьба разрешить
**Type:** fixed phrase — emphasis marker &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken
**Patterns:** *Let me tell you*, + clause &middot; clause + , *let me tell you* &middot; *let me tell you* + **something** &middot; *let me tell you* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *let me tell you* + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***let me to tell you*** — *let* takes a bare infinitive, never *to*. Also ***let me tell to you*** — *tell* takes its person bare, which is the same rule as in the [tell](#tell) entry.
**It is not a request for permission.** Nobody is being asked. The phrase is a pointer that says *what comes next matters*, and with *something* attached it usually introduces a complaint or a warning: *Let me tell you something — that's not how this works.*
**Reports:** nothing that was previously said — it announces the speaker's own next statement and marks it as worth hearing. Compare [tell](#tell), which reports a message that actually reached a listener.
**It does not survive being reported,** for the same reason [if you ask me](#if-you-ask-me) doesn't: the emphasis belongs to the person speaking now. Reporting it means finding a different verb.

> "Let me tell you, that was a long drive," he said.  →  He said **emphatically** that it **had been** a long drive. / He **insisted** it **had been** a long drive.

1. *Let me tell you*, that was the coldest I have ever been.
2. *Let me tell you* something: nobody reads the second page.
3. It wasn't cheap, *let me tell you*.
4. *Let me tell you* about the time our flight got canceled in Denver.
5. And *let me tell you* why that matters.
6. *Let me tell you*, I have never been so glad to see a rest stop.

**See also:** [if you ask me](#if-you-ask-me), [tell](#tell), [put it](#put-it)

### mention

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɛnʃən/ &middot; *MEN-shuhn*
**Русский:** упомянуть — сказать вскользь, мимоходом, без нажима
**Type:** reporting verb — a passing statement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *mention* + **that**-clause &middot; *mention* + noun phrase &middot; *mention* + **sb** (as a topic, never as a listener) &middot; *mention* + **wh**-clause &middot; *mention* + **-ing** &middot; *mention* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *mention* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; impersonal *it is worth mentioning that*
**Never:** ***mention about the meeting*** — *mention* takes its object directly, with no preposition: *mentioned the meeting*. This is a stubborn error, probably by analogy with *talk about* and *tell someone about*. Also ***mention me that*** — repair with *mention it to me*, or *mentioned to me that…* Note the trap in the bare-object frame: *He mentioned you* means you were the subject of the remark, not the person who heard it.
**Reports:** something brought up briefly and without emphasis. The word itself is a claim about weight: *He mentioned that he was leaving* says it came up in passing, and if he actually made a speech about it, *mention* is the wrong verb. That is also why it works as an apology in advance — *I should mention that I'll be late* softens the news by pretending it is minor.

> "Oh, and I'm out on Friday," she said.  →  She **mentioned that** she **would be** out on Friday.

1. He *mentioned* that his sister lives about an hour from here.
2. Did anyone *mention* the parking situation?
3. She *mentioned* seeing him at the conference last spring.
4. I don't think he ever *mentioned* what he did before this.
5. Don't *mention* it to Dave — he'll worry.
6. It's worth *mentioning* that the warranty expired in March.

**See also:** [note](#note), [remark](#remark), [add](#add), [say](#say)

### note

**Pronunciation:** /noʊt/ &middot; *NOHT*
**Русский:** отметить, обратить внимание — книжн.; подаёт сказанное как установленный факт
**Type:** reporting verb — a statement of a fact taken as given &middot; **Register:** formal, mostly written
**Patterns:** *note* + **that**-clause &middot; *note* + noun phrase &middot; *note* + **wh**-clause &middot; *note* + direct quotation (as a tag) &middot; passive *it should be noted that* / *it is worth noting that*
**Never:** ***note me that*** — no bare personal object. The *to*-form (*noted **to** reporters that…*) does turn up in journalism, but it is far rarer than with [remark](#remark) or [comment](#comment); in most writing *note* assumes a reader rather than addressing one.
**It presupposes truth.** This is the one place *note* is not neutral: *He noted that attendance was down* takes it as established that attendance was down, and reports him as calling attention to a fact. Compare *He claimed that attendance was down*, which does the opposite. If the point is contested, *note* quietly takes a side.
**Reports:** the act of registering something for the record — observing it, and expecting the reader to accept it.

> "Attendance is down about nine percent," the report said.  →  The report **noted that** attendance **was** down about nine percent.

1. The judge *noted* that neither party had filed on time.
2. It should be *noted* that these figures exclude part-time employees.
3. She *noted* the discrepancy in the margin and moved on.
4. Critics *noted* how little had changed since the first audit.
5. Please *note* that the building has no visitor parking.
6. He *noted*, correctly, that we had no budget for any of this.

**See also:** [observe](#observe), [remark](#remark), [mention](#mention), [indicate](#indicate)

### notify

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊtəˌfaɪ/ &middot; *NOH-tuh-fye*
**Русский:** уведомить — офиц., юр.: официальное извещение, положенное по правилам или договору
**Type:** reporting verb — formal notification &middot; **Register:** formal — administrative, legal
**Patterns:** *notify* + **sb** &middot; *notify* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *notify* + **sb** + **of** + noun phrase &middot; *notify* + **sb** + **of** + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *be notified that* / *be notified of*
**Never:** ***notify that the class was canceled*** — the person is required. And ***notify us the date*** — no double object; repair with *notify us **of** the date*. Also ***notify to me***: like *tell*, this verb takes its person bare. One more line separates it from [inform](#inform) — *notify* will not take a bare **wh**-clause: ~~*notify us what you decide*~~ → *notify us **of** what you decide*, where *inform us what you decide* is perfectly good. (A *when*-clause after *notify* is a time adverbial, not the message: *notifies you when a payment fails* says when the notice arrives, not what it says.)
**Reports:** an official act of informing, usually one that some rule or contract requires. It differs from [inform](#inform) in that *notify* often stands alone with no message at all — *Notify the family* is a complete instruction, while *Inform the family* invites the question "of what?"

> "You'll hear from us within ten days," they said.  →  They said we **would be notified** within ten days.

1. The school *notified* parents that the trip had been canceled.
2. Tenants must *notify* the landlord of any change of address.
3. We were never *notified*.
4. *Notify* the on-call engineer immediately.
5. She was *notified* by certified mail on the 12th.
6. The system *notifies* you when a payment fails.

**See also:** [inform](#inform), [advise](#advise), [announce](#announce), [tell](#tell)

### observe

**Pronunciation:** /əbˈzɚv/ &middot; *uhb-ZURV*
**Русский:** заметить (вслух) — книжн., замечание как итог наблюдения; в другой рамке — наблюдать
**Type:** reporting verb — a considered remark &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *observe* + **that**-clause &middot; *observe* + direct quotation &middot; *observe* + noun phrase &middot; *observe* + **wh**-clause &middot; *observe* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause (literary) &middot; *observe* + **sb** + **-ing** / bare infinitive (the *watch* sense) &middot; passive *it has often been observed that*
**Never:** ***observe me that*** — no bare personal object in the speech sense. The literary *to*-form is available — *observed **to** her sister that…* — but the bare object never is.
**Two verbs, and the frames tell them apart:** with a **that**-clause or a quotation it means "said, having noticed" — *He observed that the door was open*. With a person plus **-ing** it means "watched" — *He observed her leaving*. There is no frame where the two readings compete, which is why the ambiguity almost never causes trouble in practice.
**Reports:** a remark presented as the product of noticing something. Drier and more deliberate than [remark](#remark), and unlike [note](#note) it is a spoken act, not a written one.

> "Nobody here has read the contract," he said.  →  He **observed that** nobody there **had read** the contract.

1. "You're up early," she *observed*.
2. He *observed* that the two accounts contradicted each other on every important point.
3. One reviewer *observed* how carefully the film avoids explaining itself.
4. She *observed* the whole exchange from across the room.
5. Visitors may *observe* the class but may not participate.
6. It has often been *observed* that committees expand to fill the time available.

**See also:** [remark](#remark), [note](#note), [comment](#comment), [say](#say)

### put it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpʊt ɪt/ &middot; *PUUT it*
**Русский:** выразиться, сформулировать — as he put it «как он выразился»: о словах, а не о факте
**Type:** reporting verb — attribution of wording &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *as* + sb + *put(s) it* &middot; *to put it* + adverb (*bluntly, mildly, simply, another way*) &middot; *put it* + *this way / like this / another way* &middot; *put it* + **that**-clause (formal and rare) &middot; *put it* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause (chiefly British, in questioning)
**Never:** ***as she put*** — the *it* is not optional and never changes: not *as she put them*, not *as she put the matter*. The whole expression is frozen around that one pronoun.
**Reports:** the **wording**, not the fact. *As he put it, the project was "resting"* attributes the phrasing to him and quietly declines to endorse it — the same move as scare quotes, done with a verb. That is why *as he put it* so often precedes a euphemism.

> "We're pausing the rollout," she said.  →  The rollout was, **as she put it**, "paused."

1. The building is, as the listing *puts it*, "full of character."
2. To *put it* mildly, the presentation did not go well.
3. Let me *put it* another way: there is no money.
4. He was, as his coach *put it*, "not a natural."
5. To *put it* bluntly, nobody wants the job.
6. She *put it* better than I could have.

**See also:** [quote](#quote), [describe](#describe), [say](#say), [if you ask me](#if-you-ask-me)

### quote

**Pronunciation:** /kwoʊt/ &middot; *KWOHT*
**Русский:** цитировать, приводить дословно — единственный здесь глагол, обещающий точность слов
**Type:** reporting verb — verbatim attribution &middot; **Register:** neutral, journalistic
**Patterns:** *quote* + **sb** &middot; *quote* + noun phrase &middot; *quote* + direct quotation &middot; *quote* + **from** + noun phrase &middot; *quote* + **sb** + **as saying** / **as calling** / **as describing** &middot; *quote* + **sb** + **on** + topic &middot; passive *be quoted as saying that…*
**Never:** ***quote that the deal was dead*** — *quote* takes no **that**-clause on its own, and no personal object plus a clause either: ***quoted me that the deal was dead***. Repairs: *quoted him **as saying** that the deal was dead*, *quoted him: "The deal is dead."*
**It is the one verb here that claims exactness.** Every other verb in this file reports content; *quote* reports words. Getting them wrong is not a style problem — misquoting someone is a factual error and, in journalism, a correctable one. Use [say](#say) or [state](#state) when you are paraphrasing.
**The passive is the workhorse:** *was quoted as saying* is the standard newspaper frame for a statement the reporter did not personally hear.
**Reports:** someone's exact words, reproduced. The judgment it carries is a promise of accuracy rather than an opinion about the content — *quote* says nothing about whether the speaker was right, only that this is what came out of their mouth.

> "This deal is dead," he told reporters.  →  He **was quoted as saying** the deal **was** dead.

1. The article *quotes* two former employees who describe the same pattern.
2. She was *quoted* as calling the decision "indefensible."
3. Don't *quote* me on the exact number.
4. He *quoted* his grandmother at least once an hour.
5. The study is *quoted* far more often than it is read.
6. Can I *quote* you on that?

**See also:** [put it](#put-it), [report](#report), [state](#state), [say](#say)

### recount

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈkaʊnt/ &middot; *rih-KOWNT*
**Русский:** подробно рассказать, изложить по порядку — книжн., о череде событий
**Type:** reporting verb — extended narrative &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *recount* + noun phrase &middot; *recount* + **how**-clause &middot; *recount* + **wh**-clause &middot; *recount* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *recount* + **to** + sb + **how**-clause
**Never:** ***recount me the story*** — the listener attaches with *to*: *recounted the story **to** me*. And *recount* + a plain **that**-clause (*He recounted that he had been fired*) is stiff and rare; native writers use a **how**-clause or a noun phrase instead: *recounted **how** he had been fired*.
**Stress matters:** /rɪˈkaʊnt/ with second-syllable stress is this verb. /ˈriːkaʊnt/ with first-syllable stress is the election noun — *a recount* — and the verb meaning "count again."
**Reports:** the telling of a sequence of events at length and in order. Its object is an episode, not a proposition, which is exactly why the **that**-clause fits so badly.

> "So I got there, and the office was already locked, and…" she said.  →  She **recounted how** she **had arrived** to find the office already locked.

1. He *recounted* the entire trip, hour by hour.
2. She *recounted* how the two of them had met.
3. The memoir *recounts* his years in Chicago.
4. Survivors *recounted* what they had seen to a federal panel.
5. I've heard him *recount* that story a dozen times.
6. The chapter *recounts* the strike from the union's side.

**See also:** [relate](#relate), [describe](#describe), [report](#report), [tell](#tell)

### relate

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈleɪt/ &middot; *rih-LAYT*
**Русский:** поведать, изложить — книжн., устаревающее; ср. relate to — относиться к, сопереживать
**Type:** reporting verb — orderly telling &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *relate* + noun phrase &middot; *relate* + **that**-clause &middot; *relate* + **how** / **wh**-clause &middot; *relate* + noun phrase + **to** + sb
**Never:** ***relate me the story*** — no double object; repair with *relate the story **to** me*.
**Three unrelated verbs share this spelling,** and only the first is a reporting verb: *relate a story* (tell), *relate **to** something* (be connected with — *questions relating to the budget*), and *relate **to** someone* (identify with — *I can relate*). The third is informal American English and now far more common in speech than the reporting sense, which is why *He related the incident* sounds distinctly bookish today.
**Reports:** a full, orderly account given to someone. Slightly more formal than [recount](#recount) and slightly less committed to length.

> "It started when the alarm didn't go off," he said.  →  He **related how** the whole day **had started** with an alarm that didn't go off.

1. She *related* the events of that morning to the investigators.
2. He *related* that the two companies had been in talks for a year.
3. The book *relates* the founding of the town in some detail.
4. Strange as it may seem, he *related* the whole thing without once raising his voice.
5. The witness *related* what she had heard through the wall.
6. Ask him to *relate* the story about the ferry.

**See also:** [recount](#recount), [describe](#describe), [report](#report), [tell](#tell)

### remark

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈmɑrk/ &middot; *rih-MARK*
**Русский:** заметить, обронить — короткое замечание вслух; тему вводит предлог on
**Type:** reporting verb — a brief spoken observation &middot; **Register:** neutral, tilting literary
**Patterns:** *remark* + **that**-clause &middot; *remark* + direct quotation &middot; *remark* + **on** / **upon** + noun phrase &middot; *remark* + **on** + **wh**-clause &middot; *remark* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***he remarked the change*** — the topic takes *on*: *remarked **on** the change*. (The bare-object use survives only in the archaic sense "notice," as in *a change no one remarked*.) Also ***remark me that***.
**Reports:** a short, unweighted comment made out loud, often in passing. Compared with its neighbors: [observe](#observe) is more deliberate, [comment](#comment) is more evaluative, [note](#note) is written and presupposes truth, and *remark* is the plainest of the four — it reports that something was said aloud and stops there.

> "It's colder than it was last week," she said.  →  She **remarked that** it **was** colder than it **had been** the week before.

1. "You've been quiet," he *remarked*.
2. She *remarked* that the parking lot was already half empty at four.
3. Several people *remarked* on how much the neighborhood had changed.
4. He *remarked* to a friend that he'd never seen the boss so cheerful.
5. Somebody *remarked* that the coffee tasted like the pot it came in.
6. It's rude to *remark* on what other people are eating.

**See also:** [comment](#comment), [observe](#observe), [note](#note), [mention](#mention)

### repeat

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈpit/ &middot; *rih-PEET*
**Русский:** повторить — сказать во второй раз, часто с оттенком настойчивости
**Type:** reporting verb — restatement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *repeat* + **that**-clause &middot; *repeat* + noun phrase &middot; *repeat* + direct quotation &middot; *repeat* + **wh**-clause &middot; *repeat* + noun phrase + **to** / **for** + sb &middot; *repeat* + **oneself**
**Never:** ***repeat me the question*** — no double object. Repairs: *repeat the question **for** me*, *Could you repeat that?* The bare *Repeat that, please* is the ordinary spoken form.
**Redundancy watch:** *repeat again* is a double, since *repeat* already contains the "again." It is common in speech and universally cut in editing. Say *repeat* or say *said it again*, not both.
**Reports:** a statement made a second time, which carries a small implication of insistence or of not having been heard the first time — *She repeated that she wasn't going* is a stronger sentence than *She said again that she wasn't going*.

> "I'm not signing it. I am not signing it," he said.  →  He **repeated that** he **was not** going to sign it.

1. She *repeated* that the deadline had not moved.
2. Could you *repeat* the last part?
3. He *repeated* the address back to the dispatcher.
4. Don't *repeat* this to anyone.
5. The recording *repeats* the same three instructions in a loop.
6. I'll *repeat* it for the people in the back.

### report

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈpɔrt/ &middot; *rih-PORT*
**Русский:** сообщить, доложить — нейтральная передача сведений: журн. и офиц. основной глагол
**Type:** reporting verb — an account given to someone entitled to it &middot; **Register:** neutral, journalistic and institutional
**Patterns:** *report* + **that**-clause &middot; *report* + noun phrase &middot; *report* + **-ing** &middot; *report* + **wh**-clause &middot; *report* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *report* + **sb** + **as** + **-ing** / adjective &middot; *report* + **on** + noun phrase &middot; *report* + **sb** + **to** + sb (denunciation — see below) &middot; passive *it was reported that* / *sb is reported to have*
**Never:** ***report me that*** — the listener attaches with *to*: *reported **to** the board that…* And ***report about the findings*** — the topic takes *on*: *reported **on** the findings*.
**Watch the other senses.** *Report someone to someone* is a denunciation, not a report of speech — *She reported him to HR* has nothing to do with what he said. And *report to* plus a person also means "have as a supervisor": *He reports to the CFO*.
**Reports:** the passing along of information, and it is scrupulously neutral about truth — the reporter neither endorses the content nor doubts it. That neutrality is the reason it dominates journalism, where *claimed* would editorialize and *revealed* would concede the point.

> "Sales fell about four percent last quarter," the company said.  →  The company **reported that** sales **had fallen** about four percent the previous quarter.

1. Two papers *reported* that the talks had collapsed.
2. She *reported* the outage to the utility just after midnight.
3. Residents *reported* hearing a loud bang around two in the morning.
4. He is *reported* to have left the country in June.
5. The committee *reported* on its findings in November.
6. Nobody ever *reported* what happened to the missing equipment.

**See also:** [state](#state), [announce](#announce), [say](#say), [quote](#quote), [indicate](#indicate)

### say

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ/ &middot; *SAY* — past and participle **said** /sɛd/ &middot; *SED*, third person **says** /sɛz/ &middot; *SEZ*
**Русский:** сказать — нейтральный глагол речи; адресат только через to, «say me» невозможно
**Type:** reporting verb — statement (the default) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *say* + **that**-clause &middot; *say* + direct quotation &middot; *say* + noun phrase (short fixed list) &middot; *say* + **to** + sb (+ **that**-clause) &middot; *say* + **wh**-clause (mostly under negation or a modal: *I couldn't say what he meant*) &middot; *say* + **to**-infinitive with no object (spoken American English only — see the footnote below)
**Never:** ***say me*** / ***say someone something*** / ***say me that you're sorry*** — *say* takes **no personal object**, ever, in any register. Repairs: *say **to** me*, or switch verbs to *tell me*. This and its mirror image under [tell](#tell) are the two errors an American reader notices instantly.
**Pronunciation trap:** *said* rhymes with *bed*, not with *paid*, and *says* rhymes with *fez*, not with *days*. The vowel changes even though the spelling doesn't.
**say to vs. tell:** *say to someone* is perfectly grammatical, but when a listener is named, *tell* is far more idiomatic. Reserve *say to* for when the listener is contrasted or stressed: *He didn't say a word to **me***.
**The fixed noun objects:** *say hello, say goodbye, say yes, say no, say thank you, say grace, say a few words, say nothing, say so, needless to say, say the word, that is to say*. None of them needs quotation marks — *She said hello* is complete as it stands.
**The honest footnote:** objectless *say* + **to**-infinitive — *He said to wait. She said not to touch it.* — is extremely common in spoken American English and is what most Americans would actually produce. Edited prose still prefers *He told us to wait*. What is never possible in any register is the personal object: ~~*He said me to wait*~~. See [Reported commands and requests](../06-commands-requests.md).
**Reports:** that words were produced. It is the unmarked, weightless default — the verb you use when you have no reason to use any other, and the one that adds nothing at all to the message.

> "I'm not coming," she said.  →  She **said (that) she wasn't** coming.
> "I'll call you tomorrow," she said.  →  She said she **would** call me **the next day**.

1. He *said* he'd be here by six.
2. "That's not what I meant," she *said*.
3. She didn't *say* a word to anyone all afternoon.
4. What did the doctor *say*?
5. The sign *says* the pool closes at nine.
6. I couldn't *say* why he changed his mind.
7. Just *say* the word and we'll leave.

**See also:** [tell](#tell), [state](#state), [mention](#mention), [remark](#remark), [report](#report), [The basics: say, tell, ask, speak, talk](../01-basics.md)

### share

**Pronunciation:** /ʃɛr/ &middot; *SHAIR*
**Русский:** поделиться (новостью, информацией) — адресат через with, не to; корпоративный оттенок
**Type:** reporting verb — statement offered to a group &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a workplace and therapeutic flavor
**Patterns:** *share* + **that**-clause &middot; *share* + noun phrase &middot; *share* + noun phrase + **with** + sb &middot; *share* + **with** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *share* + **wh**-clause &middot; *share* (intransitive — *Would anyone like to share?*)
**Never:** ***share me the news*** — no double object. And ***share the news to me*** — this verb takes **with**, not *to*: *share the news **with** me*. The *to* version is spreading in some varieties of English and in social-media usage (*share it to your story*), but American edited prose still requires *with* for a listener.
**Register warning, said plainly:** *share that* has become a soft corporate and therapeutic substitute for *tell* and *say* — *I wanted to share that we're restructuring*. It is completely grammatical, and plenty of readers find it evasive, because it dresses an announcement up as a gift. In neutral prose, *tell* and *say* are usually better.
**Reports:** the offering of information to a person or group, framed as generous or voluntary.

> "I've decided to take the job," she told the group.  →  She **shared with the group that** she **had decided** to take the job.

1. He *shared* the news with his family before it went public.
2. She *shared* that she'd been out of work for most of the year.
3. Would anyone like to *share* before we wrap up?
4. I'll *share* the document with you this afternoon.
5. Nobody *shared* what had been discussed in the closed session.
6. Thanks for *sharing* that with us.

**See also:** [tell](#tell), [say](#say), [mention](#mention), [communicate](#communicate), [discuss](#discuss)

### state

**Pronunciation:** /steɪt/ &middot; *STAYT*
**Русский:** заявить, констатировать — офиц., «под запись»; в отличие от claim, нейтрально к истине
**Type:** reporting verb — a deliberate, on-the-record statement &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *state* + **that**-clause &middot; *state* + noun phrase &middot; *state* + **wh**-clause &middot; *state* + direct quotation &middot; *state* + **to** + sb + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it is stated that*
**Never:** ***state me that*** — no personal object; use *state **to** the court that…* Also ***state about the delay*** — *state* takes its object directly or takes a clause, never *about*.
**Reports:** a statement made explicitly and for the record — heavier and more deliberate than [say](#say), and neutral about truth in a way *claim* is not. If someone writes *He stated that he was home all evening*, they are reporting a formal assertion without commenting on it; *He claimed that he was home all evening* invites you to doubt it.
**The weakness of overuse:** in student writing *state* is often substituted for *say* to sound serious, and it backfires. *My mother stated that dinner was ready* is comic. Save it for occasions that genuinely have a record.

> "I was at home all evening," he said.  →  He **stated that** he **had been** at home all evening.

1. The contract *states* that either party may cancel with thirty days' notice.
2. She *stated* her objection for the record.
3. Please *state* your name and occupation.
4. The memo *stated* clearly that no exceptions would be made.
5. He *stated* to investigators that he had never seen the documents.
6. It is *stated* nowhere in the policy that overtime must be pre-approved.

**See also:** [say](#say), [report](#report), [announce](#announce), [note](#note), [quote](#quote)

### tell

**Pronunciation:** /tɛl/ &middot; *TELL* — past and participle **told** /toʊld/ &middot; *TOHLD*
**Русский:** сказать, рассказать кому-л. — требует адресата без предлога: tell me, не «tell to me»
**Type:** reporting verb — statement, command, or information delivered to a named listener &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *tell* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *tell* + **sb** + noun phrase (double object) &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **wh**-clause &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **of** + noun phrase (formal) &middot; *tell* + **sb** + direct quotation (as a tag) &middot; *tell* + noun phrase (fixed objects only — see below) &middot; passive *be told that* / *be told to*
**Never:** ***tell that the road was closed*** — the person is required; repair with *tell **me** that the road was closed*. And ***tell to me*** — the person attaches bare, with no preposition. A direct quotation cannot rescue it either: ~~*"I'm late," he told.*~~ → *"I'm late," he told **me***.
**The fixed objects — *tell* with no listener.** A short, closed list where *tell* takes a non-person object and names nobody:

| Expression | Example |
|---|---|
| tell the truth | Just *tell the truth*. |
| tell a lie | He's *telling a lie* and he knows it. |
| tell a story / a joke | She *told a story* about her first job. |
| tell time | He learned to *tell time* in kindergarten. (British: *tell **the** time*) |
| tell the difference | I can't *tell the difference* between the two files. |
| tell A from B | Can you *tell* margarine *from* butter? |
| tell fortunes | Her aunt *tells fortunes* at the county fair. |
| tell tales | Stop *telling tales* about your brother. |
| you never can tell | It might work. *You never can tell*. |
| to tell (you) the truth | *To tell you the truth*, I forgot. |

**The other verb:** in several of those, *tell* does not mean "say" at all — it means **distinguish** or **know**: *I can't tell if it's working. You can tell she's nervous.* That sense takes no listener and no message, and it never appears in reported speech.
**The passive is everywhere:** *I was told the office had moved. We were told to wait.* Because English promotes the person object, the passive of *tell* is one of the commonest ways to report a statement whose source you can't or won't name.
**Reports:** the delivery of a message to a specific person. Where [say](#say) reports that words existed, *tell* reports that they reached someone — which is why it, and not *say*, carries commands: *told me to sit down*.

> "The road is closed," he said to me.  →  He **told me (that)** the road **was** closed.
> "Sit down," she said.  →  She **told me to sit down**.

1. She *told* me the meeting had moved to Thursday.
2. Nobody *told* us where to park.
3. He *told* the kids to stay off the ice.
4. *Tell* me about the new place.
5. I was *told* the check had already been mailed.
6. You can *tell* she's done this before.
7. To *tell* you the truth, I'd rather stay home.

**See also:** [say](#say), [inform](#inform), [notify](#notify), [advise](#advise), [share](#share), [let me tell you](#let-me-tell-you), [Reported commands and requests](../06-commands-requests.md)

### write

**Pronunciation:** /raɪt/ &middot; *RYTE* — past **wrote** /roʊt/ &middot; *ROHT*, participle **written** /ˈrɪtən/ &middot; *RIT-n*
**Русский:** написать, сообщить письменно — в амер. англ. допустимо write me без предлога to
**Type:** reporting verb — statement made in writing &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *write* + **that**-clause &middot; *write* + noun phrase &middot; *write* + direct quotation &middot; *write* + **sb** + **that**-clause (American English) &middot; *write* + **to** + sb &middot; *write* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *write* + **wh**-clause &middot; *write* + noun phrase + **to** + sb &middot; *write* + **saying that**…
**American/British split — and here American English is the looser one:**

| | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Person alone | *I'll write you next week.* | *I'll write **to** you next week.* |
| Person + message | *She wrote me that she'd arrived.* | *She wrote **to** me to say she'd arrived.* |
| With *to* | Also fine: *I'll write to you.* | Required |

*Write me* is standard American English, not an error, and it is the one verb in this file that takes a bare personal object besides *tell* and its formal relatives.
**Never:** ***write me to come*** — unlike *tell*, this verb does not take a person plus a **to**-infinitive. Repairs: *wrote **to tell me to** come*, *wrote **that I should** come*.
**Reports:** a statement made in writing, which is the only thing separating it from [say](#say). Everything about backshift and pronoun shift works identically — see [Backshift](../02-backshift.md).

> "I've arrived safely," she wrote.  →  She **wrote that** she **had arrived** safely.

1. He *wrote* that the apartment was smaller than the pictures suggested.
2. She *wrote* me twice from Lisbon and then went quiet.
3. *Write* to your representative if you feel strongly about it.
4. The critic *wrote* that the second act saves the play.
5. He *wrote* a long email explaining exactly what had gone wrong.
6. She never *wrote* back.

**See also:** [say](#say), [report](#report), [state](#state), [note](#note), [Backshift](../02-backshift.md)

## <a id="see-also-note"></a>A note on cross-references

Two verbs turn up in the *See also* lines above without entries of their own in this file, because they are not neutral. **Express** (*expressed regret*, *expressed doubt*) reports a feeling rather than a proposition, which puts it with the verbs of [emotion and evaluation](07-emotion.md) rather than here. **Point out** presupposes that what was said is true, which is a judgment — *He pointed out that the form was late* concedes that the form was late, while *He said the form was late* does not; it is catalogued at [point out](08-ask-explain.md#point-out).

The same test sorts the whole dictionary. Ask what the verb commits *you*, the reporter, to. *Say*, *state*, *report*, and *mention* commit you to nothing but the fact that words were produced. *Note* and *point out* commit you to the truth of the content. *Claim* and *allege* commit you to doubting it. *Admit* and *confess* commit you to the premise that there was a fault to own. Only the first group belongs in this file.
