# Asking, wondering, explaining, and concluding

> The verbs that report a question, an explanation, or the conclusion somebody drew — everything a speaker does with information other than simply assert it.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

Three speech acts share one piece of syntax here. A verb that reports a question takes an **embedded question** — a **wh**-clause or an **if/whether**-clause with statement word order and no question mark (*She asked where the office **was***, never ***where was the office***). A verb that reports an explanation or a conclusion takes a **that**-clause, and almost all of them take a **wh**-clause too, which is why *explain what happened* and *explain that nothing happened* are equally good. The trap that runs through the whole group is the **personal object**: *tell*, *show*, and *ask* take one directly (*told me what happened*, *show me how it works*), while *explain*, *point out*, *demonstrate*, *indicate*, and *inquire* refuse it outright — ***explain me*** is wrong in every register of American English, and the repair is always a preposition (*explain **to** me*). The conclusion verbs add one more dimension the question verbs do not have: how much the reporter is willing to bet on the content, running from *prove* and *point out*, which take the statement as true, through *think* and *gather*, down to *speculate*, which offers it as a guess.

**Which verbs take a personal object.** This one line decides half the errors in the group.

| Verb | *V* + **sb** + clause | *V* + **to sb** + clause | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| *tell* | ✓ *told me what happened* | ✗ ***told to me*** | — |
| *show* | ✓ *showed me what happened* | ✓ *showed it to me* | — |
| *ask* | ✓ *asked me where it was* | ✗ ***asked to me*** | — |
| *explain* | ✗ ***explained me*** | ✓ *explained to me what happened* | add *to* |
| *point out* | ✗ ***pointed me out that*** | ✓ *pointed out to me that…* | add *to* |
| *demonstrate* | ✗ ***demonstrated me*** | ✓ *demonstrated to me that…* | add *to* |
| *indicate* | ✗ ***indicated me*** | ✓ *indicated to me that…* | add *to* |
| *inquire* | ✗ ***inquired me*** | ✓ *inquired of me whether…* (formal) | use *ask* |
| *say* | ✗ ***said me*** | ✓ *said to me that…* | use *tell* |

*Tell* is the mirror image of *explain*: it **requires** the personal object, so ***He told that he was late*** ✗ is exactly as wrong as ***He explained me that he was late*** ✗. The repairs run in opposite directions — *told **me** that he was late* ✓ and *explained **to me** that he was late* ✓.

Three more groups sit behind that table. The **restating verbs** — *clarify*, *summarize*, *recap*, *paraphrase*, *spell out*, *repeat* — put the beneficiary on **for**, not *to*, and never bare: *summarize the report **for** me* ✓, ***summarize me the report*** ✗. The **person-only verbs** — *question*, *quiz*, *interrogate*, *consult* — take the **person** as their object and no clause of their own at all, so the subject matter has to arrive on *about*: *quizzed her **about** the flight* ✓, ***quizzed whether the flight was on time*** ✗. And the **thinking verbs** — *think*, *believe*, *suppose*, *assume*, *gather*, *reckon*, *figure*, *wonder* — have no hearer slot of any kind, because nothing was necessarily said to anybody.

**Which verbs take an embedded question.** Most verbs here that take a **that**-clause take a **wh**-clause too, but not all of them, and the exceptions are a tidy set worth memorizing.

| Takes both **that** and **wh** | **that**-clause only | Question clause only (no **that**) |
|---|---|---|
| *explain, clarify, show, demonstrate, indicate, point out, spell out, prove, calculate, estimate, deduce, infer, predict, forecast, understand* | *assume, believe, suppose, gather, think, conclude, reason, reckon, figure, argue, speculate, echo, reiterate, repeat* | *ask, wonder, inquire, query, question, want to know, demand to know, recap, summarize* |

The middle column is the one that trips learners: ***I assumed where he had gone*** ✗, ***She concluded what had gone wrong*** ✗, ***I gather where he went*** ✗. Those verbs report a settled proposition, and a question is not one — the repair is either a **that**-clause (*concluded that the seal had failed*) or a different verb (*worked out what had gone wrong*). Several of them will accept a question on a preposition instead: *argued **about** whether to go* ✓, *speculated **about** what had happened* ✓. The right-hand column is the reverse — a question verb needs a question, so ***She asked that the office was closed*** ✗ and ***He wondered that she was home*** ✗ (except in the literary "be surprised" sense of *wonder*).

One thing that looks like an embedded question but is not: *repeat what he said*, *echo what she had said*, *paraphrase what the memo said*, *reiterate what she said in June*. That **what**-clause is a **free relative** — it means "the thing he said," a noun phrase — and it is available to every verb in the table, including the ones that refuse questions. The test is whether you can substitute a plain noun phrase: *repeat **the question*** ✓ proves it is a free relative; ***repeat whether he had met the man*** ✗ proves *repeat* still takes no real question.

**How much the verb commits the reporter.** Choosing among these verbs is not a neutral act. Several of them state the **reporter's** own position on the content, not just the speaker's, and a learner who picks by meaning alone will editorialize by accident.

| Verb | What you commit to by using it |
|---|---|
| *prove* | the content is **true** and the question is closed |
| *show*, *demonstrate* | the content is **true**, and the evidence is on display |
| *point out* | the content is **true**, and worth noticing |
| *explain*, *clarify* | the content is **true**, and it resolves something |
| *reiterate*, *repeat*, *echo* | the content was **said before** (*echo* adds agreement and hints the speaker added nothing) |
| *indicate* | someone **gestured at** it without committing to it |
| *say*, *argue*, *predict*, *ask* | nothing either way |
| *assume* | the speaker **skipped the evidence** — a quiet accusation |
| *speculate* | it is **a guess**, and the reporter is labeling it one |
| *claim*, *allege* ([see Asserting](02-assert.md)) | the reporter **does not vouch** for it |

Write *He pointed out that the figures were wrong* and you have said the figures were wrong. Write *He claimed that the figures were wrong* and you have not. Write *He assumed the figures were wrong* and you have accused him of not checking. The same trap runs the other way with *understand* and *gather*, which pass a claim along while quietly declining to stand behind it.

### argue

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑrɡju/ &middot; *AR-gyoo*
**Type:** reporting verb — reasoned claim &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *argue* + **that**-clause &middot; *argue* + **for / against** + noun phrase &middot; *argue* + **for / against** + **-ing** &middot; *argue* + **about / over** + noun phrase &middot; *argue* + **about / over** + **wh**-clause &middot; *argue* + **with** + person &middot; *argue* + sb + **into / out of** + **-ing** &middot; *argue* + noun phrase (*argue a case*, *argue the point*) &middot; passive *it is argued that*
**Never:** ***argue someone to do something*** and ***argue someone that*** — *argue* takes no personal object in front of a clause. The verb for talking a person into something is *persuade* or *convince*: *He argued that we should wait* ✓ / *He persuaded us to wait* ✓ / ***He argued us to wait*** ✗. The one genuine personal-object frame is *argue* + sb + **into / out of** + **-ing**, and it reports persuasion rather than a claim: *She argued him out of resigning* ✓. And no bare question clause — ***argue why the plan would fail*** ✗, ***argue whether to go*** ✗ — the question has to ride in on a preposition: *argued about why the plan would fail* ✓, *argued over whether to go* ✓.
**Reports:** a position put forward with reasons attached to it. *Claim* signals the reporter's doubt and *prove* concedes the case; *argue* is neutral on whether the reasoning worked, reporting only that reasoning was offered.

> "The data is too thin to act on," she said.  →  She **argued that** the data **was** too thin to act on.
> "We should hire before January," he said.  →  He **argued for** hiring before January.

1. The defense *argued* that the search had been illegal from the start.
2. Economists *argue* that the credit mostly benefits people who would have bought anyway.
3. She *argued* for a small pilot before anyone committed to a full rollout.
4. They spent an hour *arguing* about whether the deposit was refundable.
5. Nobody *argued* that the old system was better — only that it was cheaper.
6. He *argued* against moving the deadline, and the committee overruled him.
7. She *argued* him out of resigning, and he stayed another four years.
8. It has long been *argued* that the tax pays for itself, and it has never been shown.

**See also:** [reason](#reason), [conclude](#conclude), [point out](#point-out), [prove](#prove), [demonstrate](#demonstrate)

### ask

**Pronunciation:** /æsk/ &middot; *ASK*
**Type:** reporting verb — question, and with an infinitive a request &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default verb for reporting any question
**Patterns:** *ask* + **wh**-clause &middot; *ask* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *ask* + sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *ask* + sb + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *ask* + noun phrase (*ask a question*) &middot; *ask* + sb + noun phrase (*ask me a favor*) &middot; *ask* + **to**-infinitive (*ask to leave* — you want to act) &middot; *ask* + sb + **to**-infinitive (*ask me to leave* — you want them to act) &middot; *ask* + **that**-clause + subjunctive (formal request) &middot; *ask* + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *ask* + sb + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *ask* + **about / after** + noun phrase &middot; *ask* + **wh**-word + **to**-infinitive &middot; *ask* + direct quotation, as a quotative tag (*"Where do you live?" she asked*) &middot; passive *be asked to do something*
**Never:** ***ask to someone whether*** — the person goes straight after the verb with no preposition: *She asked me whether I was free*, never ***She asked to me whether***. And never keep question word order inside the clause: ***He asked where was I*** ✗ → *He asked where I was* ✓. *Ask* takes no **-ing** complement — ***ask leaving early*** ✗ → *She asked to leave early* ✓ — and it cannot take a **that**-clause in the question sense: ***ask that she was free*** ✗ → *She asked whether she was free* ✓. *Ask that* is always a request, and then it takes the subjunctive (*She asked that the room **be** cleared*), never a question.
**Reports:** a question actually spoken. It is the unmarked choice, carrying no formality (*inquire*), no impatience (*demand to know*), and no doubt about the answer (*query*).

> "Where do you live?" she asked.  →  She asked **where I lived**.
> "Are you free Thursday?" he asked me.  →  He asked me **whether I was** free **that** Thursday.

1. The nurse *asked* whether I had eaten anything since midnight.
2. He *asked* me why the invoice was two hundred dollars short.
3. She *asked* to see the manager, and then *asked* the manager for a refund.
4. Somebody in the back *asked* how long the recording would stay up.
5. I *asked* about the deposit twice and never got a straight answer.
6. Don't *ask* me what he meant by that.
7. The witness was *asked* to remain in the building until the hearing ended.
8. She *asked* the front desk for a later checkout and got it.

**See also:** [inquire](#inquire), [wonder](#wonder), [want to know](#want-to-know), [query](#query), [question](#question), [Reported questions](../05-questions.md#ask), [Requests: the *ask* family](../06-commands-requests.md#requests-the-ask-family)

### assume

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsum/ &middot; *uh-SOOM* — American English drops the *y* glide; British *uh-SYOOM*
**Type:** reporting verb — belief taken for granted &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *assume* + **that**-clause &middot; *assume* + sb/sth + **to**-infinitive, nearly always *to be* (formal: *assumed him to be the owner*) &middot; *assume* + noun phrase &middot; *assume* + **so / not** &middot; passive *it is assumed that* &middot; passive *sb is assumed to be*
**Never:** ***assume me that*** and ***assume to someone that*** — no personal object and no *to*-phrase for the hearer, because *assume* reports a private belief rather than something said to anybody. *Assume* also takes no **wh**-clause and no **-ing**: ***assume where he had gone*** ✗ and ***assume him going*** ✗ → *I assumed he had gone home* ✓ or *I guessed where he had gone* ✓.
**Reports:** a belief adopted without checking it. That is the judgment the word carries: *conclude* implies evidence was weighed, *assume* implies it was skipped, which is why *You assumed I'd say yes* is a complaint and *You concluded I'd say yes* is not.

> "You sent it already, I take it," he said.  →  He **assumed** I **had** already sent it.
> "Everyone here has a login, presumably."  →  She **assumed that** everyone there **had** a login.

1. I *assumed* the meeting was still on, since nobody said otherwise.
2. The estimate *assumes* that materials arrive by the first of the month.
3. She wrongly *assumed* the fee covered parking.
4. Let's not *assume* he saw the email — it's easy to miss.
5. The report *assumed* the plant to be operating at full capacity.
6. It is generally *assumed* that the two cases are related.
7. "Is the deal still on?" — "I *assume* so, but nobody has confirmed it."

**See also:** [suppose](#suppose), [gather](#gather), [believe](#believe), [think](#think), [infer](#infer), [conclude](#conclude)

### believe

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈliv/ &middot; *bi-LEEV*
**Type:** reporting verb — belief, often hedged &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; slightly more careful than *think*
**Patterns:** *believe* + **that**-clause &middot; *believe* + sb/sth + **to**-infinitive (formal) &middot; *believe* + noun phrase (*believe her*, *believe the story*) &middot; *believe* + **in** + noun phrase &middot; *believe* + **in** + **-ing** &middot; *believe so / believe not* &middot; passive *sb is believed to be* &middot; passive *it is believed that*
**Never:** ***believe to me that*** and ***believe me that the door was locked*** — *believe me* means "trust what I say," so it cannot double as the hearer slot of a report; the repair is *told me the door was locked* ✓. *Believe* also takes no **wh**-clause and no bare **-ing**: ***believe where he went*** ✗, ***believe him lying*** ✗ → *I believe he is lying* ✓.
**Reports:** something held to be true without direct knowledge of it. Against *know*, it concedes the possibility of error; against *think*, it is a touch more formal and more committed, which is why news writing uses *is believed to be* and not *is thought to be* when it wants to sound careful.

> "The building went up sometime in the 1920s," she said.  →  She **believed** the building **had gone** up sometime in the 1920s.
> "He's the owner, I'm fairly sure."  →  She **believed him to be** the owner.

1. I *believe* the office closes at four on Fridays.
2. Investigators *believe* the fire started in the crawl space.
3. She didn't *believe* a word of it.
4. The painting is *believed* to be a copy made around 1810.
5. We have no reason to *believe* that anyone else was involved.
6. Do you *believe* he actually said that?
7. She *believes* in saying exactly what she means, which has cost her more than one client.
8. It is widely *believed* that the two companies had already agreed on a price.

**See also:** [think](#think), [suppose](#suppose), [understand](#understand), [gather](#gather), [assume](#assume)

### calculate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkælkjəleɪt/ &middot; *KAL-kyuh-layt*
**Type:** reporting verb — conclusion reached by figuring &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *calculate* + **that**-clause &middot; *calculate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *calculate* + noun phrase &middot; *calculate* + noun phrase + **at** + amount &middot; passive *it is calculated that* &middot; passive *be calculated to* (= designed to)
**Never:** ***calculate someone that*** and ***calculate me the total*** — no personal object in either slot; if you need a hearer at all, use *for*: *calculated the total for me* ✓. And keep the two *to*-forms apart: *be calculated to* means "designed to produce an effect" (*a remark calculated to offend*), not "worked out that" — ***He calculated the trip to take four hours*** ✗ → *He calculated that the trip would take four hours* ✓.
**Reports:** a figure or a judgment arrived at by working something out, spoken aloud. *Estimate* admits the number is approximate; *calculate* claims it was derived.

> "At this burn rate we've got eleven months," he said.  →  He **calculated that** they **had** eleven months left at that burn rate.
> "How much will the tariffs add?"  →  Analysts **calculated how much** the tariffs **would** add.

1. She *calculated* that the detour would cost them forty minutes.
2. The actuary *calculated* how much the policy would pay out over thirty years.
3. He *calculated* the total in his head and came up two dollars short.
4. The city *calculates* its water rates on a sliding scale.
5. Nobody had *calculated* what the overtime would do to the budget.
6. The insult was *calculated* to land in front of an audience.

**See also:** [estimate](#estimate), [conclude](#conclude), [reckon](#reckon), [figure](#figure), [predict](#predict)

### cite

**Pronunciation:** /saɪt/ &middot; *SYTE* — a homophone of *site* and *sight*
**Type:** reporting verb — attributed source or reason &middot; **Register:** formal; standard in journalism, law, and academic writing
**Patterns:** *cite* + noun phrase &middot; *cite* + noun phrase + **as** + noun phrase (*cited the ruling as precedent*) &middot; *cite* + sb + **as saying** (+ **that**-clause) &middot; *cite* + noun phrase + **for** + noun phrase (= issue a citation: *cited him for speeding*) &middot; *cite* + noun phrase + **in support of / as grounds for** + noun phrase &middot; passive *be cited as* &middot; passive *be cited for*
**Never:** ***cite that the deal was dead*** — *cite* takes a noun phrase, not a **that**-clause. Repairs: *cited the collapse of the deal*, *cited sources as saying that the deal was dead*, or simply *said that the deal was dead*. It takes no personal object for the hearer either (***cited me the ruling*** ✗ → *cited the ruling to me* ✓) and no embedded question (***cited why the deal died*** ✗ → *cited the reason the deal died* ✓).
**Reports:** the source or the reason behind a statement rather than the statement itself. *Quote* reproduces the words; *cite* names where they came from or what they support.

> "I'm resigning for family reasons," he said.  →  He resigned, **citing family reasons**.
> "Two officials told us the vote was postponed."  →  The paper **cited** two officials **as saying** the vote **had been** postponed.

1. She *cited* three studies, none of which said what she claimed.
2. The judge *cited* an 1897 ruling that nobody in the room had read.
3. He turned down the offer, *citing* the commute.
4. The article *cites* an anonymous source close to the negotiations.
5. Both reports *cite* the same faulty sensor as the cause.
6. You have to *cite* every source you paraphrase, not just the ones you quote.

**See also:** [quote](#quote), [paraphrase](#paraphrase), [indicate](#indicate), [point out](#point-out), [echo](#echo)

### clarify

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklɛrəfaɪ/ &middot; *KLAIR-uh-fye*
**Type:** reporting verb — explanation that removes an ambiguity &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; heavy in business and official speech
**Patterns:** *clarify* + **that**-clause &middot; *clarify* + **wh**-clause &middot; *clarify* + **whether**-clause &middot; *clarify* + noun phrase &middot; *clarify* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; *clarify* + **to / for** sb + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it was clarified that*
**Never:** ***clarify me the rules*** — no personal object. The beneficiary takes *for* far more often than *to*: *Could you clarify that for me?* is the natural form, and ***Could you clarify me that?*** is not English. *Clarify* also takes no **to**-infinitive and no **-ing**: ***clarify to be leaving*** ✗, ***clarify sending it*** ✗ → *She clarified that she was leaving* ✓, *He clarified that he had sent it* ✓.
**Reports:** a second statement that fixes the meaning of a first one. *Explain* supplies information that was missing; *clarify* repairs information that was already there and came out ambiguous. Note what that presupposes: calling something a clarification concedes that the first statement was unclear, which is why officials reach for it when they are walking a remark back without admitting they changed it.

> "To be clear, the deadline is Friday, not Monday," she said.  →  She **clarified that** the deadline **was** Friday, not Monday.
> "What I meant was the second invoice."  →  He **clarified which** invoice he **had meant**.

1. She *clarified* that the discount applies only to the first order.
2. Could you *clarify* what "reasonable notice" means in this contract?
3. The spokesman *clarified* the timeline for reporters the next morning.
4. Let me *clarify* one thing before we go any further.
5. The memo was sent out to *clarify* who signs off on refunds.
6. He *clarified* for the committee that no money had actually changed hands.

**See also:** [explain](#explain), [spell out](#spell-out), [point out](#point-out), [indicate](#indicate), [reiterate](#reiterate)

### conclude

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈklud/ &middot; *kun-KLOOD*
**Type:** reporting verb — judgment reached from evidence &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *conclude* + **that**-clause &middot; *conclude* + **from** + noun phrase + **that**-clause &middot; *conclude* + noun phrase (*conclude an agreement* — a different sense) &middot; *conclude* + **by** + **-ing** (= finish) &middot; *conclude* + **with** + noun phrase (= finish) &middot; passive *it was concluded that*
**Never:** ***conclude someone that*** and ***conclude to do something*** — no personal object, and no infinitive in the active. And no **wh**-clause: ***conclude what had gone wrong*** ✗ → *She concluded that the seal had failed* ✓ or *She worked out what had gone wrong* ✓. Keep the two senses apart as well: *conclude* + noun phrase means *finish* or *settle* (*concluded the meeting*, *concluded a deal*), never *decide about* — ***conclude the cause*** ✗ → *He concluded that the cause was electrical* ✓.
**Reports:** a judgment presented as the endpoint of reasoning. *Assume* skips the evidence, *speculate* offers a guess, *conclude* claims the evidence has been weighed and the matter settled.

> "So the leak has to be upstream of the valve," she said.  →  She **concluded that** the leak **had to be** upstream of the valve.
> "Given all that, I don't think he knew."  →  She **concluded** he **hadn't** known.

1. The board *concluded* that no policy had been violated.
2. From the tone of the email I *concluded* we weren't getting the contract.
3. Two separate reviews *concluded* the same thing.
4. The inspector *concluded* that the crack was cosmetic.
5. She *concluded* her remarks by thanking the outgoing treasurer.
6. It would be premature to *conclude* that the trend is permanent.

**See also:** [infer](#infer), [deduce](#deduce), [gather](#gather), [reason](#reason), [argue](#argue), [assume](#assume)

### consult

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsʌlt/ &middot; *kun-SULT*
**Type:** reporting verb — question put to an authority &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *consult* + person or reference (*consult a lawyer*, *consult the manual*) &middot; *consult* + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *consult* + **with** + sb (standard American) &middot; *consult* + **with** + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *consult* + sb + **about** + **wh**-clause &middot; *consult* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; passive *be consulted about*
**Never:** ***consult to someone*** and ***consult me whether the road was open*** — *consult* never takes a clause of its own. The question has to ride in on a preposition: *consulted the ranger about whether the road was open* ✓. It takes no **to**-infinitive either: ***consulted him to fix the wiring*** ✗ → *consulted him about the wiring* ✓ or *asked him to fix the wiring* ✓.
**Reports:** asking someone with standing to answer — a professional, a document, a colleague whose agreement you need. *Ask* is neutral about who is being asked; *consult* implies expertise or authority on the other end.

> "What are my options here?" she asked her attorney.  →  She **consulted** her attorney **about** her options.
> "Is this covered?" he asked, and called the insurer.  →  He **consulted with** the insurer **about whether** it **was** covered.

1. *Consult* your doctor before you double the dose.
2. She *consulted* two contractors and got two very different numbers.
3. He *consulted* the manual, which turned out to be for a different model.
4. The mayor said she would *consult with* the council before signing anything.
5. Nobody *consulted* the people who actually use the building.
6. I'd *consult* an accountant about that if I were you.

**See also:** [ask](#ask), [inquire](#inquire), [query](#query), [question](#question), [quiz](#quiz)

### deduce

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈdus/ &middot; *di-DOOSS* — American English drops the glide; British *di-DYOOSS*
**Type:** reporting verb — conclusion drawn by strict inference &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; slightly literary
**Patterns:** *deduce* + **that**-clause &middot; *deduce* + **wh**-clause &middot; *deduce* + noun phrase &middot; *deduce* + noun phrase + **from** + noun phrase &middot; *deduce* + **from** + noun phrase + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it can be deduced that*
**Never:** ***deduce someone that*** and ***deduce to someone*** — no personal object. *Deduce* also has no **-ing** frame and no infinitive: ***deduce her being there*** ✗, ***deduce her to have been there*** ✗ → *He deduced that she had been there* ✓.
**Reports:** a conclusion the speaker presents as forced by the premises. *Infer* leaves room for probability; *deduce* claims necessity, which is why the detective *deduces* and the reader *infers*.

> "The mud is still wet, so he can't have left before noon," he said.  →  He **deduced that** the man **could not have** left before noon.
> "Only three people had a key."  →  She **deduced who** the visitor **had been**.

1. From the postmark she *deduced* that the letter had sat somewhere for a week.
2. He *deduced* the password from a photograph of the desk.
3. You can *deduce* how old the tree is by counting the rings.
4. Nothing in the file lets you *deduce* who authorized the payment.
5. She *deduced*, correctly, that the meeting had been canceled without her.
6. The whole plot depends on a conclusion no reader could possibly *deduce*.

**See also:** [infer](#infer), [conclude](#conclude), [gather](#gather), [reason](#reason), [figure](#figure)

### demand to know

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˌmænd tə ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *di-MAND tuh NOH*
**Type:** reporting verb — question asked with force &middot; **Register:** neutral, but strongly marked for confrontation; common in news writing
**Patterns:** *demand to know* + **wh**-clause &middot; *demand to know* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *demand to know* + noun phrase (*demand to know the reason*) &middot; *demand to know* + **from** sb + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***demand me to know*** and ***demand to know me why*** — nothing may come between *demand* and *to know*, and no personal object follows. The person addressed rides on a preposition or not at all: *She demanded to know from the manager why the account had been frozen* ✓. Note also that plain *demand* + **that**-clause takes the subjunctive (*demanded that he leave*), a request rather than a question.
**Reports:** a question asked as a challenge, with the implication that an answer is owed. It is the confrontational end of *ask* and the exact opposite of the deferential *I was wondering*.

> "Who authorized this?" she said.  →  She **demanded to know who had** authorized it.
> "Are you even licensed?" he said.  →  He **demanded to know whether** she **was** even licensed.

1. He *demanded to know* why his bag had been searched.
2. Shareholders *demanded to know* what the severance package cost.
3. She called back and *demanded to know* whether the charge had been reversed.
4. A reporter *demanded to know* who was paying for the flights.
5. The parents *demanded to know* how the school had missed it for two years.
6. I don't think you're in a position to *demand to know* anything.

### demonstrate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɛmənstreɪt/ &middot; *DEM-un-strayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — proof by showing &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *demonstrate* + **that**-clause &middot; *demonstrate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *demonstrate* + noun phrase &middot; *demonstrate* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *demonstrate* + **to** sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *demonstrate* + noun phrase + **to** sb &middot; *demonstrate* + **how / what** + **to**-infinitive &middot; passive *it has been demonstrated that*
**Never:** ***demonstrate me how it works*** — this is the same error as ***explain me***, and it is worth learning as a pair with *show*, which does take the object: *show me how it works* ✓, *demonstrate to me how it works* ✓, ***demonstrate me how it works*** ✗. The block holds in front of every complement, so ***demonstrate the committee that the lock could be picked*** ✗ → *demonstrated to the committee that…* ✓. And no **-ing**: ***demonstrate the seal failing*** ✗ → *demonstrated that the seal failed* ✓.
**Reports:** establishing something by making it visible — an experiment, a walkthrough, a table of results. *Show* is the everyday word for the same act; *demonstrate* is its formal counterpart and implies a deliberate display. Both are **factive**, like *point out*: write *The trial demonstrated that the drug worked* and you have stated as your own that the drug worked. If you are only reporting a claim, the verb is *said* or *claimed*.

> "Watch — the seal fails at forty pounds," she said.  →  She **demonstrated that** the seal **failed** at forty pounds.
> "Here's how you reset it."  →  He **demonstrated how to** reset it.

1. The trial *demonstrated* that the drug worked no better than a placebo.
2. She *demonstrated* to the committee how easily the lock could be picked.
3. The instructor *demonstrated* the technique twice before letting anyone try.
4. These numbers *demonstrate* what happens when maintenance gets deferred.
5. Nothing in the audit *demonstrates* fraud — only sloppiness.
6. He *demonstrated* to us that the software had been logging keystrokes.

**See also:** [show](#show), [prove](#prove), [explain](#explain), [indicate](#indicate), [point out](#point-out)

### echo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛkoʊ/ &middot; *EK-oh* — third person *echoes*, past *echoed*
**Type:** reporting verb — repetition of someone else's statement &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in journalism
**Patterns:** *echo* + noun phrase (*echo his words*, *echo the sentiment*) &middot; *echo* + sb (*echoed the senator*) &middot; *echo* + free relative (*echoed what she had said*) &middot; *echo* + **that**-clause (journalistic and marginal — the object is normally a noun phrase)
**Never:** ***echo to someone that*** and ***echo someone that*** — no hearer slot at all. What is echoed is the object: *She echoed his warning*, never ***She echoed us his warning***. And *echo* takes no embedded question: ***He echoed whether the plan would work*** ✗, ***He echoed why she had left*** ✗. *Echoed what she had said* ✓ only looks like an exception — that **what**-clause is a free relative standing in for a noun phrase ("the thing she had said"), not a question. Treat *echo* + **that**-clause as journalistic shorthand and prefer *echoed the point that…* or *agreed that…* in careful prose.
**Reports:** repeating what someone else has already said, with agreement implied. *Repeat* says the same words again, possibly your own; *echo* says someone else's, and takes their side by doing so. It carries a second, quieter judgment as well: an echo adds nothing, so *He merely echoed the party line* is a criticism in a way that *He repeated the party line* is not.

> "I said the same thing last month," he said.  →  He **echoed** the point she **had made** the month before.
> "She's right — the schedule is impossible."  →  He **echoed her complaint** about the schedule.

1. Several council members *echoed* her concerns about the parking plan.
2. The governor's statement *echoed* language used by the industry group almost word for word.
3. He *echoed* what the previous speaker had said and sat down.
4. The review *echoes* a complaint that shows up in half the ratings.
5. Her boss *echoed* the sentiment without committing to anything.
6. Every op-ed that week *echoed* the same three talking points.

### enquire

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkwaɪr/ &middot; *in-KWYRE*
**Type:** reporting verb — question &middot; **Register:** **British spelling.** American English writes *inquire* in every sense; *enquire* in an American document reads as a typo or an affectation
**Patterns:** identical to [inquire](#inquire) — *enquire* + **wh**-clause &middot; *enquire* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *enquire* + **about / after / into** + noun phrase &middot; *enquire* + **of** sb + **whether** (very formal)
**Never:** ***enquire me*** — like *inquire*, it takes no personal object, and no **that**-clause either (***enquired that the position was open*** ✗). And in American writing, avoid the spelling itself: use *inquire*. British usage splits the two by sense — *enquire* to ask a question, *inquire* to investigate formally — but that split is not observed in the United States, where *inquire* covers both. The noun follows the verb: British *enquiry*, American *inquiry*.
**Other British spellings in this territory:** *enquire* is one of a family a learner will meet in the same sources, and every one of them is wrong in an American document — British *summarise*, *apologise*, *criticise*, *recognise*, *emphasise*, *analyse* → American *summarize*, *apologize*, *criticize*, *recognize*, *emphasize*, *analyze*. Two further differences are not spelling at all but grammar: British *reckon to do something* (*I reckon to finish by six*) is not American, and British writers use *query* + **wh**-clause more freely than American ones do.
**Reports:** exactly what *inquire* reports: a question asked formally or politely. The entry exists here so the spelling can be recognized in British sources and then not copied.

> "Is the position still open?" she asked. *(British)*  →  She **enquired whether** the position **was** still open.
> *(American)*  →  She **inquired whether** the position **was** still open.

1. *(British usage)* She *enquired* about the opening hours of the archive.
2. *(British usage)* He *enquired* whether a table by the window was available.
3. *(British usage)* Guests should *enquire* at the front desk.
4. In American English, that last sentence would read *inquire* at the front desk.
5. The style guide flags *enquire* and replaces it with *inquire* automatically.
6. British firms often label the contact page *Enquiries*; American ones write *Inquiries*.

**See also:** [inquire](#inquire), [ask](#ask), [query](#query), [*inquire*, not *enquire*](../08-special-cases.md#inquire-not-enquire)

### estimate

**Pronunciation:** verb /ˈɛstəmeɪt/ &middot; *ES-tuh-mayt*; noun /ˈɛstəmət/ &middot; *ES-tuh-mit* — the last vowel is full in the verb and reduced in the noun
**Type:** reporting verb — approximate figure &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *estimate* + **that**-clause &middot; *estimate* + **wh**-clause (*estimate how many*) &middot; *estimate* + noun phrase &middot; *estimate* + noun phrase + **at** + amount &middot; *estimate* + noun phrase + **to be** + value (formal: *estimated the loss to be four million dollars*) &middot; passive *be estimated to be / at* &middot; passive *it is estimated that*
**Never:** ***estimate someone that*** and ***estimate me the cost*** — no personal object in either slot. The infinitive is narrower than it looks: *estimate* + noun phrase + **to be** is standard in formal writing (*estimated the age of the tree to be two hundred years* ✓), but there is no object-less infinitive (***estimated to take a week*** ✗ in the active), and an eventive infinitive reads badly even with an object (***estimated repairs to take a week*** ✗). Say *estimated that repairs would take a week* ✓.
**Reports:** a number offered as approximate. *Calculate* claims the figure was derived and is exact; *estimate* concedes up front that it is not.

> "Somewhere around eight thousand people showed up," he said.  →  He **estimated that** about eight thousand people **had** shown up.
> "Call it three weeks, give or take."  →  She **estimated** the work **at** three weeks.

1. The contractor *estimated* that the roof had five years left in it.
2. Police *estimated* the crowd at twelve hundred; organizers said four thousand.
3. Can you *estimate* how much of the budget goes to overtime?
4. The damage is *estimated* at nine million dollars.
5. She *estimated* the drive at four hours and was off by two.
6. Nobody could *estimate* what the delay would cost.
7. The appraiser *estimated* the collection to be worth about sixty thousand dollars.

**See also:** [calculate](#calculate), [reckon](#reckon), [figure](#figure), [predict](#predict), [speculate](#speculate)

### explain

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈspleɪn/ &middot; *ik-SPLAYN*
**Type:** reporting verb — explanation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *explain* + **that**-clause &middot; *explain* + **wh**-clause &middot; *explain* + **whether**-clause &middot; *explain* + noun phrase &middot; *explain* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *explain* + **to** sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *explain* + noun phrase + **to** sb &middot; *explain* + **how / what / why** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *explain oneself* &middot; passive *it was explained that*
**Never:** ***explain me something*** — this is the single most common error in the whole group. *Explain* takes **no personal object** before its complement; the hearer must arrive on *to*. Repairs: *explain it to me*, *explain to me what happened*, *explain to me that it was an accident*. The same block applies to ***explain me how*** ✗ → *explain to me how* ✓. Note that *tell* and *show* behave the opposite way (*tell me what happened*, *show me what happened*), which is exactly why the error is so persistent. *Explain* also takes no **-ing** and no **to**-infinitive complement: ***explain losing the file*** ✗, ***explain to have lost the file*** ✗ → *He explained that he had lost the file* ✓ or *He explained how he had lost the file* ✓.
**Word order:** when both the hearer and the content are present, the short one goes first. *Explain it to me* ✓ / ***explain to me it*** ✗; but with a full clause the *to*-phrase leads: *He explained to the class why the experiment had failed* ✓, and *He explained why the experiment had failed to the class* is grammatical but reads as though the experiment failed at the class.
**Reports:** supplying the information or the reason that makes something understandable. *Say* just reports words; *explain* claims those words answered a *why* or a *how*.

> "The server was down all morning, so nothing went out," he said.  →  He **explained that** the server **had been** down all morning.
> "Here's why the check bounced."  →  She **explained to me why** the check **had** bounced.

1. She *explained* that the fee is charged per bag, not per passenger.
2. Could you *explain* to me why my account was closed?
3. He *explained* the rules twice and half the room still got it wrong.
4. Nobody ever *explained* how the two departments were supposed to share the budget.
5. The label *explains* what to do if the paint gets on your skin.
6. I tried to *explain* the situation to my landlord and got nowhere.

**See also:** [clarify](#clarify), [spell out](#spell-out), [demonstrate](#demonstrate), [show](#show), [point out](#point-out), [Reported questions](../05-questions.md#which-verbs-take-a-person-object)

### figure

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɪɡjɚ/ &middot; *FIG-yer* — American English keeps the *y* glide; British *FIG-uh*
**Type:** reporting verb — casual conclusion &middot; **Register:** informal; distinctly American
**Patterns:** *figure* + **that**-clause (the *that* is nearly always dropped) &middot; *figure* + sb/sth + **for** + noun phrase (*figured him for a cop*) &middot; *figure* + **on** + noun phrase &middot; *figure* + **on** + **-ing** &middot; *figure* + **to**-infinitive (= be likely to: *the game figures to be close*) &middot; *figure out* + **wh**-clause &middot; *figure out* + **that**-clause &middot; *figure out* + noun phrase &middot; *figure* + noun phrase + *out* (separable: *figure it out*)
**Never:** ***figure someone that*** and ***figure me the answer*** — no personal object in either slot. Keep *figure* apart from *figure out*: plain *figure* takes a **that**-clause and reports a guess (*I figured he'd be late*), while *figure out* takes a **wh**-clause and reports a solved problem (*I figured out why he was late*). ***figure why he was late*** is not English. Keep the two *to*-forms apart as well: *X figures to be Y* means "is likely to be" and wants a non-personal subject (*the game figures to be close* ✓), while ***I figured to leave early*** meaning "I intended to" is dialectal at best — say *I figured I'd leave early* ✓. And the particle rule holds: *figure it out* ✓, ***figure out it*** ✗.
**Reports:** a working assumption arrived at quickly and offered without much weight behind it. It is the everyday American equivalent of *reckon* and *suppose*, and it is at home in speech and dialogue but not in a report or a brief.

> "He'll be late, I bet," she said.  →  She **figured** he **would be** late.
> "I couldn't work out why the door stuck."  →  He couldn't **figure out why** the door **stuck**.

1. I *figured* you'd want the window seat.
2. She *figured* the store would be closed by the time they got there.
3. We *figured* on about an hour of traffic and got two.
4. He *figured* her for a reporter the moment she pulled out a notebook.
5. It took the mechanic ten minutes to *figure out* what was rattling.
6. Go ahead and start — we'll *figure out* who's paying later.
7. With both starters hurt, the game *figures* to be low-scoring.
8. She finally *figured out* that the two invoices were for the same job.

**See also:** [reckon](#reckon), [suppose](#suppose), [gather](#gather), [assume](#assume), [think](#think)

### forecast

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɔrkæst/ &middot; *FOR-kast* — past tense *forecast* or *forecasted*; *forecast* is preferred in American weather and business usage
**Type:** reporting verb — prediction, usually quantified &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; institutional
**Patterns:** *forecast* + **that**-clause &middot; *forecast* + noun phrase &middot; *forecast* + **wh**-clause (*forecast how much*) &middot; *forecast* + noun phrase + **at** + amount (*forecast the deficit at two billion*) &middot; passive *be forecast to* &middot; passive *it is forecast that*
**Never:** ***forecast someone that*** and ***forecast someone to do something*** — no personal object. And in careful prose *forecast* takes no *to*-infinitive in the active: say *forecast that sales would rise*, not ***forecast sales to rise***. The infinitive frame does surface in financial writing and headlines, but only the passive is fully standard — *sales are forecast to rise* ✓.
**Reports:** a prediction backed by a model or a method, usually about a measurable quantity. *Predict* is the general word and covers hunches; *forecast* implies data behind the number.

> "We expect three to five inches overnight," she said.  →  The forecaster **forecast that** three to five inches **would** fall overnight.
> "Revenue should be up about 4 percent."  →  The bank **forecast** a 4 percent rise in revenue.

2. Analysts *forecast* that the shortage would ease by summer.
3. She *forecasted* the same slowdown a year before anyone else did.
4. Utilities are *forecast* to raise rates twice in the next two years.
5. Nobody *forecast* how quickly the market would turn.
6. The model *forecasts* demand district by district.
7. The budget office *forecast* the shortfall at just under a billion dollars.

### gather

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡæðɚ/ &middot; *GA-ther*
**Type:** reporting verb — secondhand understanding &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning conversational and polite
**Patterns:** *gather* + **that**-clause (the *that* is usually dropped) &middot; *gather* + **from** + noun phrase + **that**-clause &middot; *gather* + **as much / so** (*So I gathered*) &middot; *I gather* as a parenthetical (*You're leaving, I gather*)
**Never:** ***gather someone that*** and ***gather to someone*** — no personal object. *Gather* also avoids the **wh**-clause and the **-ing**: ***gather where he went*** ✗, ***gather him leaving*** ✗; use the fixed phrase *from what I gather* instead, or a **that**-clause.
**Reports:** a belief picked up indirectly, with the source deliberately left vague. *I gather you're leaving* means someone told me, I am not saying who, and I am giving you room to correct it — which is why it works as a polite opener where *I heard* would sound like gossip. It also hedges: like *understand*, it passes the claim along without vouching for it, so the reporter is not on the hook if it turns out to be wrong.

> "People are saying the branch is closing."  →  She **gathered that** the branch **was** closing.
> "So you two have met, then."  →  He **gathered** they **had** already met.

1. I *gather* you've already spoken to legal about this.
2. From the silence in the room she *gathered* that the news was bad.
3. He *gathered* the position had been filled weeks earlier.
4. "They're not coming." — "So I *gathered*."
5. From what I *gather*, the lease runs through next spring.
6. We *gathered* from her tone that the conversation was over.

### indicate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪndəkeɪt/ &middot; *IN-di-kayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — guarded statement or signal &middot; **Register:** formal; heavy in official, legal, and news writing
**Patterns:** *indicate* + **that**-clause &middot; *indicate* + **wh**-clause &middot; *indicate* + **whether**-clause &middot; *indicate* + noun phrase &middot; *indicate* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *indicate* + **to** sb + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *it was indicated that*
**Never:** ***indicate me that*** and ***indicate someone something*** — no personal object; the hearer takes *to*, as in *indicated to reporters that no arrest was imminent*. *Indicate* also takes no **to**-infinitive in the active and no **-ing**: ***indicate to resign*** ✗, ***indicate resigning*** ✗ → *He indicated that he would resign* ✓.
**Reports:** two things at once, which is why officials love it. With a person as subject it reports a statement the writer does not want to call a promise (*He indicated that he would resign*: he said something in that direction, and it was not a commitment). With an inanimate subject it reports evidence rather than speech (*The data indicate that*), where it sits a step below *show* and *demonstrate* in confidence.

> "I'm not ruling out a run," he said.  →  He **indicated that** he **might** run.

2. Early returns *indicate* a close race in three counties.
3. She *indicated* to her supervisor that she was considering other offers.
4. Nothing in the file *indicates* who approved the transfer.
5. The survey *indicates* how far behind the district has fallen.
6. He *indicated* his willingness to testify without ever quite saying yes.

### infer

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈfɚ/ &middot; *in-FUR* — doubles the *r*: *inferred*, *inferring*
**Type:** reporting verb — conclusion drawn by the listener &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Patterns:** *infer* + **that**-clause &middot; *infer* + **wh**-clause &middot; *infer* + noun phrase &middot; *infer* + noun phrase + **from** + noun phrase &middot; *infer* + **from** + noun phrase + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it can be inferred that*
**Never:** ***infer someone that*** and ***infer to someone*** — no personal object, and no **-ing** (***inferred him lying*** ✗). And do not use *infer* to mean *imply*: ***Are you inferring that I lied?*** is a very common error for *implying*. **The speaker implies; the listener infers.** The mistaken sense is old and widespread in speech, and it is still corrected everywhere it matters, so it is worth keeping straight.
**Reports:** what a hearer concluded from what was said, not what the speaker said. *Deduce* claims necessity; *infer* claims only that the conclusion was reasonable.

> "I'd say the meeting went about as well as it could have."  →  From that, she **inferred that** it **had gone** badly.
> "He never mentioned the second offer."  →  They **inferred that** no second offer **existed**.

1. From his silence I *inferred* that the answer was no.
2. Readers were left to *infer* what the company actually planned to do.
3. She *inferred* from the invoice that the work had already been done.
4. You can't *infer* causation from a chart like that.
5. He *inferred* a threat where none was intended.
6. What am I supposed to *infer* from a two-word reply?

### inquire

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkwaɪr/ &middot; *in-KWYRE*
**Type:** reporting verb — question, formally or politely put &middot; **Register:** formal. **American English uses this spelling in every sense**; *enquire* is British
**Patterns:** *inquire* + **wh**-clause &middot; *inquire* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *inquire* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *inquire* + **after** + person (health) &middot; *inquire* + **into** + noun phrase (investigate) &middot; *inquire* + **as to** + noun phrase or **wh**-clause (very formal) &middot; *inquire* + **of** sb + **whether / wh**-clause (very formal) &middot; *inquire* + direct quotation, as a quotative tag
**Never:** ***inquire me*** and ***inquire the registrar whether*** — *inquire* takes no personal object. And no **that**-clause: ***inquire that the position was open*** ✗ — a question verb needs a question, so *inquired whether the position was open* ✓.

5. A guest *inquired* of the concierge where the nearest pharmacy was.

7. Counsel *inquired* as to whether the witness had been shown the document beforehand.

### interrogate

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈtɛrəɡeɪt/ &middot; *in-TERR-uh-gayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — sustained hostile questioning &middot; **Register:** formal; institutional or figurative
**Patterns:** *interrogate* + person &middot; *interrogate* + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *interrogate* + sb + **about** + **wh**-clause &middot; *interrogate* + noun phrase (academic: *interrogate the assumption*) &middot; passive *be interrogated about*
**Never:** ***interrogate whether he was home*** and ***interrogate someone something*** — *interrogate* cannot take a clause at all. Its object is the **person** being questioned, and the subject matter arrives on *about*: *interrogated him about where he had been* ✓, ***interrogated where he had been*** ✗. No **that**-clause either (***interrogate that he had lied*** ✗) and no double object.
**Reports:** questioning as a process rather than a question. It reports that someone was put under pressure over time, not that one thing was asked, so it never carries the content of a single question the way *ask* does.

> "Where were you Tuesday night? Who saw you? Why didn't you call?"  →  They **interrogated** him **about** his whereabouts on Tuesday night.
> "Answer me — all of it, from the beginning."  →  She **interrogated** the witness **for** two hours.

1. He was *interrogated* for six hours without a lawyer present.
2. Border agents *interrogated* her about the purpose of the trip.
3. The film *interrogates* the idea that hard work alone explains success.
4. My mother *interrogated* me about every friend whose name she didn't recognize.
5. They *interrogated* both drivers separately and got the same story.
6. Nobody *interrogated* the numbers before they went into the press release.

**See also:** [question](#question), [quiz](#quiz), [query](#query), [demand to know](#demand-to-know), [ask](#ask)

### paraphrase

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɛrəfreɪz/ &middot; *PAIR-uh-frayz*
**Type:** reporting verb — restatement in other words &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *paraphrase* + noun phrase &middot; *paraphrase* + sb (*paraphrase Orwell*) &middot; *paraphrase* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; *paraphrase* + free relative (*paraphrase what he said*) &middot; used absolutely (*I'm paraphrasing*) &middot; **to paraphrase** + sb, as a sentence adverbial &middot; passive *be paraphrased as*
**Never:** ***paraphrase that the policy had changed*** and ***paraphrase me the argument*** — *paraphrase* takes a noun phrase, not a **that**-clause and not a double object. Repairs: *paraphrased the memo*, *paraphrased the memo for me*, or *said, paraphrasing the memo, that the policy had changed*. It takes no embedded question either: ***paraphrased whether the policy had changed*** ✗.
**Reports:** the content of what someone said, rewritten. It is the honest label for indirect speech itself: a quotation reproduces the words, a paraphrase keeps the meaning and drops the wording, which is why paraphrased material still needs a citation.

> "I have nothing to add at this time," he said.  →  **Paraphrasing**, he said he **had** nothing more to say.
> "As Twain more or less put it, history rhymes."  →  She **paraphrased** Twain on history rhyming.

1. She *paraphrased* the ruling for readers who weren't going to read forty pages of it.
2. I'm *paraphrasing*, but that was the gist of what he told me.
3. To *paraphrase* my grandmother, nothing bought on sale was ever a bargain.
4. The summary *paraphrases* three separate reports without citing any of them.
5. He *paraphrased* the complaint so gently that it stopped sounding like a complaint.
6. If you *paraphrase* a source, you still owe it a footnote.

**See also:** [quote](#quote), [summarize](#summarize), [cite](#cite), [recap](#recap), [echo](#echo)

### point out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpɔɪnt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *poynt OWT*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement of a fact the speaker treats as true &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *point out* + **that**-clause &middot; *point out* + **wh**-clause &middot; *point out* + noun phrase &middot; *point out* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *point out* + **to** sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *point* + noun phrase + *out* + **to** sb (separable with a noun object: *point the error out to him*) &middot; passive *it should be pointed out that*
**Never:** ***point out me that*** and ***point me out that*** — no personal object in either slot. The hearer takes *to*: *pointed out to me that the deadline had passed* ✓. Note the particle rule: a pronoun object must sit inside the verb (*point it out*, never ***point out it***), but a **that**-clause never can (***point that the deadline had passed out*** ✗). And no **-ing** or infinitive complement: ***pointed out him being late*** ✗, ***pointed out to be late*** ✗.
**Reports:** drawing attention to something the speaker takes to be a fact — and, crucially, that the **reporter** also takes to be a fact. This is the presupposition that separates it from *claim*:

| Verb | What the reporter signals | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *point out* | the statement is true, and worth noticing | *He pointed out that the deadline had passed.* |
| *say* | nothing either way | *He said that the deadline had passed.* |
| *claim* | doubt — the reporter is not vouching for it | *He claimed that the deadline had passed.* |

Write *He pointed out that the report was fabricated* and you have said the report was fabricated. If you are not prepared to say that, the verb is *claimed* or *alleged*.

> "You do realize the store closed an hour ago," she said.  →  She **pointed out that** the store **had** closed an hour **earlier**.
> "Notice that neither invoice is signed."  →  He **pointed out that** neither invoice **was** signed.

1. She *pointed out* that we'd already paid for the extra night.
2. Someone finally *pointed out* to the group that nobody had booked a room.
3. He *pointed out* how much cheaper the annual plan works out to be.
4. It's worth *pointing out* that the rule has never once been enforced.
5. I *pointed* the discrepancy *out* to accounting and heard nothing back.
6. As critics *point out*, the survey never asked people who had left.
7. She *pointed out* to the new hires exactly where the process breaks down.

**See also:** [indicate](#indicate), [explain](#explain), [clarify](#clarify), [show](#show), [argue](#argue), [prove](#prove)

### predict

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈdɪkt/ &middot; *pri-DIKT*
**Type:** reporting verb — statement about the future &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *predict* + **that**-clause &middot; *predict* + **wh**-clause &middot; *predict* + **whether**-clause &middot; *predict* + noun phrase &middot; passive *be predicted to* &middot; passive *it is predicted that*
**Never:** ***predict someone that*** and ***predict me the outcome*** — no personal object. *Predict someone to win* turns up in sports and betting writing but is not standard elsewhere; use *predicted that she would win*. And no **-ing**: ***predicted the bill dying in committee*** ✗ → *predicted that the bill would die in committee* ✓.
**Backshift:** the *will* inside a prediction becomes *would* under a past reporting verb — *"It will rain," he predicted* → *He predicted that it **would** rain* — and *would* is what makes the prediction read as still open rather than already settled.
**Reports:** a claim about what is going to happen. *Forecast* implies a model; *speculate* admits a guess; *predict* sits between them and commits the speaker.

> "Nobody will show up in that weather," he said.  →  He **predicted that** nobody **would** show up in that weather.
> "Who takes the division?"  →  She **predicted who would** take the division, and got it right.

1. He *predicted* that the bill would die in committee, and it did.
2. Every forecaster *predicted* a mild winter.
3. She *predicted* what the landlord would say almost word for word.
4. The storm is *predicted* to weaken before it reaches the coast.
5. Nobody *predicted* how fast the neighborhood would change.
6. I'm not going to *predict* the outcome of a case I haven't read.

**See also:** [forecast](#forecast), [speculate](#speculate), [estimate](#estimate), [calculate](#calculate), [assume](#assume)

### prove

**Pronunciation:** /pruv/ &middot; *PROOV* — past participle *proved* or *proven*; American English prefers *proved* as a verb (*has proved*) and *proven* as an adjective (*a proven method*)
**Type:** reporting verb — demonstration that settles a question &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *prove* + **that**-clause &middot; *prove* + **wh**-clause (*prove who did it*) &middot; *prove* + noun phrase &middot; *prove* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; *prove* + **to** sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *prove* + noun phrase + adjective (*prove him wrong*) &middot; *prove* + noun phrase + noun phrase (*prove him a liar*) &middot; *prove* + noun phrase + **to be** (*proved the claim to be false*) &middot; *prove* + reflexive + adjective (*proved herself capable*) &middot; *prove* + **to**-infinitive (= turn out: *proved to be false*) &middot; passive *it has been proved / proven that*
**Never:** ***prove me that it works*** — the hearer takes *to*: *prove to me that it works* ✓. The one place a bare object is fine is the object-complement frame, which is a different structure entirely: *prove me wrong* ✓, *prove him a liar* ✓ — there the noun phrase is what is being characterized, not the person being told. And no **-ing** complement: ***proved him taking the money*** ✗ → *proved that he had taken the money* ✓.
**Reports:** the strongest claim in the group — that the matter is now beyond dispute. *Argue* offers reasons, *demonstrate* makes something visible, *prove* asserts the question is closed, so a careful writer reserves it for cases where it really is. It is fully **factive**: *The receipts prove that the payment cleared* states, in the reporter's own voice, that the payment cleared.

> "Look at the timestamps — he couldn't have been there," she said.  →  She **proved that** he **could not have** been there.
> "You said it would never work."  →  The pilot **proved him wrong**.

1. The receipts *prove* that the payment cleared in March.
2. Nothing in the record *proves* intent.
3. She set out to *prove* to the board that the process could be automated.
4. He *proved* everyone wrong within a season.
5. The rumor *proved* to be false, though it took a month to die.
6. You can't *prove* a negative, as the saying goes.
7. She *proved* herself capable of running the department in about six weeks.
8. Two of the three signatures were later *proved* to be forgeries.

**See also:** [demonstrate](#demonstrate), [show](#show), [argue](#argue), [indicate](#indicate), [conclude](#conclude)

### query

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkwɪri/ &middot; *KWEER-ee* — also /ˈkwɛri/ *KWAIR-ee* in some American speech
**Type:** reporting verb — question that challenges &middot; **Register:** formal; business, editorial, and technical
**Patterns:** *query* + noun phrase (*query a charge*) &middot; *query* + **whether**-clause &middot; *query* + **wh**-clause (*queried why the charge appeared twice* — commoner in British writing) &middot; *query* + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *query* + direct quotation (fiction: *"Are you sure?" he queried*) &middot; passive *be queried*
**Never:** ***query to someone*** and ***query me the total*** — no double object and no *to*. No clause after a personal object either: ***query me whether the total was right*** ✗ → *She queried whether the total was right* ✓ or *She asked me whether the total was right* ✓. And no **that**-clause: ***query that the figures were correct*** ✗ → *He queried whether the figures were correct* ✓. Also avoid *query* as a plain substitute for *ask* in narrative: *"What time is it?" he queried* reads as amateur fiction, and *asked* is the word. The verb is in general commoner in British than American English, where *question* and *ask about* carry most of this load.
**Reports:** raising a question about whether something is correct. *Ask* seeks information; *query* signals suspicion that there is an error, which is why an editor *queries* a line and a customer *queries* a charge.

> "This line item doesn't look right to me," she said.  →  She **queried** the line item.
> "Are we sure the totals reconcile?"  →  He **queried whether** the totals **reconciled**.

1. She *queried* the shipping charge and had it removed.
2. The copy editor *queried* two dates and a spelling.
3. Several members *queried* whether the vote had been properly noticed.
4. He *queried* the bank about a withdrawal he didn't recognize.
5. Nobody *queried* the figure until the audit, eight months later.
6. If anything looks off, *query* it before the file goes to print.

**See also:** [question](#question), [ask](#ask), [inquire](#inquire), [clarify](#clarify), [Reported questions](../05-questions.md#query-and-question)

### question

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkwɛstʃən/ &middot; *KWES-chun*
**Type:** reporting verb — sustained questioning, or expressed doubt &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *question* + person &middot; *question* + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *question* + noun phrase (*question the decision*) &middot; *question* + **whether / if**-clause &middot; *question* + **wh**-clause (*question how* / *question why*) &middot; *question* + **that**-clause, chiefly under negation (*I don't question that she meant well*) &middot; passive *be questioned about*
**Never:** ***question me the details*** — no double object; the topic takes *about*: *questioned me about the details* ✓. And the two senses do not mix their frames: with a **person** object it means interrogate (*They questioned him for an hour*), with a **thing** object or a **whether**-clause it means doubt (*She questioned the timing*, *She questioned whether it was legal*). ***question him whether he had been there*** ✗ → *asked him whether…* ✓. Watch the **that**-clause: it is fine under negation (*Nobody questions that the policy works* ✓) but odd in the affirmative — ***question that the vote was legal*** ✗ → *She questioned whether the vote was legal* ✓. And *question* + **wh**-clause raises a challenge rather than reporting an act of asking: *He questioned why the same vendor kept winning* means he was calling it into doubt, not that he put the question to somebody.
**Reports:** either the act of putting questions to someone, or the act of casting doubt. Which one you get is decided entirely by what follows the verb, so the frame carries the meaning here more than the word does.

> "We'd like to know where you were between six and eight."  →  Detectives **questioned** him **about** his movements that evening.
> "I'm not convinced these numbers add up."  →  She **questioned whether** the numbers **added** up.

1. Police *questioned* three witnesses at the scene.
2. She *questioned* whether the committee had the authority to do that.
3. Reporters *questioned* the mayor about the missing emails for twenty minutes.
4. Nobody *questioned* the budget until it was already approved.
5. He *questioned* why the same vendor kept winning every contract.
6. I don't *question* her motives — only her arithmetic.
7. Nobody *questions* that the program works; the argument is over what it costs.

**See also:** [interrogate](#interrogate), [query](#query), [quiz](#quiz), [ask](#ask), [demand to know](#demand-to-know)

### quiz

**Pronunciation:** /kwɪz/ &middot; *KWIZ* — doubles the *z*: *quizzed*, *quizzing*
**Type:** reporting verb — persistent questioning &middot; **Register:** informal; also a staple of headline and tabloid writing
**Patterns:** *quiz* + sb &middot; *quiz* + sb + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *quiz* + sb + **about / on** + **wh**-clause &middot; passive *be quizzed about / on*
**Never:** ***quiz whether the flight was on time*** and ***quiz someone something*** — like *interrogate*, *quiz* takes a person as its object and never a clause of its own. Repairs: *quizzed her about the flight*, *asked whether the flight was on time*. No **that**-clause either (***quiz that the flight was late*** ✗), and no *to*: ***quiz to her about it*** ✗.
**Reports:** questioning that is repeated and a little pushy, but not hostile — a parent, a teacher, or a reporter working through a list. Where *interrogate* is grim, *quiz* is closer to nosy.

> "Where were you? Who with? Whose car?"  →  His mother **quizzed** him **about** the whole evening.
> "Run me through the state capitals again."  →  She **quizzed** him **on** the state capitals.

1. Reporters *quizzed* the coach about his starting lineup.
2. She *quizzed* me on vocabulary the whole way to school.
3. He got *quizzed* about the gap in his résumé at every interview.
4. The kids *quizzed* their grandfather about what the town used to look like.
5. Nobody *quizzed* him too hard about where the money came from.
6. I *quizzed* the salesman on the warranty until he gave up and got the manager.

**See also:** [question](#question), [interrogate](#interrogate), [query](#query), [ask](#ask), [demand to know](#demand-to-know)

### quote

**Pronunciation:** /kwoʊt/ &middot; *KWOHT*
**Type:** reporting verb — reproduction of exact words &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *quote* + noun phrase (*quote the statute*, *quote his exact words*) &middot; *quote* + sb &middot; *quote* + sb + **as saying** (+ **that**-clause) &middot; *quote* + sb + **on** + noun phrase (*don't quote me on that*) &middot; *quote* + noun phrase + **to** sb &middot; *quote* + **from** + noun phrase &middot; *quote* + sb + amount (*quote me a price* — a different sense) &middot; passive *be quoted as saying*
**Never:** ***quoted him that the deal was off*** — *quote* cannot take a bare **that**-clause after a person. The journalistic frame is fixed: *quoted him **as saying** that the deal was off* ✓. There is no embedded question either (***quote him whether the deal was off*** ✗), and the only double-object frame belongs to the pricing sense (*quote me a price* ✓, ***quote me the statute*** ✗ → *quote the statute to me* ✓). And do not use *quote* for a paraphrase: if the words are not the speaker's own, the verb is *paraphrase* or *say*.
**Reports:** the speaker's actual wording, presented as verbatim. That is the whole distinction — *say* reports content, *quote* reports the words themselves and puts the reporter on the hook for their accuracy.

> "This department has been starved for a decade," she said.  →  The article **quoted** her **as saying** the department **had been** starved for a decade.
> "As Lincoln put it, 'a house divided against itself cannot stand.'"  →  He **quoted** Lincoln on the house divided.

1. She *quoted* the lease back to him, clause and line number.
2. The story *quotes* two neighbors who heard the argument.
3. He was widely *quoted* as saying the plan was dead on arrival.
4. Don't *quote* me on the exact figure — I'd have to look it up.
5. The professor *quoted* from a letter nobody knew existed.
6. The dealer *quoted* us four thousand dollars for the same repair.

**See also:** [cite](#cite), [paraphrase](#paraphrase), [echo](#echo), [repeat](#repeat), [Punctuating direct speech](../01-basics.md#2-punctuating-direct-speech-in-american-english)

### reason

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrizən/ &middot; *REE-zun*
**Type:** reporting verb — conclusion presented with its reasoning &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary; most at home in narrative
**Patterns:** *reason* + **that**-clause &middot; *reason* + direct quotation, as a quotative tag (*"He must have missed the exit," she reasoned.*) &middot; *reason* + **from** + noun phrase (+ **to** + noun phrase) &middot; *reason* + **about** + noun phrase (formal, in logic and philosophy) &middot; *reason out* + noun phrase or **wh**-clause &middot; *reason* + **with** + person (a different verb: try to persuade)
**Never:** ***reason someone that*** and ***reason to do something*** — no personal object, no infinitive. And no bare **wh**-clause: ***reason why the door stuck*** ✗ → *He reasoned that the door stuck because the frame had swollen* ✓ or *He reasoned out why the door stuck* ✓. Keep *reason that* apart from *reason with*: *She reasoned that he was lying* reports a conclusion; *She reasoned with him* reports an attempt to talk him around, and takes no clause at all.
**Reports:** a conclusion together with the thinking behind it, usually silent. It is the narrator's verb — *he reasoned* labels a line of thought the reader is being shown, which is why it appears far more often in fiction than in conversation.

> "The lights are on, so somebody must be home," he thought.  →  He **reasoned that** somebody **had to be** home.
> "If she'd left, the car would be gone."  →  She **reasoned that** he **hadn't** left yet.

1. He *reasoned* that a second key had to exist somewhere in the house.
2. She *reasoned* that nobody would check the file until Monday.
3. "They'd have called by now," he *reasoned*.
4. The committee *reasoned* that a smaller pilot carried less risk.
5. I *reasoned* that the fastest route was also the one everyone else would take.
6. You can't *reason with* someone who has already decided.

**See also:** [conclude](#conclude), [infer](#infer), [deduce](#deduce), [argue](#argue), [figure](#figure)

### recap

**Pronunciation:** verb /riˈkæp/ &middot; *ree-KAP*; noun /ˈrikæp/ &middot; *REE-kap* — doubles the *p*: *recapped*, *recapping*
**Type:** reporting verb — brief restatement of what was already covered &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; standard in meetings, sports, and broadcasting
**Patterns:** *recap* + noun phrase &middot; *recap* + **wh**-clause &middot; *recap* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; *recap* + **for** sb &middot; **to recap**, as a sentence adverbial &middot; used absolutely (*Let me recap*)
**Never:** ***recap that the deadline had moved*** and ***recap me the meeting*** — no **that**-clause and no double object. Repairs: *recapped the decision*, *recapped the meeting for me*, *recapped what had been decided*. The beneficiary takes *for*, never *to*: ***recapped the meeting to me*** ✗.
**Reports:** a short replay of ground already covered, for people who were there or who just arrived. *Summarize* condenses a body of material; *recap* replays a sequence of events or points in order.

> "So: we approved the budget, tabled the hiring, and moved the retreat."  →  She **recapped what** the meeting **had** decided.
> "Quick reminder of where we left off."  →  He **recapped** the previous session **for** the newcomers.

1. Let me *recap* where we landed before everybody scatters.
2. She *recapped* the first two episodes for anyone joining late.
3. He *recapped* what each department had committed to.
4. To *recap*: the deposit is refundable, the fee is not.
5. The blog *recaps* every game in about four hundred words.
6. Nobody wanted to sit through another *recap* of the same argument.

**See also:** [summarize](#summarize), [repeat](#repeat), [reiterate](#reiterate), [paraphrase](#paraphrase), [spell out](#spell-out)

### reckon

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɛkən/ &middot; *REK-un*
**Type:** reporting verb — casual opinion or rough calculation &middot; **Register:** informal. In American English it is regional — ordinary in Southern and rural speech, and elsewhere either jocular or a bit of borrowed British
**Patterns:** *reckon* + **that**-clause (the *that* is nearly always dropped) &middot; *reckon* + noun phrase (*reckon the cost* — the older "calculate" sense) &middot; *reckon* + noun phrase + **at** + amount &middot; *reckon* + sb/sth + **to be** (mostly passive: *is reckoned to be*) &middot; *reckon* + **so / not** &middot; *reckon* + **with** + noun phrase (a different sense, = deal with: *a force to be reckoned with*) &middot; *reckon* + **on** + noun phrase or **-ing** (chiefly British)
**Never:** ***reckon someone that*** — no personal object, and no **wh**-clause (***reckon where he went*** ✗). And in American English avoid ***reckon to do something*** meaning "expect to," which is a British and dialectal frame: say *I reckon I'll head out* ✓, not ***I reckon to head out*** ✗. *Reckon on* is British too — an American says *counted on* or *figured on*.
**Reports:** an opinion held loosely and offered without ceremony. *Think* is the neutral version; *reckon* adds a regional and conversational color that will be heard, so it does not belong in formal writing.

> "I'd say we've got about an hour of daylight," he said.  →  He **reckoned** they **had** about an hour of daylight left.
> "That's the best barbecue in three counties."  →  She **reckoned** it **was** the best barbecue in three counties.

1. I *reckon* we'll be there by six if the traffic holds.
2. He *reckoned* the truck had another winter in it.
3. "Think it'll rain?" — "I *reckon* so."
4. She *reckoned* the cost of the repair at about half what the shop quoted.
5. The bridge is *reckoned* to be the oldest still in use in the county.
6. Nobody around here *reckons* much of the new zoning plan.
7. The county assessor is a force to be *reckoned* with, whatever you think of her.

**See also:** [figure](#figure), [suppose](#suppose), [think](#think), [estimate](#estimate), [calculate](#calculate)

### reiterate

**Pronunciation:** /riˈɪtəreɪt/ &middot; *ree-IT-uh-rayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — deliberate restatement &middot; **Register:** formal; institutional and diplomatic
**Patterns:** *reiterate* + **that**-clause &middot; *reiterate* + noun phrase (*reiterate his support*, *reiterate the offer*) &middot; *reiterate* + free relative (*reiterated what she had said in June*) &middot; *reiterate* + **to** sb + **that**-clause &middot; passive *it was reiterated that*
**Never:** ***reiterate me that*** and ***reiterate someone the offer*** — no personal object. No embedded question either: ***reiterated whether the deadline would move*** ✗ → *reiterated that the deadline would not move* ✓. And avoid ***reiterate again***: *re-* already carries the repetition, so the *again* is redundant — a mistake common enough in business writing that editors flag it on sight.
**Reports:** saying the same thing a second time on purpose, to close off doubt. *Repeat* is neutral about why; *reiterate* implies the first statement was not accepted or not heard, which is why it turns up in press briefings more than anywhere else.

> "I'll say it again: no decision has been made," she said.  →  She **reiterated that** no decision **had been** made.
> "Our position hasn't changed."  →  The ambassador **reiterated** his government's position.

1. He *reiterated* that the deadline would not move under any circumstances.
2. The company *reiterated* its guidance for the fourth quarter.
3. She *reiterated* to the staff that nobody's job was at risk.
4. The court *reiterated* a principle it had already stated twice that term.
5. Let me *reiterate* one point, because it got lost the first time.
6. I've *reiterated* this in three separate emails.

**See also:** [repeat](#repeat), [echo](#echo), [clarify](#clarify), [spell out](#spell-out), [recap](#recap)

### repeat

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈpit/ &middot; *ri-PEET*
**Type:** reporting verb — restatement, one's own or someone else's &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *repeat* + **that**-clause &middot; *repeat* + noun phrase &middot; *repeat* + free relative (*repeat what he said*) &middot; *repeat* + direct quotation &middot; *repeat* + noun phrase + **to** sb &middot; *repeat* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; *repeat oneself* &middot; *repeat* + **after** sb &middot; passive *be repeated*
**Never:** ***repeat me the question*** — no double object. The hearer takes *to* or *for*: *repeat the question for me* ✓, *repeat it to the class* ✓. No embedded question either — ***repeat whether he had met the man*** ✗ → *He repeated that he had never met the man* ✓; *repeat what he said* ✓ is a free relative, not a question. ***Repeat again*** is redundant in the same way as *reiterate again*, though it is far more common in speech and rarely worth correcting there.
**Reports:** saying something over — either restating your own point or passing along someone else's words. Unlike *echo*, it implies no agreement, and unlike *quote*, it does not promise the wording is exact.

> "As I said, the office is closed on Fridays," she said.  →  She **repeated that** the office **was** closed on Fridays.
> "Don't tell anyone I told you."  →  He asked me not to **repeat** what he **had** said.

1. He *repeated* that he had never met the man.
2. Could you *repeat* the last part? The line cut out.
3. She *repeated* the instructions slowly, one step at a time.
4. Don't *repeat* any of this to your brother.
5. The witness *repeated* what she had told police the night before.
6. The teacher had the class *repeat* each phrase after her.

**See also:** [reiterate](#reiterate), [echo](#echo), [recap](#recap), [quote](#quote), [clarify](#clarify)

### show

**Pronunciation:** /ʃoʊ/ &middot; *SHOH* — past participle *shown*; *showed* also occurs and is accepted, though *shown* is standard in careful American writing
**Type:** reporting verb — demonstration or evidence &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *show* + **that**-clause &middot; *show* + **wh**-clause &middot; *show* + **whether**-clause &middot; *show* + sb + **that**-clause &middot; *show* + sb + **wh**-clause &middot; *show* + sb + noun phrase &middot; *show* + noun phrase + **to** sb &middot; *show* + sb + **wh**-word + **to**-infinitive (*show me what to do*, *show me how to reset it*) &middot; passive *it has been shown that*
**Never:** ***show to me how it works*** as a substitute for the double object — *show* is one of the verbs that **does** take a personal object directly, so *show me how it works* is the natural form. Use *to* only when the thing shown comes first: *showed the photo to me* ✓, *showed me the photo* ✓, ***showed to me the photo*** ✗. And keep the **-ing** frame out of reports: *showed him leaving the building* is perception, not a report of anything said or established — for a report, *showed that he had left the building* ✓.
**Reports:** making something evident, either by displaying it or by supplying evidence for it. Learn it as the counterweight to *explain* and *demonstrate*: *show me* ✓, *explain to me* ✓, ***explain me*** ✗ — same meaning territory, opposite object rules. Like *prove* and *demonstrate* it is **factive**: *Every study shows the same pattern* asserts the pattern in the reporter's own voice.

> "Look at the chart — enrollment fell every year," she said.  →  She **showed that** enrollment **had** fallen every year.
> "Here's how you jump the battery."  →  He **showed me how to** jump the battery.

1. The receipts *show* that the shipment left on the ninth.
2. She *showed* me exactly where the pipe was leaking.
3. Every study on this *shows* the same pattern.
4. He *showed* the inspector how the alarm had been bypassed.
5. Let me *show* you what happens when you click the second tab.
6. The photos *show* what the building looked like before the fire.

**See also:** [demonstrate](#demonstrate), [prove](#prove), [explain](#explain), [indicate](#indicate), [point out](#point-out)

### speculate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈspɛkjəleɪt/ &middot; *SPEK-yuh-layt*
**Type:** reporting verb — guess offered as a guess &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *speculate* + **that**-clause &middot; *speculate* + **about / on** + noun phrase &middot; *speculate* + **about / on** + **wh**-clause &middot; *speculate* + **about / on** + **whether**-clause &middot; *speculate* + **as to** + **wh**-clause or noun phrase (formal) &middot; passive *it was speculated that*
**Never:** ***speculate someone that*** and ***speculate what happened*** — no personal object, and no bare **wh**-clause: the question needs a preposition. Repairs: *speculated about what had happened* ✓, *speculated that the roof had failed* ✓. No **to**-infinitive either: ***speculated the roof to have failed*** ✗. Keep this sense apart from the financial one, which takes *in* or *on* and a market (*speculated in currencies*).
**Reports:** a possibility floated without evidence, and labeled as such. It is the weakest claim in this group: *conclude* says the evidence settles it, *predict* commits the speaker, *speculate* explicitly declines to.

> "Maybe he was pushed out — who knows," he said.  →  He **speculated that** the director **had been** pushed out.
> "People keep asking whether she'll run again."  →  Reporters **speculated about whether** she **would** run again.

1. Analysts *speculated* that the merger had fallen apart over price.
2. She refused to *speculate* about an investigation she wasn't part of.
3. Neighbors *speculated* for weeks about who had bought the place.
4. I don't want to *speculate* on motives without more information.
5. The article *speculates* that a second site was under consideration.
6. It's pointless to *speculate* about what he would have wanted.
7. The memo *speculates* as to which of the three sites the company actually preferred.

**See also:** [predict](#predict), [assume](#assume), [suppose](#suppose), [wonder](#wonder), [conclude](#conclude)

### spell out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌspɛl ˈaʊt/ &middot; *spel OWT*
**Type:** reporting verb — explanation made explicit &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning informal
**Patterns:** *spell out* + noun phrase &middot; *spell out* + **that**-clause &middot; *spell out* + **wh**-clause &middot; *spell* + noun phrase + *out* (separable) &middot; *spell out* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; passive *be spelled out*
**Never:** ***spell out me the terms*** and ***spell out it*** — no personal object, and a **pronoun** object must go inside the verb: *spell it out* ✓, ***spell out it*** ✗. The hearer takes *for*: *spell it out for me* ✓. A full noun phrase may go either side (*spell out the terms* / *spell the terms out*), but a **that**-clause always follows the whole verb (*spelled out that the fee was nonrefundable*).
**Reports:** stating in plain, unmissable terms something that was implied or left vague. *Explain* fills a gap in the hearer's knowledge; *spell out* removes the hearer's room to pretend not to have understood.

> "Let me be blunt: miss this deadline and the contract is void," she said.  →  She **spelled out that** missing the deadline **would** void the contract.
> "Do I have to say it in so many words?"  →  He made her **spell it out**.

1. The email *spelled out* exactly what would happen if the balance went unpaid.
2. She *spelled out* the terms twice, and he signed anyway.
3. Do I have to *spell it out* for you?
4. The policy never *spells out* who is responsible for the deductible.
5. He *spelled out* that no exceptions would be made, for anyone, ever.
6. Somebody needs to *spell* the consequences *out* before the vote.

**See also:** [clarify](#clarify), [explain](#explain), [point out](#point-out), [reiterate](#reiterate), [demonstrate](#demonstrate)

### summarize

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌməraɪz/ &middot; *SUM-uh-ryze* — spelled *-ize* in American English; British *summarise*
**Type:** reporting verb — condensed restatement &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *summarize* + noun phrase &middot; *summarize* + **wh**-clause &middot; *summarize* + noun phrase + **for** sb &middot; *summarize* + noun phrase + **in** + noun phrase (*summarized the report in three findings*) &middot; *summarize* + **by** + **-ing** &middot; used absolutely (*Let me summarize*) &middot; **to summarize**, as a sentence adverbial &middot; passive *be summarized as*
**Never:** ***summarize me the report*** — no double object; the beneficiary takes *for*: *summarize the report for me* ✓, while ***summarize the report to me*** ✗ is not idiomatic. A **that**-clause is also a poor fit (***He summarized that the project was over budget***); use *summarized the findings* or shift to *concluded that* if a judgment is what you mean.
**Reports:** compressing a body of material into its main points. *Recap* replays events in order for people who were present; *summarize* boils down content for people who were not.

> "The whole report comes down to three findings," she said.  →  She **summarized** the report **in** three findings.
> "In short, the pilot worked and the rollout didn't."  →  He **summarized what had** gone wrong.

1. She *summarized* the forty-page filing in a single paragraph.
2. Could you *summarize* what the consultants actually recommended?
3. The abstract *summarizes* the study for readers who won't get past page one.
4. He *summarized* the debate so fairly that both sides complained.
5. To *summarize*: we're over budget and behind schedule.
6. The minutes *summarize* every motion but record no discussion.

**See also:** [recap](#recap), [paraphrase](#paraphrase), [conclude](#conclude), [clarify](#clarify), [repeat](#repeat)

### suppose

**Pronunciation:** /səˈpoʊz/ &middot; *suh-POHZ*
**Type:** reporting verb — tentative belief &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *suppose* + **that**-clause &middot; *suppose* + sb/sth + **to**-infinitive (chiefly passive: *is supposed to be*) &middot; imperative *suppose (that)…* introducing a hypothesis &middot; *suppose so / suppose not* &middot; passive *it is supposed that*
**Never:** ***suppose someone that*** and ***suppose to do something*** in the active — no personal object at all, and *I suppose to leave early* ✗ is a mangling of the passive idiom *I'm supposed to leave early* ✓. And no **wh**-clause and no **-ing**: ***suppose where he went*** ✗, ***suppose him leaving*** ✗.
**Negative raising:** *I don't suppose* usually negates the inner clause, not the supposing — *I don't suppose you know his number* means "I suppose you don't," and in practice it is a soft request: it is the politest way in American English to ask for something you expect to be refused.
**Reports:** a belief held without much conviction and offered with a shrug. *Assume* takes it as settled and moves on; *suppose* leaves it open and half-invites disagreement.

> "I guess that's fair," he said.  →  He **supposed** it **was** fair.
> "You probably don't have change for a twenty."  →  She said she **didn't suppose** I **had** change for a twenty.

1. I *suppose* we could push the meeting to Thursday.
2. She *supposed* the noise was just the furnace again.
3. "Is he any good?" — "I *suppose* so."
4. I don't *suppose* you'd know where the circuit breaker is?
5. *Suppose* the flight is canceled — what then?
6. You're *supposed* to check in an hour early for international flights.

**See also:** [assume](#assume), [think](#think), [believe](#believe), [reckon](#reckon), [figure](#figure), [gather](#gather)

### think

**Pronunciation:** /θɪŋk/ &middot; *THINGK* — the *n* is the *-ng* sound, not the *n* of *thin*
**Type:** reporting verb — opinion or belief; also unspoken thought &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default hedge on any statement
**Patterns:** *think* + **that**-clause (the *that* is usually dropped) &middot; *think* + sb/sth + **to be** (formal, chiefly passive: *is thought to be*) &middot; *think* + noun phrase + adjective (*think it odd*) &middot; *think* + **of / about** + noun phrase &middot; *think* + **about / of** + **-ing** &middot; *think* + **to**-infinitive (= remember to: *didn't think to ask*) &middot; *think* + **wh**-clause, exclamative or deliberative only (*Think what that would mean*, *I'm still thinking what to say*) &middot; *think so / think not* &middot; passive *it is thought that*
**Never:** ***think someone that*** and ***think to me that*** — no personal object and no hearer slot. *Think* also does not report a question: ***think where he had gone*** ✗ → *I wondered where he had gone* ✓; the **wh**-clause it does take is exclamative or deliberative, not an embedded question. Note also that *think to do something* means "remember to," not "intend to": *I didn't think to ask* ✓ means the question never occurred to me.
**Negative raising:** *I don't think he's coming* means *I think he isn't coming* — the negative attaches to the reporting verb but is understood inside the clause, and this is the ordinary way to say it. ***I think he isn't coming*** is grammatical but sounds oddly emphatic, and the tag question follows the inner clause: *I don't think he's coming, **is he?***
**Reports:** an opinion the speaker will not claim to be certain of. It is the least committed of the everyday belief verbs and the most common by a wide margin.

> "The store closes at nine, I'm pretty sure," she said.  →  She **thought** the store **closed** at nine.
> "It won't rain, in my opinion."  →  He **didn't think** it **would** rain.

1. I *think* the paperwork went out on Monday.
2. She *thought* the whole thing was a misunderstanding.
3. He's *thought* to be the largest single donor.
4. I don't *think* anyone actually read the terms.
5. Nobody *thought* to check whether the door locked behind them.
6. *Think* what this place would look like with the walls knocked out.

**See also:** [believe](#believe), [suppose](#suppose), [figure](#figure), [reckon](#reckon), [gather](#gather), [assume](#assume)

### understand

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndɚˈstænd/ &middot; *un-der-STAND*
**Type:** reporting verb — belief acquired from an unnamed source &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a staple of careful journalism and polite business writing
**Patterns:** *understand* + **that**-clause &middot; *understand* + **wh**-clause &middot; *understand* + noun phrase &middot; *understand* + **from** + noun phrase + **that**-clause (*I understand from your letter that…*) &middot; *understand* + sb/sth + **to be** (formal: *I understood him to be the owner*) &middot; passive *it is understood that*
**Never:** ***understand me that*** and ***understand to someone*** — no personal object in the reporting sense. (*Understand me* exists, but it is an imperative meaning "grasp what I am telling you," not a hearer slot.) No **-ing** either: ***understand him leaving*** ✗ → *I understand that he is leaving* ✓ or *I understand why he left* ✓.
**Reports:** something the speaker has been told and is passing on without vouching for it or naming who said it. *I understand the office is closing* means "I have heard this from somewhere I consider reliable" — it hedges the claim and protects the source at the same time, which is exactly why reporters use it.

> "Word is they've already picked someone," she said.  →  She **understood that** the position **had** already **been** filled.
> "You're the one handling the account, right?"  →  He **understood her to be** the account manager.

1. I *understand* the building changed hands last month.
2. We *understand* that no charges have been filed.
3. She *understood* the deposit to be refundable, and it wasn't.
4. It is *understood* that the two sides met privately on Tuesday.
5. I *understand* why they did it; I just don't agree with it.
6. Am I right in *understanding* that the fee covers both years?

**See also:** [gather](#gather), [believe](#believe), [infer](#infer), [think](#think), [assume](#assume)

### want to know

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɑnt tə ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *wont tuh NOH* — in running speech *want to* reduces to *wanna*: */ˈwɑnə/*
**Type:** reporting verb — question, often relayed live &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Patterns:** *want to know* + **wh**-clause &middot; *want to know* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *want to know* + noun phrase (*want to know the reason*) &middot; *want to know* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *want to know* + **from** sb + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***want to know me why*** and ***want me to know why*** as a report — the first has no object slot at all, and the second is a different sentence entirely (it reports a wish about what *you* should know). The person asked takes *from*: *She wanted to know from the manager why the account was frozen* ✓.
**Reports:** a question, usually one being relayed to the person who can answer it. Because the reporting verb is often in the **present tense** (*She wants to know…*), nothing backshifts, and the report reads as live: *She wants to know **whether you're** free* keeps the present because the question is still open.

> "Why hasn't anyone called me back?" she said.  →  She **wants to know why** nobody **has** called **her** back.
> "Is the deposit refundable?" he asked.  →  He **wanted to know whether** the deposit **was** refundable.

1. Your sister *wants to know* what time we're eating.
2. He *wanted to know* whether the warranty covered water damage.
3. The client *wants to know* why the invoice went up by four hundred dollars.
4. I *want to know* who signed off on this.
5. She *wanted to know* about the school district before making an offer.
6. Everybody *wants to know* how much it costs and nobody wants to ask.

**See also:** [demand to know](#demand-to-know), [ask](#ask), [wonder](#wonder), [inquire](#inquire), [Present-tense reporting verbs](../03-no-backshift.md#present-tense-reporting-verbs)

### wonder

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌndɚ/ &middot; *WUN-der*
**Type:** reporting verb — unspoken question &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *wonder* + **if / whether**-clause &middot; *wonder* + **wh**-clause &middot; *wonder* + **whether** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *wonder* + **wh**-word + **to**-infinitive (*wonder what to do*) &middot; *wonder* + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *wonder* + **at** + noun phrase (= marvel, literary) &middot; *wonder* + **that**-clause (= be surprised that — literary: *I don't wonder that she left*)
**Never:** ***wonder if to wait*** — only *whether* may stand in front of a *to*-infinitive: *wondered whether to wait* ✓, ***wondered if to wait*** ✗. Also ***wonder me*** and ***wonder someone whether*** — no personal object, since nothing was said to anybody. And keep statement order inside the clause: ***I wondered where was he*** ✗ → *I wondered where he was* ✓. Do not use the **that**-clause to report a question: *He wondered that she was home* does not mean he asked himself whether she was home — it means, in a literary register, that he was surprised she was home. For the question, *He wondered whether she was home* ✓.
**Reports:** a question the speaker asked themselves and did not say out loud. The contrast with *ask* is real and worth preserving: *He asked whether she was home* means he spoke; *He wondered whether she was home* means he did not.
**The polite formula:** *I was wondering if…* is not a report at all — it is a softened request, and the past continuous is doing the softening. *I was wondering if you had a minute* is a question about now, ends in a period, and expects an answer.

> "Is anyone actually reading this?"  →  She **wondered whether** anyone **was** actually reading it.
> "Should I call her today?" she thought.  →  She **wondered whether to** call her **that day**.

1. I *wondered* why the lights were still on at two in the morning.
2. She *wondered* whether she'd left the stove on, and turned the car around.
3. He *wondered* what to say to someone he hadn't spoken to in fifteen years.
4. We all *wondered* about the second signature on the form.
5. It makes you *wonder* how many other files are missing.
6. I was *wondering* if you could take a look at this before Friday.
7. I don't *wonder* that she left, after the way that meeting went. *(literary — "I'm not surprised")*
