Asking, wondering, explaining, and concluding#

The verbs that report a question, an explanation, or the conclusion somebody drew — everything a speaker does with information other than simply assert it.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

Three speech acts share one piece of syntax here. A verb that reports a question takes an embedded question — a wh-clause or an if/whether-clause with statement word order and no question mark (She asked where the office was, never where was the office). A verb that reports an explanation or a conclusion takes a that-clause, and almost all of them take a wh-clause too, which is why explain what happened and explain that nothing happened are equally good. The trap that runs through the whole group is the personal object: tell, show, and ask take one directly (told me what happened, show me how it works), while explain, point out, demonstrate, indicate, and inquire refuse it outright — explain me is wrong in every register of American English, and the repair is always a preposition (explain to me). The conclusion verbs add one more dimension the question verbs do not have: how much the reporter is willing to bet on the content, running from prove and point out, which take the statement as true, through think and gather, down to speculate, which offers it as a guess.

Tell is the mirror image of explain: it requires the personal object, so He told that he was late ✗ is exactly as wrong as He explained me that he was late ✗. The repairs run in opposite directions — told me that he was late ✓ and explained to me that he was late ✓.

Three more groups sit behind that table. The restating verbs — clarify, summarize, recap, paraphrase, spell out, repeat — put the beneficiary on for, not to, and never bare: summarize the report for me ✓, summarize me the report ✗. The person-only verbs — question, quiz, interrogate, consult — take the person as their object and no clause of their own at all, so the subject matter has to arrive on about: quizzed her about the flight ✓, quizzed whether the flight was on time ✗. And the thinking verbs — think, believe, suppose, assume, gather, reckon, figure, wonder — have no hearer slot of any kind, because nothing was necessarily said to anybody.

Which verbs take an embedded question. Most verbs here that take a that-clause take a wh-clause too, but not all of them, and the exceptions are a tidy set worth memorizing.

The middle column is the one that trips learners: I assumed where he had gone ✗, She concluded what had gone wrong ✗, I gather where he went ✗. Those verbs report a settled proposition, and a question is not one — the repair is either a that-clause (concluded that the seal had failed) or a different verb (worked out what had gone wrong). Several of them will accept a question on a preposition instead: argued about whether to go ✓, speculated about what had happened ✓. The right-hand column is the reverse — a question verb needs a question, so She asked that the office was closed ✗ and He wondered that she was home ✗ (except in the literary "be surprised" sense of wonder).

One thing that looks like an embedded question but is not: repeat what he said, echo what she had said, paraphrase what the memo said, reiterate what she said in June. That what-clause is a free relative — it means "the thing he said," a noun phrase — and it is available to every verb in the table, including the ones that refuse questions. The test is whether you can substitute a plain noun phrase: repeat the question ✓ proves it is a free relative; repeat whether he had met the man ✗ proves repeat still takes no real question.

How much the verb commits the reporter. Choosing among these verbs is not a neutral act. Several of them state the reporter's own position on the content, not just the speaker's, and a learner who picks by meaning alone will editorialize by accident.

Write He pointed out that the figures were wrong and you have said the figures were wrong. Write He claimed that the figures were wrong and you have not. Write He assumed the figures were wrong and you have accused him of not checking. The same trap runs the other way with understand and gather, which pass a claim along while quietly declining to stand behind it.

argue#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑrɡju/ · AR-gyoo Type: reporting verb — reasoned claim · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: argue + that-clause · argue + for / against + noun phrase · argue + for / against + -ing · argue + about / over + noun phrase · argue + about / over + wh-clause · argue + with + person · argue + sb + into / out of + -ing · argue + noun phrase (argue a case, argue the point) · passive it is argued that Never: argue someone to do something and argue someone that — argue takes no personal object in front of a clause. The verb for talking a person into something is persuade or convince: He argued that we should wait ✓ / He persuaded us to wait ✓ / He argued us to wait ✗. The one genuine personal-object frame is argue + sb + into / out of + -ing, and it reports persuasion rather than a claim: She argued him out of resigning ✓. And no bare question clause — argue why the plan would fail ✗, argue whether to go ✗ — the question has to ride in on a preposition: argued about why the plan would fail ✓, argued over whether to go ✓. Reports: a position put forward with reasons attached to it. Claim signals the reporter's doubt and prove concedes the case; argue is neutral on whether the reasoning worked, reporting only that reasoning was offered.

"The data is too thin to act on," she said. → She argued that the data was too thin to act on. "We should hire before January," he said. → He argued for hiring before January.

  1. The defense argued that the search had been illegal from the start.
  2. Economists argue that the credit mostly benefits people who would have bought anyway.
  3. She argued for a small pilot before anyone committed to a full rollout.
  4. They spent an hour arguing about whether the deposit was refundable.
  5. Nobody argued that the old system was better — only that it was cheaper.
  6. He argued against moving the deadline, and the committee overruled him.
  7. She argued him out of resigning, and he stayed another four years.
  8. It has long been argued that the tax pays for itself, and it has never been shown.

See also: reason, conclude, point out, prove, demonstrate

ask#

Pronunciation: /æsk/ · ASK Type: reporting verb — question, and with an infinitive a request · Register: neutral; the default verb for reporting any question Patterns: ask + wh-clause · ask + if / whether-clause · ask + sb + wh-clause · ask + sb + if / whether-clause · ask + noun phrase (ask a question) · ask + sb + noun phrase (ask me a favor) · ask + to-infinitive (ask to leave — you want to act) · ask + sb + to-infinitive (ask me to leave — you want them to act) · ask + that-clause + subjunctive (formal request) · ask + for + noun phrase · ask + sb + for + noun phrase · ask + about / after + noun phrase · ask + wh-word + to-infinitive · ask + direct quotation, as a quotative tag ("Where do you live?" she asked) · passive be asked to do something Never: ask to someone whether — the person goes straight after the verb with no preposition: She asked me whether I was free, never She asked to me whether. And never keep question word order inside the clause: He asked where was I ✗ → He asked where I was ✓. Ask takes no -ing complement — ask leaving early ✗ → She asked to leave early ✓ — and it cannot take a that-clause in the question sense: ask that she was free ✗ → She asked whether she was free ✓. Ask that is always a request, and then it takes the subjunctive (She asked that the room be cleared), never a question. Reports: a question actually spoken. It is the unmarked choice, carrying no formality (inquire), no impatience (demand to know), and no doubt about the answer (query).

"Where do you live?" she asked. → She asked where I lived. "Are you free Thursday?" he asked me. → He asked me whether I was free that Thursday.

  1. The nurse asked whether I had eaten anything since midnight.
  2. He asked me why the invoice was two hundred dollars short.
  3. She asked to see the manager, and then asked the manager for a refund.
  4. Somebody in the back asked how long the recording would stay up.
  5. I asked about the deposit twice and never got a straight answer.
  6. Don't ask me what he meant by that.
  7. The witness was asked to remain in the building until the hearing ended.
  8. She asked the front desk for a later checkout and got it.

See also: inquire, wonder, want to know, query, question, Reported questions, Requests: the ask family

assume#

Pronunciation: /əˈsum/ · uh-SOOM — American English drops the y glide; British uh-SYOOM Type: reporting verb — belief taken for granted · Register: neutral Patterns: assume + that-clause · assume + sb/sth + to-infinitive, nearly always to be (formal: assumed him to be the owner) · assume + noun phrase · assume + so / not · passive it is assumed that · passive sb is assumed to be Never: assume me that and assume to someone that — no personal object and no to-phrase for the hearer, because assume reports a private belief rather than something said to anybody. Assume also takes no wh-clause and no -ing: assume where he had gone ✗ and assume him going ✗ → I assumed he had gone home ✓ or I guessed where he had gone ✓. Reports: a belief adopted without checking it. That is the judgment the word carries: conclude implies evidence was weighed, assume implies it was skipped, which is why You assumed I'd say yes is a complaint and You concluded I'd say yes is not.

"You sent it already, I take it," he said. → He assumed I had already sent it. "Everyone here has a login, presumably." → She assumed that everyone there had a login.

  1. I assumed the meeting was still on, since nobody said otherwise.
  2. The estimate assumes that materials arrive by the first of the month.
  3. She wrongly assumed the fee covered parking.
  4. Let's not assume he saw the email — it's easy to miss.
  5. The report assumed the plant to be operating at full capacity.
  6. It is generally assumed that the two cases are related.
  7. "Is the deal still on?" — "I assume so, but nobody has confirmed it."

See also: suppose, gather, believe, think, infer, conclude

believe#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈliv/ · bi-LEEV Type: reporting verb — belief, often hedged · Register: neutral to formal; slightly more careful than think Patterns: believe + that-clause · believe + sb/sth + to-infinitive (formal) · believe + noun phrase (believe her, believe the story) · believe + in + noun phrase · believe + in + -ing · believe so / believe not · passive sb is believed to be · passive it is believed that Never: believe to me that and believe me that the door was locked — believe me means "trust what I say," so it cannot double as the hearer slot of a report; the repair is told me the door was locked ✓. Believe also takes no wh-clause and no bare -ing: believe where he went ✗, believe him lying ✗ → I believe he is lying ✓. Reports: something held to be true without direct knowledge of it. Against know, it concedes the possibility of error; against think, it is a touch more formal and more committed, which is why news writing uses is believed to be and not is thought to be when it wants to sound careful.

"The building went up sometime in the 1920s," she said. → She believed the building had gone up sometime in the 1920s. "He's the owner, I'm fairly sure." → She believed him to be the owner.

  1. I believe the office closes at four on Fridays.
  2. Investigators believe the fire started in the crawl space.
  3. She didn't believe a word of it.
  4. The painting is believed to be a copy made around 1810.
  5. We have no reason to believe that anyone else was involved.
  6. Do you believe he actually said that?
  7. She believes in saying exactly what she means, which has cost her more than one client.
  8. It is widely believed that the two companies had already agreed on a price.

See also: think, suppose, understand, gather, assume

calculate#

Pronunciation: /ˈkælkjəleɪt/ · KAL-kyuh-layt Type: reporting verb — conclusion reached by figuring · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: calculate + that-clause · calculate + wh-clause · calculate + noun phrase · calculate + noun phrase + at + amount · passive it is calculated that · passive be calculated to (= designed to) Never: calculate someone that and calculate me the total — no personal object in either slot; if you need a hearer at all, use for: calculated the total for me ✓. And keep the two to-forms apart: be calculated to means "designed to produce an effect" (a remark calculated to offend), not "worked out that" — He calculated the trip to take four hours ✗ → He calculated that the trip would take four hours ✓. Reports: a figure or a judgment arrived at by working something out, spoken aloud. Estimate admits the number is approximate; calculate claims it was derived.

"At this burn rate we've got eleven months," he said. → He calculated that they had eleven months left at that burn rate. "How much will the tariffs add?" → Analysts calculated how much the tariffs would add.

  1. She calculated that the detour would cost them forty minutes.
  2. The actuary calculated how much the policy would pay out over thirty years.
  3. He calculated the total in his head and came up two dollars short.
  4. The city calculates its water rates on a sliding scale.
  5. Nobody had calculated what the overtime would do to the budget.
  6. The insult was calculated to land in front of an audience.

See also: estimate, conclude, reckon, figure, predict

cite#

Pronunciation: /saɪt/ · SYTE — a homophone of site and sight Type: reporting verb — attributed source or reason · Register: formal; standard in journalism, law, and academic writing Patterns: cite + noun phrase · cite + noun phrase + as + noun phrase (cited the ruling as precedent) · cite + sb + as saying (+ that-clause) · cite + noun phrase + for + noun phrase (= issue a citation: cited him for speeding) · cite + noun phrase + in support of / as grounds for + noun phrase · passive be cited as · passive be cited for Never: cite that the deal was dead — cite takes a noun phrase, not a that-clause. Repairs: cited the collapse of the deal, cited sources as saying that the deal was dead, or simply said that the deal was dead. It takes no personal object for the hearer either (cited me the ruling ✗ → cited the ruling to me ✓) and no embedded question (cited why the deal died ✗ → cited the reason the deal died ✓). Reports: the source or the reason behind a statement rather than the statement itself. Quote reproduces the words; cite names where they came from or what they support.

"I'm resigning for family reasons," he said. → He resigned, citing family reasons. "Two officials told us the vote was postponed." → The paper cited two officials as saying the vote had been postponed.

  1. She cited three studies, none of which said what she claimed.
  2. The judge cited an 1897 ruling that nobody in the room had read.
  3. He turned down the offer, citing the commute.
  4. The article cites an anonymous source close to the negotiations.
  5. Both reports cite the same faulty sensor as the cause.
  6. You have to cite every source you paraphrase, not just the ones you quote.

See also: quote, paraphrase, indicate, point out, echo

clarify#

Pronunciation: /ˈklɛrəfaɪ/ · KLAIR-uh-fye Type: reporting verb — explanation that removes an ambiguity · Register: neutral to formal; heavy in business and official speech Patterns: clarify + that-clause · clarify + wh-clause · clarify + whether-clause · clarify + noun phrase · clarify + noun phrase + for sb · clarify + to / for sb + that-clause · passive it was clarified that Never: clarify me the rules — no personal object. The beneficiary takes for far more often than to: Could you clarify that for me? is the natural form, and Could you clarify me that? is not English. Clarify also takes no to-infinitive and no -ing: clarify to be leaving ✗, clarify sending it ✗ → She clarified that she was leaving ✓, He clarified that he had sent it ✓. Reports: a second statement that fixes the meaning of a first one. Explain supplies information that was missing; clarify repairs information that was already there and came out ambiguous. Note what that presupposes: calling something a clarification concedes that the first statement was unclear, which is why officials reach for it when they are walking a remark back without admitting they changed it.

"To be clear, the deadline is Friday, not Monday," she said. → She clarified that the deadline was Friday, not Monday. "What I meant was the second invoice." → He clarified which invoice he had meant.

  1. She clarified that the discount applies only to the first order.
  2. Could you clarify what "reasonable notice" means in this contract?
  3. The spokesman clarified the timeline for reporters the next morning.
  4. Let me clarify one thing before we go any further.
  5. The memo was sent out to clarify who signs off on refunds.
  6. He clarified for the committee that no money had actually changed hands.

See also: explain, spell out, point out, indicate, reiterate

conclude#

Pronunciation: /kənˈklud/ · kun-KLOOD Type: reporting verb — judgment reached from evidence · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: conclude + that-clause · conclude + from + noun phrase + that-clause · conclude + noun phrase (conclude an agreement — a different sense) · conclude + by + -ing (= finish) · conclude + with + noun phrase (= finish) · passive it was concluded that Never: conclude someone that and conclude to do something — no personal object, and no infinitive in the active. And no wh-clause: conclude what had gone wrong ✗ → She concluded that the seal had failed ✓ or She worked out what had gone wrong ✓. Keep the two senses apart as well: conclude + noun phrase means finish or settle (concluded the meeting, concluded a deal), never decide about — conclude the cause ✗ → He concluded that the cause was electrical ✓. Reports: a judgment presented as the endpoint of reasoning. Assume skips the evidence, speculate offers a guess, conclude claims the evidence has been weighed and the matter settled.

"So the leak has to be upstream of the valve," she said. → She concluded that the leak had to be upstream of the valve. "Given all that, I don't think he knew." → She concluded he hadn't known.

  1. The board concluded that no policy had been violated.
  2. From the tone of the email I concluded we weren't getting the contract.
  3. Two separate reviews concluded the same thing.
  4. The inspector concluded that the crack was cosmetic.
  5. She concluded her remarks by thanking the outgoing treasurer.
  6. It would be premature to conclude that the trend is permanent.

See also: infer, deduce, gather, reason, argue, assume

consult#

Pronunciation: /kənˈsʌlt/ · kun-SULT Type: reporting verb — question put to an authority · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: consult + person or reference (consult a lawyer, consult the manual) · consult + sb + about / on + noun phrase · consult + with + sb (standard American) · consult + with + sb + about / on + noun phrase · consult + sb + about + wh-clause · consult + about + noun phrase · passive be consulted about Never: consult to someone and consult me whether the road was open — consult never takes a clause of its own. The question has to ride in on a preposition: consulted the ranger about whether the road was open ✓. It takes no to-infinitive either: consulted him to fix the wiring ✗ → consulted him about the wiring ✓ or asked him to fix the wiring ✓. Reports: asking someone with standing to answer — a professional, a document, a colleague whose agreement you need. Ask is neutral about who is being asked; consult implies expertise or authority on the other end.

"What are my options here?" she asked her attorney. → She consulted her attorney about her options. "Is this covered?" he asked, and called the insurer. → He consulted with the insurer about whether it was covered.

  1. Consult your doctor before you double the dose.
  2. She consulted two contractors and got two very different numbers.
  3. He consulted the manual, which turned out to be for a different model.
  4. The mayor said she would consult with the council before signing anything.
  5. Nobody consulted the people who actually use the building.
  6. I'd consult an accountant about that if I were you.

See also: ask, inquire, query, question, quiz

deduce#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈdus/ · di-DOOSS — American English drops the glide; British di-DYOOSS Type: reporting verb — conclusion drawn by strict inference · Register: formal to neutral; slightly literary Patterns: deduce + that-clause · deduce + wh-clause · deduce + noun phrase · deduce + noun phrase + from + noun phrase · deduce + from + noun phrase + that-clause · passive it can be deduced that Never: deduce someone that and deduce to someone — no personal object. Deduce also has no -ing frame and no infinitive: deduce her being there ✗, deduce her to have been there ✗ → He deduced that she had been there ✓. Reports: a conclusion the speaker presents as forced by the premises. Infer leaves room for probability; deduce claims necessity, which is why the detective deduces and the reader infers.

"The mud is still wet, so he can't have left before noon," he said. → He deduced that the man could not have left before noon. "Only three people had a key." → She deduced who the visitor had been.

  1. From the postmark she deduced that the letter had sat somewhere for a week.
  2. He deduced the password from a photograph of the desk.
  3. You can deduce how old the tree is by counting the rings.
  4. Nothing in the file lets you deduce who authorized the payment.
  5. She deduced, correctly, that the meeting had been canceled without her.
  6. The whole plot depends on a conclusion no reader could possibly deduce.

See also: infer, conclude, gather, reason, figure

demand to know#

Pronunciation: /dɪˌmænd tə ˈnoʊ/ · di-MAND tuh NOH Type: reporting verb — question asked with force · Register: neutral, but strongly marked for confrontation; common in news writing Patterns: demand to know + wh-clause · demand to know + if / whether-clause · demand to know + noun phrase (demand to know the reason) · demand to know + from sb + wh-clause Never: demand me to know and demand to know me why — nothing may come between demand and to know, and no personal object follows. The person addressed rides on a preposition or not at all: She demanded to know from the manager why the account had been frozen ✓. Note also that plain demand + that-clause takes the subjunctive (demanded that he leave), a request rather than a question. Reports: a question asked as a challenge, with the implication that an answer is owed. It is the confrontational end of ask and the exact opposite of the deferential I was wondering.

"Who authorized this?" she said. → She demanded to know who had authorized it. "Are you even licensed?" he said. → He demanded to know whether she was even licensed.

  1. He demanded to know why his bag had been searched.
  2. Shareholders demanded to know what the severance package cost.
  3. She called back and demanded to know whether the charge had been reversed.
  4. A reporter demanded to know who was paying for the flights.
  5. The parents demanded to know how the school had missed it for two years.
  6. I don't think you're in a position to demand to know anything.

See also: want to know, ask, question, interrogate, Reported questions

demonstrate#

Pronunciation: /ˈdɛmənstreɪt/ · DEM-un-strayt Type: reporting verb — proof by showing · Register: formal Patterns: demonstrate + that-clause · demonstrate + wh-clause · demonstrate + noun phrase · demonstrate + to sb + that-clause · demonstrate + to sb + wh-clause · demonstrate + noun phrase + to sb · demonstrate + how / what + to-infinitive · passive it has been demonstrated that Never: demonstrate me how it works — this is the same error as explain me, and it is worth learning as a pair with show, which does take the object: show me how it works ✓, demonstrate to me how it works ✓, demonstrate me how it works ✗. The block holds in front of every complement, so demonstrate the committee that the lock could be picked ✗ → demonstrated to the committee that… ✓. And no -ing: demonstrate the seal failing ✗ → demonstrated that the seal failed ✓. Reports: establishing something by making it visible — an experiment, a walkthrough, a table of results. Show is the everyday word for the same act; demonstrate is its formal counterpart and implies a deliberate display. Both are factive, like point out: write The trial demonstrated that the drug worked and you have stated as your own that the drug worked. If you are only reporting a claim, the verb is said or claimed.

"Watch — the seal fails at forty pounds," she said. → She demonstrated that the seal failed at forty pounds. "Here's how you reset it." → He demonstrated how to reset it.

  1. The trial demonstrated that the drug worked no better than a placebo.
  2. She demonstrated to the committee how easily the lock could be picked.
  3. The instructor demonstrated the technique twice before letting anyone try.
  4. These numbers demonstrate what happens when maintenance gets deferred.
  5. Nothing in the audit demonstrates fraud — only sloppiness.
  6. He demonstrated to us that the software had been logging keystrokes.

See also: show, prove, explain, indicate, point out

echo#

Pronunciation: /ˈɛkoʊ/ · EK-oh — third person echoes, past echoed Type: reporting verb — repetition of someone else's statement · Register: neutral to formal; common in journalism Patterns: echo + noun phrase (echo his words, echo the sentiment) · echo + sb (echoed the senator) · echo + free relative (echoed what she had said) · echo + that-clause (journalistic and marginal — the object is normally a noun phrase) Never: echo to someone that and echo someone that — no hearer slot at all. What is echoed is the object: She echoed his warning, never She echoed us his warning. And echo takes no embedded question: He echoed whether the plan would work ✗, He echoed why she had left ✗. Echoed what she had said ✓ only looks like an exception — that what-clause is a free relative standing in for a noun phrase ("the thing she had said"), not a question. Treat echo + that-clause as journalistic shorthand and prefer echoed the point that… or agreed that… in careful prose. Reports: repeating what someone else has already said, with agreement implied. Repeat says the same words again, possibly your own; echo says someone else's, and takes their side by doing so. It carries a second, quieter judgment as well: an echo adds nothing, so He merely echoed the party line is a criticism in a way that He repeated the party line is not.

"I said the same thing last month," he said. → He echoed the point she had made the month before. "She's right — the schedule is impossible." → He echoed her complaint about the schedule.

  1. Several council members echoed her concerns about the parking plan.
  2. The governor's statement echoed language used by the industry group almost word for word.
  3. He echoed what the previous speaker had said and sat down.
  4. The review echoes a complaint that shows up in half the ratings.
  5. Her boss echoed the sentiment without committing to anything.
  6. Every op-ed that week echoed the same three talking points.

See also: repeat, reiterate, quote, paraphrase, cite

enquire#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈkwaɪr/ · in-KWYRE Type: reporting verb — question · Register: British spelling. American English writes inquire in every sense; enquire in an American document reads as a typo or an affectation Patterns: identical to inquire — enquire + wh-clause · enquire + if / whether-clause · enquire + about / after / into + noun phrase · enquire + of sb + whether (very formal) Never: enquire me — like inquire, it takes no personal object, and no that-clause either (enquired that the position was open ✗). And in American writing, avoid the spelling itself: use inquire. British usage splits the two by sense — enquire to ask a question, inquire to investigate formally — but that split is not observed in the United States, where inquire covers both. The noun follows the verb: British enquiry, American inquiry. Other British spellings in this territory: enquire is one of a family a learner will meet in the same sources, and every one of them is wrong in an American document — British summarise, apologise, criticise, recognise, emphasise, analyse → American summarize, apologize, criticize, recognize, emphasize, analyze. Two further differences are not spelling at all but grammar: British reckon to do something (I reckon to finish by six) is not American, and British writers use query + wh-clause more freely than American ones do. Reports: exactly what inquire reports: a question asked formally or politely. The entry exists here so the spelling can be recognized in British sources and then not copied.

"Is the position still open?" she asked. (British) → She enquired whether the position was still open. (American) → She inquired whether the position was still open.

  1. (British usage) She enquired about the opening hours of the archive.
  2. (British usage) He enquired whether a table by the window was available.
  3. (British usage) Guests should enquire at the front desk.
  4. In American English, that last sentence would read inquire at the front desk.
  5. The style guide flags enquire and replaces it with inquire automatically.
  6. British firms often label the contact page Enquiries; American ones write Inquiries.

See also: inquire, ask, query, inquire, not enquire

estimate#

Pronunciation: verb /ˈɛstəmeɪt/ · ES-tuh-mayt; noun /ˈɛstəmət/ · ES-tuh-mit — the last vowel is full in the verb and reduced in the noun Type: reporting verb — approximate figure · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: estimate + that-clause · estimate + wh-clause (estimate how many) · estimate + noun phrase · estimate + noun phrase + at + amount · estimate + noun phrase + to be + value (formal: estimated the loss to be four million dollars) · passive be estimated to be / at · passive it is estimated that Never: estimate someone that and estimate me the cost — no personal object in either slot. The infinitive is narrower than it looks: estimate + noun phrase + to be is standard in formal writing (estimated the age of the tree to be two hundred years ✓), but there is no object-less infinitive (estimated to take a week ✗ in the active), and an eventive infinitive reads badly even with an object (estimated repairs to take a week ✗). Say estimated that repairs would take a week ✓. Reports: a number offered as approximate. Calculate claims the figure was derived and is exact; estimate concedes up front that it is not.

"Somewhere around eight thousand people showed up," he said. → He estimated that about eight thousand people had shown up. "Call it three weeks, give or take." → She estimated the work at three weeks.

  2. Police estimated the crowd at twelve hundred; organizers said four thousand.
  3. Can you estimate how much of the budget goes to overtime?
  4. The damage is estimated at nine million dollars.
  5. She estimated the drive at four hours and was off by two.
  6. Nobody could estimate what the delay would cost.
  7. The appraiser estimated the collection to be worth about sixty thousand dollars.

explain#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈspleɪn/ · ik-SPLAYN Type: reporting verb — explanation · Register: neutral Patterns: explain + that-clause · explain + wh-clause · explain + whether-clause · explain + noun phrase · explain + to sb + that-clause · explain + to sb + wh-clause · explain + noun phrase + to sb · explain + how / what / why + to-infinitive · explain oneself · passive it was explained that Never: explain me something — this is the single most common error in the whole group. Explain takes no personal object before its complement; the hearer must arrive on to. Repairs: explain it to me, explain to me what happened, explain to me that it was an accident. The same block applies to explain me how ✗ → explain to me how ✓. Note that tell and show behave the opposite way (tell me what happened, show me what happened), which is exactly why the error is so persistent. Explain also takes no -ing and no to-infinitive complement: explain losing the file ✗, explain to have lost the file ✗ → He explained that he had lost the file ✓ or He explained how he had lost the file ✓. Word order: when both the hearer and the content are present, the short one goes first. Explain it to me ✓ / explain to me it ✗; but with a full clause the to-phrase leads: He explained to the class why the experiment had failed ✓, and He explained why the experiment had failed to the class is grammatical but reads as though the experiment failed at the class. Reports: supplying the information or the reason that makes something understandable. Say just reports words; explain claims those words answered a why or a how.

"The server was down all morning, so nothing went out," he said. → He explained that the server had been down all morning. "Here's why the check bounced." → She explained to me why the check had bounced.

  1. She explained that the fee is charged per bag, not per passenger.
  2. Could you explain to me why my account was closed?
  3. He explained the rules twice and half the room still got it wrong.
  4. Nobody ever explained how the two departments were supposed to share the budget.
  5. The label explains what to do if the paint gets on your skin.
  6. I tried to explain the situation to my landlord and got nowhere.

figure#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɪɡjɚ/ · FIG-yer — American English keeps the y glide; British FIG-uh Type: reporting verb — casual conclusion · Register: informal; distinctly American Patterns: figure + that-clause (the that is nearly always dropped) · figure + sb/sth + for + noun phrase (figured him for a cop) · figure + on + noun phrase · figure + on + -ing · figure + to-infinitive (= be likely to: the game figures to be close) · figure out + wh-clause · figure out + that-clause · figure out + noun phrase · figure + noun phrase + out (separable: figure it out) Never: figure someone that and figure me the answer — no personal object in either slot. Keep figure apart from figure out: plain figure takes a that-clause and reports a guess (I figured he'd be late), while figure out takes a wh-clause and reports a solved problem (I figured out why he was late). figure why he was late is not English. Keep the two to-forms apart as well: X figures to be Y means "is likely to be" and wants a non-personal subject (the game figures to be close ✓), while I figured to leave early meaning "I intended to" is dialectal at best — say I figured I'd leave early ✓. And the particle rule holds: figure it out ✓, figure out it ✗. Reports: a working assumption arrived at quickly and offered without much weight behind it. It is the everyday American equivalent of reckon and suppose, and it is at home in speech and dialogue but not in a report or a brief.

"He'll be late, I bet," she said. → She figured he would be late. "I couldn't work out why the door stuck." → He couldn't figure out why the door stuck.

  2. She figured the store would be closed by the time they got there.
  3. We figured on about an hour of traffic and got two.
  4. He figured her for a reporter the moment she pulled out a notebook.
  5. It took the mechanic ten minutes to figure out what was rattling.
  6. Go ahead and start — we'll figure out who's paying later.
  7. With both starters hurt, the game figures to be low-scoring.
  8. She finally figured out that the two invoices were for the same job.

forecast#

gather#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡæðɚ/ · GA-ther Type: reporting verb — secondhand understanding · Register: neutral, leaning conversational and polite Patterns: gather + that-clause (the that is usually dropped) · gather + from + noun phrase + that-clause · gather + as much / so (So I gathered) · I gather as a parenthetical (You're leaving, I gather) Never: gather someone that and gather to someone — no personal object. Gather also avoids the wh-clause and the -ing: gather where he went ✗, gather him leaving ✗; use the fixed phrase from what I gather instead, or a that-clause. Reports: a belief picked up indirectly, with the source deliberately left vague. I gather you're leaving means someone told me, I am not saying who, and I am giving you room to correct it — which is why it works as a polite opener where I heard would sound like gossip. It also hedges: like understand, it passes the claim along without vouching for it, so the reporter is not on the hook if it turns out to be wrong.

"People are saying the branch is closing." → She gathered that the branch was closing. "So you two have met, then." → He gathered they had already met.

  1. I gather you've already spoken to legal about this.
  2. From the silence in the room she gathered that the news was bad.
  3. He gathered the position had been filled weeks earlier.
  4. "They're not coming." — "So I gathered."
  5. From what I gather, the lease runs through next spring.
  6. We gathered from her tone that the conversation was over.

indicate#

Pronunciation: /ˈɪndəkeɪt/ · IN-di-kayt Type: reporting verb — guarded statement or signal · Register: formal; heavy in official, legal, and news writing Patterns: indicate + that-clause · indicate + wh-clause · indicate + whether-clause · indicate + noun phrase · indicate + to sb + that-clause · indicate + to sb + wh-clause · passive it was indicated that Never: indicate me that and indicate someone something — no personal object; the hearer takes to, as in indicated to reporters that no arrest was imminent. infer#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈfɚ/ · in-FUR — doubles the r: inferred, inferring Type: reporting verb — conclusion drawn by the listener · Register: formal to neutral Patterns: infer + that-clause · infer + wh-clause · infer + noun phrase · infer + noun phrase + from + noun phrase · infer + from + noun phrase + that-clause · passive it can be inferred that Never: infer someone that and infer to someone — no personal object, and no -ing (inferred him lying ✗). And do not use infer to mean imply: Are you inferring that I lied? is a very common error for implying. The speaker implies; the listener infers. The mistaken sense is old and widespread in speech, and it is still corrected everywhere it matters, so it is worth keeping straight. Reports: what a hearer concluded from what was said, not what the speaker said. Deduce claims necessity; infer claims only that the conclusion was reasonable.

  2. Readers were left to infer what the company actually planned to do.
  3. She inferred from the invoice that the work had already been done.
  4. You can't infer causation from a chart like that.
  5. He inferred a threat where none was intended.
  6. What am I supposed to infer from a two-word reply?

It is the honest label for indirect speech itself: a quotation reproduces the words, a paraphrase keeps the meaning and drops the wording, which is why paraphrased material still needs a citation.

predict#

question#

Pronunciation: /ˈkwɛstʃən/ · KWES-chun Type: reporting verb — sustained questioning, or expressed doubt · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: question + person · question + sb + about / on + noun phrase · question + noun phrase (question the decision) · question + whether / if-clause · question + wh-clause (question how / question why) · question + that-clause, chiefly under negation (I don't question that she meant well) · passive be questioned about Never: question me the details — no double object; the topic takes about: questioned me about the details ✓. And the two senses do not mix their frames: with a person object it means interrogate (They questioned him for an hour), with a thing object or a whether-clause it means doubt (She questioned the timing, She questioned whether it was legal). question him whether he had been there ✗ → asked him whether… ✓. Which one you get is decided entirely by what follows the verb, so the frame carries the meaning here more than the word does.

quote#

Pronunciation: /ˈrizən/ · REE-zun Type: reporting verb — conclusion presented with its reasoning · Register: neutral to literary; most at home in narrative Patterns: reason + that-clause · reason + direct quotation, as a quotative tag ("He must have missed the exit," she reasoned.) · reason + from + noun phrase (+ to + noun phrase) · reason + about + noun phrase (formal, in logic and philosophy) · reason out + noun phrase or wh-clause · reason + with + person (a different verb: try to persuade) Never: reason someone that and reason to do something — no personal object, no infinitive. And no bare wh-clause: reason why the door stuck ✗ → He reasoned that the door stuck because the frame had swollen ✓ or He reasoned out why the door stuck ✓. Keep reason that apart from reason with: She reasoned that he was lying reports a conclusion; She reasoned with him reports an attempt to talk him around, and takes no clause at all. Reports: a conclusion together with the thinking behind it, usually silent. It is the narrator's verb — he reasoned labels a line of thought the reader is being shown, which is why it appears far more often in fiction than in conversation.

"The lights are on, so somebody must be home," he thought. → He reasoned that somebody had to be home. "If she'd left, the car would be gone." → She reasoned that he hadn't left yet.

  1. He reasoned that a second key had to exist somewhere in the house.
  2. She reasoned that nobody would check the file until Monday.
  3. "They'd have called by now," he reasoned.
  4. The committee reasoned that a smaller pilot carried less risk.
  5. I reasoned that the fastest route was also the one everyone else would take.
  6. You can't reason with someone who has already decided.

recap#

Pronunciation: verb /riˈkæp/ · ree-KAP; noun /ˈrikæp/ · REE-kap — doubles the p: recapped, recapping Type: reporting verb — brief restatement of what was already covered · Register: informal to neutral; standard in meetings, sports, and broadcasting Patterns: recap + noun phrase · recap + wh-clause · recap + noun phrase + for sb · recap + for sb · to recap, as a sentence adverbial · used absolutely (Let me recap) Never: recap that the deadline had moved and recap me the meeting — no that-clause and no double object. Repairs: recapped the decision, recapped the meeting for me, recapped what had been decided. The beneficiary takes for, never to: recapped the meeting to me ✗. Reports: a short replay of ground already covered, for people who were there or who just arrived. Summarize condenses a body of material; recap replays a sequence of events or points in order.

"So: we approved the budget, tabled the hiring, and moved the retreat." → She recapped what the meeting had decided. "Quick reminder of where we left off." → He recapped the previous session for the newcomers.

  1. Let me recap where we landed before everybody scatters.
  2. She recapped the first two episodes for anyone joining late.
  3. He recapped what each department had committed to.
  4. To recap: the deposit is refundable, the fee is not.
  5. The blog recaps every game in about four hundred words.

reckon#

Pronunciation: /ˈrɛkən/ · REK-un Type: reporting verb — casual opinion or rough calculation · Register: informal. In American English it is regional — ordinary in Southern and rural speech, and elsewhere either jocular or a bit of borrowed British Patterns: reckon + that-clause (the that is nearly always dropped) · reckon + noun phrase (reckon the cost — the older "calculate" sense) · reckon + noun phrase + at + amount · reckon + sb/sth + to be (mostly passive: is reckoned to be) · reckon + so / not · reckon + with + noun phrase (a different sense, = deal with: a force to be reckoned with) · reckon + on + noun phrase or -ing (chiefly British) Never: reckon someone that — no personal object, and no wh-clause (reckon where he went ✗). Reckon on is British too — an American says counted on or figured on. Reports: an opinion held loosely and offered without ceremony. Think is the neutral version; reckon adds a regional and conversational color that will be heard, so it does not belong in formal writing.

"I'd say we've got about an hour of daylight," he said. → He reckoned they had about an hour of daylight left. "That's the best barbecue in three counties." → She reckoned it was the best barbecue in three counties.

  7. The county assessor is a force to be reckoned with, whatever you think of her.

reiterate#

Pronunciation: /riˈɪtəreɪt/ · ree-IT-uh-rayt Type: reporting verb — deliberate restatement · Register: formal; institutional and diplomatic Patterns: reiterate + that-clause · reiterate + noun phrase (reiterate his support, reiterate the offer) · reiterate + free relative (reiterated what she had said in June) · reiterate + to sb + that-clause · passive it was reiterated that Never: reiterate me that and reiterate someone the offer — no personal object. No embedded question either: reiterated whether the deadline would move ✗ → reiterated that the deadline would not move ✓. And avoid reiterate again: re- already carries the repetition, so the again is redundant — a mistake common enough in business writing that editors flag it on sight. Reports: saying the same thing a second time on purpose, to close off doubt. Repeat is neutral about why; reiterate implies the first statement was not accepted or not heard, which is why it turns up in press briefings more than anywhere else.

"I'll say it again: no decision has been made," she said. → She reiterated that no decision had been made. "Our position hasn't changed." → The ambassador reiterated his government's position.

  1. He reiterated that the deadline would not move under any circumstances.
  2. The company reiterated its guidance for the fourth quarter.
  3. She reiterated to the staff that nobody's job was at risk.
  4. The court reiterated a principle it had already stated twice that term.
  5. Let me reiterate one point, because it got lost the first time.
  6. I've reiterated this in three separate emails.

repeat#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈpit/ · ri-PEET Type: reporting verb — restatement, one's own or someone else's · Register: neutral Patterns: repeat + that-clause · repeat + noun phrase · repeat + free relative (repeat what he said) · repeat + direct quotation · repeat + noun phrase + to sb · repeat + noun phrase + for sb · repeat oneself · repeat + after sb · passive be repeated Never: repeat me the question — no double object. The hearer takes to or for: repeat the question for me ✓, repeat it to the class ✓. No embedded question either — repeat whether he had met the man ✗ → He repeated that he had never met the man ✓; repeat what he said ✓ is a free relative, not a question. Repeat again is redundant in the same way as reiterate again, though it is far more common in speech and rarely worth correcting there. Reports: saying something over — either restating your own point or passing along someone else's words. Unlike echo, it implies no agreement, and unlike quote, it does not promise the wording is exact.

"As I said, the office is closed on Fridays," she said. → She repeated that the office was closed on Fridays. "Don't tell anyone I told you." → He asked me not to repeat what he had said.

  1. He repeated that he had never met the man.
  2. Could you repeat the last part? The line cut out.
  3. She repeated the instructions slowly, one step at a time.
  4. Don't repeat any of this to your brother.
  5. The witness repeated what she had told police the night before.
  6. The teacher had the class repeat each phrase after her.

show#

Pronunciation: /ʃoʊ/ · SHOH — past participle shown; showed also occurs and is accepted, though shown is standard in careful American writing Type: reporting verb — demonstration or evidence · Register: neutral Patterns: show + that-clause · show + wh-clause · show + whether-clause · show + sb + that-clause · show + sb + wh-clause · show + sb + noun phrase · show + noun phrase + to sb · show + sb + wh-word + to-infinitive (show me what to do, show me how to reset it) · passive it has been shown that Never: show to me how it works as a substitute for the double object — show is one of the verbs that does take a personal object directly, so show me how it works is the natural form. Use to only when the thing shown comes first: showed the photo to me ✓, showed me the photo ✓, showed to me the photo ✗. And keep the -ing frame out of reports: showed him leaving the building is perception, not a report of anything said or established — for a report, showed that he had left the building ✓. Reports: making something evident, either by displaying it or by supplying evidence for it. Learn it as the counterweight to explain and demonstrate: show me ✓, explain to me ✓, explain me ✗ — same meaning territory, opposite object rules. Like prove and demonstrate it is factive: Every study shows the same pattern asserts the pattern in the reporter's own voice.

"Look at the chart — enrollment fell every year," she said. → She showed that enrollment had fallen every year. "Here's how you jump the battery." → He showed me how to jump the battery.

  1. The receipts show that the shipment left on the ninth.
  2. She showed me exactly where the pipe was leaking.
  3. Every study on this shows the same pattern.
  4. He showed the inspector how the alarm had been bypassed.
  5. Let me show you what happens when you click the second tab.
  6. The photos show what the building looked like before the fire.

speculate#

Pronunciation: /ˈspɛkjəleɪt/ · SPEK-yuh-layt Type: reporting verb — guess offered as a guess · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: speculate + that-clause · speculate + about / on + noun phrase · speculate + about / on + wh-clause · speculate + about / on + whether-clause · speculate + as to + wh-clause or noun phrase (formal) · passive it was speculated that Never: speculate someone that and speculate what happened — no personal object, and no bare wh-clause: the question needs a preposition. Repairs: speculated about what had happened ✓, speculated that the roof had failed ✓. No to-infinitive either: speculated the roof to have failed ✗. Keep this sense apart from the financial one, which takes in or on and a market (speculated in currencies). Reports: a possibility floated without evidence, and labeled as such. It is the weakest claim in this group: conclude says the evidence settles it, predict commits the speaker, speculate explicitly declines to.

"Maybe he was pushed out — who knows," he said. → He speculated that the director had been pushed out. "People keep asking whether she'll run again." → Reporters speculated about whether she would run again.

  1. Analysts speculated that the merger had fallen apart over price.
  2. She refused to speculate about an investigation she wasn't part of.
  3. Neighbors speculated for weeks about who had bought the place.
  4. I don't want to speculate on motives without more information.
  5. The article speculates that a second site was under consideration.
  6. It's pointless to speculate about what he would have wanted.
  7. The memo speculates as to which of the three sites the company actually preferred.

spell out#

Pronunciation: /ˌspɛl ˈaʊt/ · spel OWT Type: reporting verb — explanation made explicit · Register: neutral, leaning informal Patterns: spell out + noun phrase · spell out + that-clause · spell out + wh-clause · spell + noun phrase + out (separable) · spell out + noun phrase + for sb · passive be spelled out Never: spell out me the terms and spell out it — no personal object, and a pronoun object must go inside the verb: spell it out ✓, spell out it ✗. The hearer takes for: spell it out for me ✓. A full noun phrase may go either side (spell out the terms / spell the terms out), but a that-clause always follows the whole verb (spelled out that the fee was nonrefundable). Reports: stating in plain, unmissable terms something that was implied or left vague. Explain fills a gap in the hearer's knowledge; spell out removes the hearer's room to pretend not to have understood.

"Let me be blunt: miss this deadline and the contract is void," she said. → She spelled out that missing the deadline would void the contract. "Do I have to say it in so many words?" → He made her spell it out.

suppose#

Pronunciation: /səˈpoʊz/ · suh-POHZ Type: reporting verb — tentative belief · Register: neutral Patterns: suppose + that-clause · suppose + sb/sth + to-infinitive (chiefly passive: is supposed to be) · imperative suppose (that)… introducing a hypothesis · suppose so / suppose not · passive it is supposed that Never: suppose someone that and suppose to do something in the active — no personal object at all, and I suppose to leave early ✗ is a mangling of the passive idiom I'm supposed to leave early ✓. And no wh-clause and no -ing: suppose where he went ✗, suppose him leaving ✗. Negative raising: I don't suppose usually negates the inner clause, not the supposing — I don't suppose you know his number means "I suppose you don't," and in practice it is a soft request: it is the politest way in American English to ask for something you expect to be refused. Reports: a belief held without much conviction and offered with a shrug. Assume takes it as settled and moves on; suppose leaves it open and half-invites disagreement.

"I guess that's fair," he said. → He supposed it was fair. "You probably don't have change for a twenty." → She said she didn't suppose I had change for a twenty.

think#

Pronunciation: /θɪŋk/ · THINGK — the n is the -ng sound, not the n of thin Type: reporting verb — opinion or belief; also unspoken thought · Register: neutral; the default hedge on any statement Patterns: think + that-clause (the that is usually dropped) · think + sb/sth + to be (formal, chiefly passive: is thought to be) · think + noun phrase + adjective (think it odd) · think + of / about + noun phrase · think + about / of + -ing · think + to-infinitive (= remember to: didn't think to ask) · think + wh-clause, exclamative or deliberative only (Think what that would mean, I'm still thinking what to say) · think so / think not · passive it is thought that Never: think someone that and think to me that — no personal object and no hearer slot. Also wonder me and wonder someone whether — no personal object, since nothing was said to anybody. And keep statement order inside the clause: I wondered where was he ✗ → I wondered where he was ✓. Do not use the that-clause to report a question: He wondered that she was home does not mean he asked himself whether she was home — it means, in a literary register, that he was surprised she was home. For the question, He wondered whether she was home ✓. Reports: a question the speaker asked themselves and did not say out loud. I was wondering if you had a minute is a question about now, ends in a period, and expects an answer.
