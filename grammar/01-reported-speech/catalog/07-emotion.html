Reporting with attitude: complaining, boasting, apologizing#

The reporting verbs that carry a feeling — the ones that tell you not just what was said but how the speaker felt about saying it, and which almost all demand a preposition before the person or the offense.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

Every verb in this file reports a speech act with an emotion welded on. Say is neutral; complain, boast, apologize, accuse, sneer at are not — choosing one commits the reporter to a judgment about the speaker, which is exactly why they are so useful in a summary and so dangerous in a neutral report. Two grammatical facts run through the whole group. First, most of these verbs reach the person and the offense through a fixed preposition (accuse sb of, blame sb for, congratulate sb on, apologize to sb for, thank sb for, complain to sb about), and the preposition is not negotiable — it is memorized with the verb. Second, the manner-of-speaking verbs at the end (whisper, mutter, snap, sigh*) split into those that can carry a full that-clause and those that really only introduce a direct quotation, and getting that split wrong produces sentences that are grammatical on paper and wrong in the ear.

A third pattern is worth naming up front: with almost none of these verbs can the person be a bare object of the verb plus a clause. He complained me that…, She apologized me, They boasted us that… are all impossible. The person is reached through to (complained to me, apologized to me), or the verb takes the person directly and the content through a preposition (accused me of it, thanked me for it) — never both as bare objects.

Four traps live in that table. Object to is a preposition, so the verb after it is -ing, never an infinitive: objected to being searched, never objected to be searched. And lament is on the list precisely because it takes no preposition: lamented about the closing is the error the table is there to prevent.

The cry, sigh, and laugh rows are the honest part. "I forgot," she sighed and She sighed that she had forgotten are both printed constantly, and both are also flagged by copy editors on the grounds that you cannot sigh a sentence. In a novel, use them. In a report, write she said with a sigh.

Note what the at column is doing, because it is not one thing. For shout, yell, snap, and growl, at marks the target of anger. For laugh and sneer it marks the target of contempt. For exclaim and groan it marks the thing reacted to — groaned at the price, not at a person. For moan (British) it marks the person nagged at, and for whine it is mostly literal noise directed at a door or a parent. Only the first two kinds report a speech act aimed at a hearer, which is why shouted at me and groaned at the price cannot be reported the same way.

What these verbs presuppose#

A reporting verb can smuggle in a claim the speaker never made. The grammar of the sentence is about the speech act; the presupposition is about the reporter's own position, and it survives negation — He did not gloat that he had won still concedes that he won. Choose from this column deliberately, because a reader will hold you to it.

VerbWhat the reporter commits to

blamethat the outcome was bad and that someone is answerable for it
boast, brag, crowthat the claim was made with self-satisfaction; brag and crow add disapproval
gloatthat someone lost — there is no gloating without a defeat to enjoy

The same trap runs through the neighboring files, and it is worth carrying the list across: claim and allege signal the writer's doubt about what was said; admit and confess presuppose fault the way apologize does; and point out presupposes that the thing pointed out is true, so she pointed out that the numbers were wrong endorses her. When a report must stay neutral, the safe verbs are say, tell, and state — every verb in this file editorializes by design.

The dictionary#

accuse#

Pronunciation: /əˈkjuz/ · uh-KYOOZ Type: reporting verb — accusation · Register: neutral Patterns: accuse + sb · accuse + sb + of + -ing · accuse + sb + of + noun phrase · accuse + sb + of + having done sth · passive be accused of Never: accuse someone that they did something, accuse someone for doing something, and accuse someone to have done something. Accuse takes no that-clause at all, no infinitive, and never for — the preposition is of, and what follows it is a gerund or a noun. Repairs: accused him of stealing it, accused him of theft. To get a clause in, change the verb: alleged that he stole it, said he had stolen it. Perfect gerund: for an offense clearly finished before the accusation, of having done is available and slightly more formal — accused her of having falsified the records. Plain -ing covers the same ground and is commoner. Reports: a charge of wrongdoing laid at a named person, and it presupposes that the accuser believes the offense real. Compare blame, which assigns responsibility for an outcome without necessarily alleging misconduct, and criticize, which faults a choice rather than charging an offense.

"You took the money," she said to him. → She accused him of taking the money. "You've been lying to me for months." → She accused him of lying to her for months.

  1. She accused her landlord of ignoring three written repair requests.
  2. He accused me of leaking the memo, which I hadn't.
  3. Two former employees have accused the company of retaliation.
  4. Nobody was accusing anyone of anything — they just wanted the numbers explained.
  5. "Don't accuse me of something you can't prove," he said.
  6. The report stops short of accusing the department of misconduct.

See also: blame, criticize, condemn, reproach

apologise#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑləˌdʒaɪz/ · uh-PAH-luh-jyze Type: reporting verb — apology · Register: neutral — British spelling Patterns: identical to apologize — apologise (intransitive) · apologise + to sb · apologise + for + -ing · apologise + for + noun phrase · apologise + to sb + for + -ing / noun phrase British spelling: apologise is the standard form in British, Irish, and Australian writing; apologize is required in American English and is also perfectly standard in Britain, where Oxford house style prefers it. The two are pronounced exactly alike — the s is /z/ either way, so nothing in the sound tells you which spelling a speaker has in mind. The same -ise/-ize split runs through criticise/criticize, recognise/recognize, and organise/organize. Never: apologise me or apologise him for the delay — the grammar is apologize's in every detail, so the person is reached only through to. And in an American text, never apologise at all: this library is American throughout, so every other example in this file uses apologize. If you are writing for a British publication, flip all of them. Reports: the same speech act as apologize — nothing about the meaning or the grammar changes with the spelling.

"Sorry I'm late," he said. → He apologised for being late. (British) "Sorry I'm late," he said. → He apologized for being late. (American)

  1. The minister apologised to the families for the delay in the inquiry. (British press)
  2. Our style guide uses apologise, so the American draft had to be respelled throughout.
  3. She apologised for the confusion and reissued the invoice. (British)
  4. Both apologise and apologize appear in the OED; only one appears in Merriam-Webster's main entry.
  5. He never learned which spelling his company used, so he wrote I'm sorry instead.
  6. The autocorrect kept changing apologise to apologize, which told him what dictionary it was using.

See also: apologize, criticise, criticize

apologize#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑləˌdʒaɪz/ · uh-PAH-luh-jyze Type: reporting verb — apology · Register: neutral Patterns: apologize (intransitive) · apologize + to sb · apologize + for + -ing · apologize + for + noun phrase · apologize + to sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · apologize + that-clause (institutional only, see below) Never: apologize me, apologize him for the delay, and apologize about the delay. The person apologized to is reached only through to, and to comes before for: apologized to the customer for the delay, never apologized the customer for the delay. The order is fixed — apologized for the delay to the customer is grammatical but sounds like an afterthought. The offense takes for and only for; about belongs to complain, not here. The that-clause: We apologize that your order was delayed is now everywhere in corporate and customer-service English. It is not an error, but it is a register marker: no one says it in conversation. In careful writing use apologize for + noun (apologize for the delay) or drop the verb entirely (we're sorry your order was delayed). Reports: an expression of regret for one's own act, which presupposes fault the way admit and confess do. It is not the same as expressing sympathy — you apologize for what you did, and you say I'm sorry for what merely happened.

"I'm sorry I snapped at you," he said. → He apologized for snapping at me. "We're sorry, the flight is overbooked." → They apologized to us for overbooking the flight.

  1. He apologized to the whole team for missing the deadline.
  2. She apologized for the confusion and sent a corrected schedule.
  3. I'm not going to apologize for wanting the contract in writing.
  4. The airline apologized profusely and gave everyone a voucher.
  5. "You could at least apologize," she said, and he did.
  6. He apologized to her, not to me, which was the whole problem.

See also: apologise, thank, blame, reproach

blame#

Pronunciation: /bleɪm/ · BLAYM Type: reporting verb — accusation · Register: neutral Patterns: blame + sb · blame + sb + for + -ing · blame + sb + for + noun phrase · blame + oneself + for · blame + sth + on + sb · blame + noun phrase (blame the weather) · be to blame (for) Never: blame someone of something — that is accuse's preposition, not blame's — and blame someone about something, which is nobody's. And no that-clause: He blamed that the driver was speeding is impossible. Repairs: blamed the driver for speeding, blamed the crash on the driver, said the driver was speeding. Two directions, two prepositions: the person takes for, the outcome takes on. She blamed me for the leak and She blamed the leak on me say the same thing with the arguments reversed. Mixing them — blamed me on the leak — reverses the meaning into nonsense. Reports: the assignment of responsibility for something bad. Unlike accuse, it need allege no misconduct — you can blame the weather, the software, or bad luck — and unlike criticize, it targets an outcome rather than a judgment call.

"It's your fault the server went down," she said. → She blamed me for the outage. / She blamed the outage on me. "The vendor missed the deadline, not us." → He blamed the delay on the vendor.

  1. Don't blame the intern — nobody told her the file was live.
  2. He blamed the loss on a single bad call in the third quarter.
  3. She blamed herself for not catching the typo before it printed.
  4. I don't blame you for walking out of that meeting.
  5. The report blames poor maintenance for the failure.
  6. Everyone knew who was to blame, and nobody said it out loud.

See also: accuse, criticize, condemn, reproach

boast#

Pronunciation: /boʊst/ · BOHST Type: reporting verb — self-praise · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: boast + that-clause · boast + about + -ing / noun · boast + of + -ing / noun · boast + to sb + that / about · boast + noun phrase (only in the "possess proudly" sense) · + direct quotation Never: boast someone that and boast to do something. The listener needs to: boasted to his brother that he'd won. There is no infinitive frame — He boasted to have won is not English. The inanimate use: boast alone among this group takes a plain noun-phrase object when the subject is a thing, and there it means "possess as a point of pride," with no speech at all: The hotel boasts three restaurants and a rooftop pool. This is a real and common sense; it just is not a reporting verb. Reports: a claim advanced with self-satisfaction. Boast is the least loaded of the four self-praise verbs — it can be neutral or even admiring (boasts a spotless record) — where brag is informal and disapproving, crow is loud, and gloat requires someone else's loss.

"I've never lost a case," he said. → He boasted that he had never lost a case. "My kid got into Stanford." → She boasted about her son getting into Stanford.

  1. He boasted that he could fix the transmission in an afternoon.
  2. She never boasts about the awards; you have to find out from someone else.
  3. They boasted of a turnaround time no competitor could match.
  4. The résumé boasted fifteen years of experience she did not have.
  5. "I boast," he admitted, "but only about things I've actually done."
  6. The new terminal boasts the shortest security lines in the region.

See also: brag, crow, gloat, praise

brag#

Pronunciation: /bræɡ/ · BRAG Type: reporting verb — self-praise · Register: informal Patterns: brag + that-clause · brag + about + -ing / noun · brag + of + -ing / noun (much rarer than with boast) · brag + to sb + about / that · + direct quotation Never: brag something — no plain noun-phrase object, so no He bragged his salary. And no inanimate subject in the "possess" sense: a hotel boasts three pools, it does not brag them. Repairs: bragged about his salary, bragged that he made six figures. Spelling: doubles the g — bragged, bragging. Reports: the same act as boast but with the disapproval built in. Calling it bragging is itself a judgment; no one describes their own speech that way except jokingly (not to brag, but…), which is the standard American hedge for bragging anyway.

"I closed four deals this week," he said. → He bragged that he had closed four deals that week. "Nobody here can beat my time." → He bragged about his time.

  1. He bragged that he'd never once been late to work.
  2. Stop bragging about the truck — everyone has seen it.
  3. She bragged to her sister about getting the corner office.
  4. Not to brag, but I fixed the sink myself.
  5. The kids bragged all afternoon about who caught the bigger fish.
  6. He brags less now that people know what the numbers actually were.

See also: boast, crow, gloat

chide#

Pronunciation: /tʃaɪd/ · CHYDE Type: reporting verb — reproof · Register: formal, literary Patterns: chide + sb · chide + sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · + direct quotation ("You promised," she chided) · passive be chided for Never: chide someone that they did something, chide someone to do something, and chide at someone. No that-clause, no infinitive, no preposition before the person. Repairs: chided him for forgetting, told him he had forgotten. Past tense: chided in modern American English. The old forms chid and chidden survive only in older texts and are not usable now. Reports: a mild, often affectionate rebuke — the gentlest verb in the scold–rebuke–reproach family, and the only one that can be teasing. It reads as bookish; in speech an American would say gave him a hard time or ribbed him.

"You really should have called," she said. → She chided him for not calling. "Manners, please." → She chided the children gently.

  1. She chided him for showing up empty-handed, but she was smiling.
  2. The columnist chided the mayor for reading a statement instead of answering.
  3. "You're impossible," she chided, and handed him the umbrella anyway.
  4. He was chided more than punished, which suited him fine.
  5. Her grandmother chided her for speaking English at the dinner table.
  6. The editorial chided both parties for walking away from the table.

See also: scold, rebuke, reproach, tease

complain#

Pronunciation: /kəmˈpleɪn/ · kuhm-PLAYN Type: reporting verb — complaint · Register: neutral Patterns: complain + that-clause · complain + about + noun phrase / -ing · complain + of + noun phrase / -ing · complain + to sb + about / of / that · complain (intransitive) · + direct quotation Never: complain me or complain the manager about the noise. There is no personal object — the hearer is reached through to, and to comes first: complained to the manager about the noise. Also complain something: the content never appears as a bare noun phrase, so no She complained the noise. And never both markers at once — complained about that the food was cold; it is either complained about the cold food or complained that the food was cold. of vs. about: complain of is for symptoms, ailments, and formal reports of a condition — complained of chest pains, complained of dizziness. Complain about is the everyday grievance — complained about the noise, the food, her boss. Using of for a grievance sounds archaic; using about for a symptom sounds casual but is not wrong. Reports: a statement of dissatisfaction, usually addressed to someone who might fix it — which is what separates it from grumble and gripe, where nobody expects redress.

"The heat hasn't worked in three days," she said. → She complained that the heat hadn't worked in three days. "This coffee is cold," he said to the server. → He complained to the server about the cold coffee.

  1. Three tenants complained that the elevator had been out since Friday.
  2. He complained to HR about the scheduling, and nothing changed.
  3. She came in complaining of a headache and a stiff neck.
  4. Passengers complained about being kept on the tarmac for two hours.
  5. "I'm not complaining," he said, complaining.
  6. Nobody complained, which the manager took as approval.

See also: gripe, grumble, moan, whine, object, protest

compliment#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɑmpləˌmɛnt/ · KAHM-pluh-ment Type: reporting verb — praise · Register: neutral Patterns: compliment + sb · compliment + sb + on + noun phrase / -ing · passive be complimented on Never: compliment someone that they did well — no that-clause. And the preposition is on, not for: complimented her for her presentation is heard, but on is the standard frame and for belongs to praise and thank. Repair: complimented her on her presentation. Homophone: compliment (praise) and complement (complete, go well with) are pronounced identically and mean nothing alike. The wine complements the fish; the chef complimented the sommelier. This is one of the most common spelling errors in American English. Reports: a short, direct expression of admiration said to the person's face. Praise can be public and about a third party; a compliment is addressed to its target.

"That was a great presentation," he said to her. → He complimented her on her presentation. "I love your jacket." → She complimented me on my jacket.

  1. He complimented her on the way she handled the hostile question.
  2. Nobody had ever complimented his handwriting before.
  3. She complimented the cook and asked for the recipe.
  4. I was complimented on my Spanish by someone who had heard four words of it.
  5. "Was that a compliment?" "It was supposed to be."
  6. He complimented the team publicly and criticized them in private.

See also: praise, congratulate, thank, boast

condemn#

Pronunciation: /kənˈdɛm/ · kuhn-DEM — the final n is silent Type: reporting verb — denunciation · Register: formal, journalistic Patterns: condemn + noun phrase · condemn + sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · condemn + sb / sth + as + noun phrase / adjective · passive be condemned (as / for) · condemn + sb + to + noun phrase (= sentence — a separate sense) Never: condemn that the policy was unfair — no that-clause — and condemn someone of something, which is accuse's preposition. Repairs: condemned the policy as unfair, condemned the policy, said the policy was unfair. Also note that condemn sb to + noun means "sentence" (condemned to death), a different verb sense entirely, so condemned him to lie does not mean he was denounced for lying — and there is no infinitive frame in the denouncing sense at all. Spelling: condemned, condemning — the silent n stays in every form. Reports: the strongest public denunciation in this group. Where criticize faults a decision and accuse charges an offense, condemn declares the thing morally unacceptable, which is why it is the standard verb of diplomatic statements.

"This attack is unacceptable and we reject it," the statement said. → The government condemned the attack. "What the company did was indefensible." → She condemned the company's conduct as indefensible.

  1. Both senators condemned the ruling within the hour.
  2. The union condemned the layoffs as a breach of the agreement.
  3. She condemned the practice for putting drivers at risk.
  4. The report stops at describing the failures; it does not condemn anyone.
  5. "I condemn it in the strongest possible terms," he said, which meant nothing would happen.
  6. The building was condemned, in the other sense, six months later.

See also: criticize, accuse, blame, rebuke

congratulate#

Pronunciation: /kənˈɡrætʃəˌleɪt/ · kuhn-GRACH-uh-layt Type: reporting verb — congratulation · Register: neutral Patterns: congratulate + sb · congratulate + sb + on + noun phrase / -ing · congratulate + oneself + on · passive be congratulated on Never: congratulate your promotion — you congratulate a person, never the event. The event goes after on: congratulated her on her promotion. And no that-clause: congratulated me that I had passed is impossible; write congratulated me on passing. on vs. for: on is the standard preposition and the only safe one. Congratulate sb for + -ing occurs in American speech and is not exactly wrong, but it shades toward praise for an effort rather than acknowledgment of an outcome, and editors change it to on. The noun: the exclamation is Congratulations! — always plural, always with the s. Congratulation! as a greeting is a fossilized learner error. Reports: an acknowledgment of someone's good fortune or achievement. It does not evaluate the person's work, which is what separates it from praise and compliment — you can congratulate someone on a lottery win.

"Congratulations on the new job!" she said. → She congratulated me on the new job. "You did it — you passed the bar." → He congratulated her on passing the bar.

  1. She congratulated the whole team on shipping two weeks early.
  2. He congratulated his opponent and left the stage without another word.
  3. I congratulated her on the baby before I realized she hadn't announced it.
  4. They congratulated themselves on a decision that had mostly been luck.
  5. "Am I supposed to congratulate you?" she asked, not warmly.
  6. The email congratulated everyone on numbers that three people had actually produced.

See also: compliment, praise, thank

criticise#

Pronunciation: /ˈkrɪtəˌsaɪz/ · KRIT-uh-syze Type: reporting verb — criticism · Register: neutral — British spelling Patterns: identical to criticize — criticise + sb / sth · criticise + sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · criticise + sb / sth + as + adjective / noun phrase · passive be criticised for British spelling: criticise is standard in British, Irish, and Australian writing; American English requires criticize, and so does Oxford house style in Britain, which prefers -ize across the board. The pronunciation is identical — the letter s here is /z/, exactly as in apologise. The related words criticism, critical, and critic have no variant spelling at all; only the verb splits, because only the verb carries the -ize / -ise suffix, which comes from Greek -izein by way of French. Never: criticise me that I was late — the grammar is criticize's in every detail, so no that-clause and no about. And in an American text, never criticise at all: this library uses criticize throughout, as American English requires. Reports: exactly what criticize reports. "The rollout was badly planned," she said. (British) → She criticised the rollout as badly planned. "They shouldn't have signed without reading it." (British) → She criticised them for signing without reading it.

  2. (British usage) He was criticised in the press for a remark he never made.
  3. (British usage) The paper criticised the ruling as an extraordinary overreach.
  4. In American English all three of those sentences take criticized, with no other change.
  5. Our house style is British, so every criticize in the draft was respelled criticise.
  6. Both spellings appear in the OED; only criticize heads the Merriam-Webster entry.

criticize#

Pronunciation: /ˈkrɪtəˌsaɪz/ · KRIT-uh-syze Type: reporting verb — criticism · Register: neutral Patterns: criticize + sb / sth · criticize + sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · criticize + sb / sth + as + adjective / noun phrase · passive be criticized for / be criticized as Never: criticize about the delay and criticize that the plan was rushed. The verb is plainly transitive and takes no that-clause, and no personal object plus clause either: criticized me that I was late is impossible. Repairs: criticized the delay, criticized the plan as rushed, criticized them for rushing the plan. British spelling: criticise, which has its own entry above. This library uses criticize throughout, as American English requires. Reports: an unfavorable judgment of a choice, a performance, or a piece of work. Weaker than condemn, broader than accuse — you can criticize something without alleging any wrongdoing at all, and academic criticism is not even negative.

"The rollout was badly planned and you knew it," she said. → She criticized the rollout as badly planned. "They shouldn't have signed without reading it." → He criticized them for signing without reading it.

  1. Two board members criticized the timing but voted for it anyway.
  2. She was criticized for saying out loud what everyone had written down.
  3. The design was criticized as expensive and hard to maintain.
  4. He doesn't take criticism well, which is why nobody criticizes him.
  5. It's easy to criticize a schedule you didn't have to build.
  6. The review criticized the pacing and praised almost everything else.

crow#

Pronunciation: /kroʊ/ · KROH Type: reporting verb — self-praise · Register: informal, disapproving Patterns: crow + that-clause · crow + about + noun phrase / -ing · crow + over + noun phrase / -ing · crow + to sb + that / about · + direct quotation Never: crow something, crow someone that, and crow of the win. No noun-phrase object, no personal object, and not boast's of — crow takes about or over. Repairs: crowed about the win, crowed that they'd won. Reports: boasting loudly and publicly, with the metaphor of a rooster fully alive in it — the verb implies noise and an audience. Crow over something specifically means gloating about a victory in front of the loser, which is gloat's territory.

"We beat them by twenty points!" he said, over and over. → He crowed that they had beaten them by twenty points. "Told you the stock would double." → She crowed about having called the stock right.

  1. He crowed about the promotion until even his friends stopped answering.
  2. The press release crowed that the company had doubled its market share.
  3. She won the bet and did not crow, which he noticed.
  4. They were still crowing over the takeover three years later.
  5. "Don't crow until the check clears," his partner said.
  6. The mayor crowed about a crime drop that had started under his predecessor.

scold#

Pronunciation: /skoʊld/ · SKOHLD Type: reporting verb — reproof · Register: neutral to informal Patterns: scold + sb · scold + sb + for + -ing / noun phrase · + direct quotation ("Not on the couch," she scolded) · passive be scolded for Never: scold someone that they did it, scold someone to do something, and scold at someone. Repairs: scolded him for doing it, told him not to do it. Reports: an angry telling-off, prototypically an adult to a child. Using it of two adults is a deliberate move — she scolded her colleague makes the colleague sound infantilized, which is sometimes exactly the point and is a well-known trap in reporting on women in public life.

"How many times have I told you not to slam that door?" → She scolded him for slamming the door. "You know better than to leave that out." → He scolded the kids for leaving the milk out.

  1. She scolded the dog, who was unmoved.
  2. He was scolded by a stranger for parking across two spaces.
  3. "Inside voices," she scolded, without looking up.
  4. The op-ed scolds readers for a problem it spends no words explaining.
  5. My grandmother scolded me in two languages, for emphasis.
  6. Nobody likes being scolded by a machine, which is why the warning gets ignored.

See also: chide, rebuke, reproach, snap, yell

shout#

Pronunciation: /ʃaʊt/ · SHOWT Type: reporting verb — manner of speaking · Register: neutral Patterns: shout + direct quotation · shout + that-clause · shout + wh-clause · shout + noun phrase (shouted an order) · shout + at sb · shout + to sb · shout + at/to sb + to-infinitive · shout (intransitive) Never: shout someone that and shout someone something. The hearer always takes a preposition: shouted to me that the gate was closing, not shouted me that. at vs. to — this is the whole verb:

FrameMeansExample
shout at sbanger, aggressionHe shouted at the referee.
shout to sbdistance, being heardHe shouted to her from the dock.

Don't shout at me is about tone; I shouted to him across the lot is about acoustics. Learners who use at for both come across as describing a fight. Commands: shout at/to sb + to-infinitive is the standard way to report a shouted order — shouted at us to get back, and see Reported commands, requests, and advice. Reports: loud speech. Unlike yell it is not necessarily angry, and unlike cry it carries no literary flavor.

"The bridge is out!" he said at the top of his voice. → He shouted that the bridge was out. "Get back from the edge!" → She shouted at us to get back from the edge.

  1. Someone shouted that the elevator was stuck between floors.
  2. He shouted to her across the parking lot and she didn't hear him.
  3. Please stop shouting at the umpire; he has a hard enough job.
  4. She shouted over the music to ask where the bathroom was.
  5. He shouted a warning about half a second before the shelf came down.
  6. They shouted until they were hoarse and the vote went the other way anyway.

See also: yell, cry, exclaim, snap, whisper

sigh#

Pronunciation: /saɪ/ · SY — the gh is silent Type: reporting verb — manner of speaking · Register: neutral Patterns: sigh (intransitive) · sigh + direct quotation · sigh + that-clause (fiction) · sigh + over / about + noun phrase / -ing · sigh + for + noun phrase (literary, = long for) · noun a sigh, with a sigh Never: sigh someone and sigh someone that. There is no object frame at all beyond the cognate object (sighed a long sigh), which reports no speech, and no personal object: if the hearer matters, write said to me with a sigh. The honest note: you cannot physically produce a sentence while sighing, and copy editors have been striking "Fine," she sighed out of manuscripts for a century. It has not worked. The construction is standard in published fiction and perfectly clear, and she sighed that she'd try again tomorrow appears in reputable journalism. In formal nonfiction, use said with a sigh; everywhere else, use it and stop worrying. Reports: resignation, weariness, or relief expressed as breath. As a reporting verb it tells you the speaker had given up, not what act was performed.

"All right, I'll do it myself," she said wearily. → She sighed that she would do it herself. / She said with a sigh that she would do it herself. "Well. That's that." → He sighed and let it go.

  1. She sighed, looked at the clock, and opened the file again.
  2. "There's nothing else to try," he sighed.
  3. He sighed over the invoice for a while and then paid it.
  4. There was a long sigh on the other end of the line.
  5. The whole room sighed with relief when the numbers came up green.
  6. She sighed that she'd been through three of these reorganizations already.

See also: groan, moan, laugh, grumble

snap#

Pronunciation: /snæp/ · SNAP Type: reporting verb — manner of speaking, irritation · Register: neutral to informal Patterns: snap + direct quotation · snap + that-clause · snap + at sb · snap + at sb + to-infinitive (snapped at him to sit down) · snap (intransitive, = lose one's temper) Never: snap someone, snap someone that, and snap to someone in this sense. The target takes at and nothing else: snapped at me. (Snap to it exists, but that is a different idiom meaning "hurry up.") Spelling: doubles the p — snapped, snapping. Reports: a short, sharp, irritated reply — brevity is the whole point. A snapped remark is one sentence, delivered in a tone that ends the exchange. Compare mutter, which is resentful but quiet, and yell, which is loud but not necessarily short.

"I said I'd handle it," she said sharply. → She snapped that she had said she'd handle it. "Do you mind?" → He snapped at the man behind him in line.

  1. "I'm not asking again," he snapped.
  2. She snapped that she'd sent the file twice already.
  3. Sorry I snapped at you — it's been a long week.
  4. He snapped at a waiter and the whole table went quiet.
  5. She almost never snaps, which is why it registered.
  6. He snapped after the third schedule change and walked out.

See also: yell, growl, mutter, scold, shout

sneer#

Pronunciation: /snɪr/ · SNEER Type: reporting verb — contempt, manner of speaking · Register: literary, journalistic — disapproving Patterns: sneer (intransitive) · sneer + at + sb / sth · sneer + that-clause · + direct quotation ("Nice try," he sneered) · noun a sneer, adjective sneering Never: sneer someone, sneer someone that, sneer to someone, and sneer about something. The verb reaches its target only through at, and that target is the thing despised, not the hearer: sneered at the suggestion, sneered at me. Unlike whisper and mutter, it has no to frame at all, so the person spoken to cannot be named inside the verb — write said to me with a sneer. Reports: contempt carried in the voice or the face — a remark delivered as though the thing said were beneath the speaker. Like whine, it is the reporter's verdict rather than a neutral description: he sneered that the plan wouldn't work instructs the reader to dislike him, and a fair report writes said dismissively instead. Compare mock, which works by imitation, and taunt, which works by provocation; a sneer needs no audience beyond the person it dismisses.

"That's the best you could do?" he said, with his lip curled. → He sneered at my draft. "Nobody serious believes that." → He sneered that nobody serious believed it.

  1. He sneered at the suggestion that anyone had asked his opinion.
  2. She sneered that the report had been written by someone who had never seen the site.
  3. "Amateurs," he sneered, and went back to his phone.
  4. The column sneers at the readers it is trying to persuade, which is why it persuades nobody.
  5. There was a sneer in his voice that the transcript does not record.
  6. Don't sneer — half of that code is still running in production.

See also: mock, taunt, gloat, laugh, snap

taunt#

Pronunciation: /tɔnt/ · TAWNT Type: reporting verb — ridicule · Register: neutral Patterns: taunt + sb · taunt + sb + about + noun phrase / -ing · taunt + sb + with + noun phrase · taunt + sb + for + -ing (less common than about) · + direct quotation ("Can't catch me," he taunted) · passive be taunted about / with · noun a taunt Never: taunt at someone and taunt someone that. The verb is transitive. Repair: taunted him about the score. Reports: repeated, provoking mockery meant to get a reaction — the playground and the sideline verb. Mock makes someone look foolish; a taunt is designed to make them respond, which is why it is the word used for what starts fights.

"Zero for four! Zero for four!" the fans said, over and over. → The fans taunted him about going zero for four. "You'll never finish it and we both know it." → She taunted him with his own unfinished projects.

  1. He taunted the goalie for the entire third period.
  2. She taunted him about the bet until he doubled it.
  3. The taunts from the away section got the game stopped for four minutes.
  4. Don't taunt a system administrator two days before your renewal.
  5. They taunted each other cheerfully for an hour and left as friends.
  6. He was taunted with a nickname he'd been given in the seventh grade.

See also: mock, tease, sneer, gloat, crow

tease#

Pronunciation: /tiz/ · TEEZ Type: reporting verb — light ridicule · Register: neutral to informal Patterns: tease + sb · tease + sb + about + noun phrase / -ing · tease + sb + for + -ing · tease + sb + into / out of + -ing · + direct quotation · passive be teased about Never: tease at someone and tease someone that. Repair: teased him about his tie. about vs. for: about is the default and the friendlier one; for leans toward blame — teased him about his accent is affectionate, teased him for losing again has an edge. Reports: joking at someone's expense, usually affectionately and usually between people who like each other. That affection is the whole difference from mock and taunt; I was only teasing is the standard American repair when the affection did not come through.

"Nice haircut. Did you lose a bet?" → She teased him about his haircut. "You've told that story four times tonight." → They teased him for repeating himself.

  1. His sisters teased him about the truck for twenty years.
  2. She teased me for alphabetizing the spice rack.
  3. "I'm teasing," he said, a beat too late.
  4. He was teased mercilessly and gave as good as he got.
  5. She teased him into trying the karaoke machine.
  6. There's a line between teasing and something else, and it moves depending on who's laughing.

See also: joke, mock, taunt, laugh, chide

thank#

Pronunciation: /θæŋk/ · THANK — voiceless th, as in thin Type: reporting verb — thanks · Register: neutral Patterns: thank + sb · thank + sb + for + noun phrase · thank + sb + for + -ing · thank + sb + for + having done sth · passive be thanked for · fixed idiom I'll thank you to + verb (= a sharp request, not a thanks) Never: thank to someone, thank someone that they came, and thank someone of something. The verb takes the person directly and the reason through for: thanked me for coming, not thanked to me and not thanked me that I came. (Thanks to exists as a prepositional phrase meaning "because of" — thanks to the rain, we canceled — and is a different construction entirely.) Two look-alikes that are not counterexamples: Thank God that nobody was hurt is a fixed exclamation, not the reporting verb, and the that-clause belongs to that idiom alone and licenses nothing else, so still never thanked her that she came. And I'll thank you to mind your own business is an infinitive after a frozen idiom meaning "kindly do it," not a thank + to-infinitive frame; you cannot say thanked him to help. Perfect for a past favor: thanked her for having covered his shift is available but unnecessary; plain -ing already covers finished action — thanked her for covering his shift. Reports: an expression of gratitude. This is the verb that turns "Thank you so much for coming" into a single reported clause, and the for + -ing frame is the most reliably useful pattern in this whole file.

"Thank you for covering my shift," he said. → He thanked me for covering his shift. "Thanks — I really appreciate it." → She thanked me.

  1. She thanked every member of the crew by name.
  2. He thanked me for the ride and I hadn't even offered yet.
  3. I want to thank whoever refilled the coffee at 6 a.m.
  4. They were thanked in the acknowledgments and paid in nothing else.
  5. Don't thank me — thank the person who caught it in review.
  6. She thanked him for his honesty in a tone that suggested otherwise.

See also: apologize, congratulate, compliment, praise

whine#

Pronunciation: /waɪn/ · WYNE — some American speakers keep an /hw/ onset, HWYNE Type: reporting verb — complaint · Register: informal, disapproving Patterns: whine + that-clause · whine + about + noun phrase / -ing · whine + to sb + about / that · whine + at + sb / sth (literal noise: whined at the door) · whine + direct quotation · whine (intransitive) Never: whine someone and whine something. No object without a preposition, and no personal object: whined me about the seat should be whined to me about the seat. Reports: complaining in a high, drawn-out, childish tone — and, crucially, the reporter's verdict that the complaint is unwarranted. There is no neutral use. He complained that the shift was unsafe takes the complaint seriously; He whined that the shift was unsafe dismisses it, which makes this a verb to choose deliberately and never by accident in a report you want read as fair. Homophone: identical to wine for most Americans.

"Why do I always have to be the one to close?" → He whined about always having to close. "It's too far to walk." → She whined that it was too far to walk.

  1. He whined about the seating chart until they moved him.
  2. She whined that nobody had told her, though the email was in her inbox.
  3. Stop whining and get in the car.
  4. The dog whined at the door for ten minutes.
  5. Calling it whining was how the complaint got buried.
  6. There's a difference between raising an issue and whining, and it is mostly tone.

See also: complain, gripe, grumble, moan

whisper#

Pronunciation: /ˈwɪspər/ · WIS-per — the h is silent Type: reporting verb — manner of speaking · Register: neutral Patterns: whisper + direct quotation · whisper + that-clause · whisper + wh-clause · whisper + noun phrase (whispered a name) · whisper + to sb + that · whisper + to sb + to-infinitive (whispered to me to wait) · whisper (intransitive) · passive it is whispered that (= rumor) Never: whisper someone that and whisper someone something. The hearer takes to: whispered to me that the boss was behind us, not whispered me that. The rumor passive: It was whispered that the deal had already been signed is a live and useful construction — the passive with no agent is exactly how you report something everyone was saying and nobody would say on the record. See Special cases for the impersonal passive report generally. Reports: speech with no voicing behind it, produced quietly on purpose — for secrecy, for intimacy, or because the room requires it. It is the manner verb that most freely takes a that-clause; nobody objects to she whispered that she'd be right back.

"Don't look now, but he's right behind you," she said very quietly. → She whispered that he was right behind me. "Meet me outside in five." → He whispered to me to meet him outside in five.

  1. She whispered something to the bailiff and the judge called a recess.
  2. He whispered that the second door was unlocked.
  3. "Not here," she whispered.
  4. It was whispered around the building that the merger had been signed weeks earlier.
  5. The kids whispered through the entire ceremony.
  6. He whispered a name I didn't catch and everyone else clearly did.

See also: murmur, mutter, mumble, shout

yell#

Pronunciation: /jɛl/ · YEL Type: reporting verb — manner of speaking · Register: informal Patterns: yell + direct quotation · yell + that-clause · yell + wh-clause · yell + noun phrase (yelled a warning) · yell + at sb · yell + to sb · yell + at / to sb + to-infinitive · yell (intransitive) Never: yell someone that and yell someone something. As with shout, the hearer needs a preposition: yelled at me to slow down. at vs. to: the same split as shout — yell at someone is anger, yell to someone is distance. He yelled at me means I was in trouble; he yelled to me means he was far away. Note that get yelled at is the ordinary American way to say you were reprimanded, and it says nothing about actual volume: I got yelled at for the expense report may describe a quiet conversation. Reports: loud, usually angry speech. More informal and more emotional than shout — a coach shouts instructions and yells at a referee.

"Slow down! You're going to miss the turn!" → She yelled at me to slow down. "The oven's been on for three hours!" → He yelled that the oven had been on for three hours.

  1. Somebody yelled that the last train was already on the platform.
  2. She yelled to him from the top of the stairs and he never heard her.
  3. Don't yell at the kid; he's four.
  4. I got yelled at for something I hadn't done and apologized anyway.
  5. He yelled until his voice gave out at the third quarter.
  6. "Nobody's yelling," she said, yelling slightly.

See also: shout, scold, snap, cry
