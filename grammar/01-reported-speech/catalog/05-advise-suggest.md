# Advising, suggesting, and warning

> The verbs that report someone trying to steer what another person does — advice offered, a course proposed, a danger pointed out, a mind changed.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

Every verb in this file reports a speech act aimed at somebody's future behavior. What separates them is force and direction: *suggest* floats an option, *urge* leans on you, *warn* tells you what will go wrong, *dissuade* reports that you were pulled back from something you meant to do. They also differ in whether they claim success — *urge* and *advise* say only that the words were spoken, while *persuade*, *convince*, *talk into*, and *prevail on* say the words worked.

But the fault line that actually breaks learners' sentences is grammatical, not semantic. Some of these verbs take a **person as a direct object followed by a to-infinitive** (*advised me to go*, *warned him not to sign*); others **refuse a personal object entirely** (*suggested that I go*, never ***suggested me to go***). Nothing about the meaning predicts which group a verb falls into — *advise* and *recommend* mean nearly the same thing and behave in opposite ways — so the split has to be learned verb by verb, and it is drawn explicitly below before the entries begin.

## The split: object + to-infinitive vs. no personal object

### Group A — takes an object plus a to-infinitive

The person being steered goes straight after the verb, with no preposition, and the directive becomes a to-infinitive. Negate with *not to*, never *to not*.

> "You should see a doctor," she said.  →  She **advised me to see** a doctor.
> "Please stay one more day."  →  They **urged us to stay** one more day.
> "Don't sign anything today."  →  He **warned me not to sign** anything that day.

**advise, urge, warn, caution, counsel, admonish, encourage, exhort, invite, persuade, convince, press, prompt, nudge, push, enjoin, forewarn**

### Group B — refuses a personal object

These verbs never put a person between themselves and their complement. To name the person, you either put them inside a *that*-clause or attach them with a preposition — and the preposition version has to sit alongside the clause, not replace it.

> "Let's meet Thursday," she said.  →  She **suggested that we meet** Thursday. (not ***suggested us to meet***)
> "You should try the branzino."  →  He **recommended trying** the branzino. (not ***recommended me to try***)
> "Why don't we postpone?"  →  She **proposed postponing**. (not ***proposed us to postpone***)

**suggest, recommend, propose, advocate, move, put forward, float, prescribe, endorse, flag**

### Group C — needs a preposition before the second verb

The stopping-and-steering verbs. The person is a direct object, but what follows is a preposition plus a gerund, never an infinitive.

> "Don't take the job."  →  She **talked me out of taking** the job.
> "You really shouldn't drive tonight."  →  They **dissuaded him from driving** that night.

**dissuade, discourage, deter, talk into, talk out of, steer** — and, with shapes of their own, **push** and **nudge** (*into* + *-ing*: *pushed me into signing*) and **prevail on**, where the person follows the preposition and an infinitive follows the person (*prevailed on her to stay*).

### Group D — takes a person, but carries no instruction

Two verbs take a personal object and still refuse *to do*. You alert somebody **to** a fact or **that** something is so; you tip somebody **off**. Neither can hold the content of an instruction, so if what was said was *do this*, you need a different verb.

> "Heads up — the second server is down."  →  He **alerted me that** the second server was down. (not ***alerted me to check*** it)

**alert, tip off** — and two more that report an outcome rather than any words at all: **win over** and **egg on** take a person and nothing else (*won her over*, *egged him on*), with *egg someone on to do something* the one thin exception.

### The table

Read the columns strictly. **+ sb + *to*** means *verb + person + to-infinitive*. **+ *that* + subjunctive** means a mandative *that*-clause with a bare base form; a ✗ there does not always mean the verb refuses a *that*-clause, only that it refuses the subjunctive. **+ *-ing*** means a bare gerund with no person named. **+ noun phrase (content)** means the verb takes a plain noun naming what was advised or warned of (*advise caution*, *warn of a delay*) — a ✗ in that column does not stop the verb from taking a person as its object.

Three cells deserve a second look. *Warn*, *advise*, *caution*, and *counsel* all take **against**, never *from* — ✗ *warned us from going*, ✓ *warned us against going* — while *dissuade*, *deter*, and *discourage* take **from** and never *against*. *Recommend* keeps its *against* even though it refuses a personal object: *The report recommends against expanding the program* is standard, and it is the one preposition that verb allows. And *prevail on* is the odd member of Group C: the object sits after the preposition, and what follows it is an infinitive, not a gerund (*prevailed on her to stay*).

### The three repairs for Group B

When a Group B verb is what you mean, there are exactly three ways to say it, and one of them is always available.

| Repair | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. *that*-clause | *V that* + subject + **base form** | She suggested **that I apply**. |
| 2. Bare clause | drop *that*; the subjunctive stays | She suggested **I apply**. |
| 3. Gerund | *V* + **-ing**, no person named | She suggested **applying**. |

Repair 3 throws away the information about who was supposed to act, so use it only where context restores that. If you need to name the person you were speaking *to*, a preposition can carry them — but only alongside a clause or gerund, never instead of one:

> She suggested **to me** that I apply. ✓ &middot; She suggested **to me** to apply. ✗
> He recommended the place **to us**. ✓ &middot; He recommended **us** the place. ✗

That last line is a second, quieter error: Group B verbs take no indirect object either. ✗ *recommended me a book*, ✗ *suggested us a restaurant* — the thing recommended comes first and the person follows *to*.

## The mandative subjunctive after *advise*, *suggest*, *recommend*, and *propose*

All four of these take a *that*-clause whose verb is a **bare base form** — no *-s* in the third person, no tense at all, regardless of the tense of the reporting verb. This is the mandative subjunctive, and in American English it is the default, not a stylistic flourish. The full treatment is in [Reported commands, requests, and advice](../06-commands-requests.md#the-mandative-subjunctive); what follows is what these four verbs specifically do with it.

| Reporting verb | Subjunctive clause | Not |
|---|---|---|
| advise | She advised that he **be** present. | *that he **is** present* |
| suggest | He suggested that she **leave** early. | *that she **leaves** early* |
| recommend | They recommended that the report **be** revised. | *that the report **is** revised* |
| propose | The board proposed that the fee **not apply** to students. | *that the fee **doesn't apply*** |

> "You should really be there by nine," she said.  →  She advised that I **be** there by nine.
> "The committee ought to reconsider."  →  He proposed that the committee **reconsider**.

Three things fall out of this.

**The negative takes a bare *not*, with no *do*.** ✓ *recommended that he **not sign*** — ✗ *that he **doesn't sign***, ✗ *that he **not to sign***.

**It never backshifts.** A subjunctive has no tense to move, so a past-tense reporting verb changes nothing: *She suggests that he go* and *She suggested that he go* have the identical clause. See [When not to backshift](../03-no-backshift.md#8-the-subjunctive-does-not-shift).

**British English prefers *should*, and Americans hear it as a different sentence.** *She suggested that he should leave* is entirely normal in Britain and sounds either stiff or oddly permissive in American English. Use the bare form. What you must not do is reach for the plain indicative, because after *suggest* and its neighbors the indicative changes the meaning outright:

| Clause | Reads as |
|---|---|
| She suggested that he **be** more careful. | advice — she told him to be careful |
| She suggested that he **was** careless. | evidence — she implied it as a fact |

*Recommend*, *propose*, *urge*, *insist*, *demand*, and *move* behave the same way. *Advise* is the mildest of the set and tolerates a *should*-clause more comfortably than the others, but the bare form is still the American default. For the verb that most often straddles the demand/claim line, see [insist](03-agree-refuse.md#insist).

**Which verbs in this file trigger it.** Ten: [advise](#advise), [advocate](#advocate), [counsel](#counsel), [enjoin](#enjoin), [move](#move), [prescribe](#prescribe), [propose](#propose), [recommend](#recommend), [suggest](#suggest), and [urge](#urge) — plus [exhort](#exhort), which takes a subjunctive clause only in archaic and biblical English (*I exhort that supplications be made*). The near-misses matter as much, because they look like they should trigger it and do not: [encourage](#encourage), [warn](#warn), [caution](#caution), [alert](#alert), [persuade](#persuade), [convince](#convince), [endorse](#endorse), [float](#float), and [put forward](#put-forward) take no mandative clause at all. ✗ *encouraged that he apply* → ✓ *encouraged him to apply*. ✗ *cautioned that the fee be waived* → ✓ *cautioned against waiving the fee*. When one of these verbs does take a *that*-clause, the clause is an ordinary indicative statement — *warned that the storm could stall*, *cautioned that the figures were preliminary* — and it reports a fact, not an instruction.

## What these verbs commit the writer to

Choosing one of these verbs is almost never neutral. Three kinds of commitment ride along with the choice, and a writer who cannot see them will editorialize by accident.

**Success.** *Persuade*, *convince*, *talk into*, *prevail on*, *win over*, *dissuade*, *talk out of*, and *deter* all assert that the words worked. Write *she persuaded him to resign* and you have said he resigned. If you know only that the attempt was made, the honest verbs are *urge*, *press*, *push*, *exhort*, *encourage*, and *advise*, none of which claims an outcome. The full split is in the table under [persuade](#persuade).

**Judgment.** Some of these verbs grade the advice as they report it, and the grade is the writer's, not the speaker's. *Egg on* says the reporter thinks the urging was reckless — nobody is ever egged on to do something sensible. *Admonish* says the hearer was already at fault. *Push* and *press* say the hearer would rather not. *Steer* says the advice was indirect, and often that it served the adviser. Where you want none of that, [suggest](#suggest), [recommend](#recommend), and [tell someone to](06-order-request.md#tell) are the flat options.

**Presupposition.** A few carry a fact you cannot cancel afterward. *Win over* presupposes the person started out opposed — you cannot win over someone who already agreed. *Tip off* presupposes the information was worth having. *Forewarn* presupposes there was time to act on the warning. *Prescribe* presupposes authority over the hearer, which is why *the doctor prescribed rest* and *my brother recommended rest* are not interchangeable. Deny the presupposition and the sentence collapses: ✗ *She won him over, though he had supported her all along.*

## The entries

### admonish

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈmɑnɪʃ/ &middot; *ad-MAH-nish*
**Type:** reporting verb — warning with reproof &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *admonish* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *admonish* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *admonish* + **sb** + *for* + **-ing** &middot; *admonish* + **sb** + *against* + **-ing** &middot; *admonish* + **sb** (bare object: *she admonished me gently*)
**Never:** ***admonish to do something*** without an object, and ***admonish someone from doing***. The preposition is *against* for the warning sense and *for* for the rebuking sense: *admonished her for interrupting*, *admonished her against interrupting again*. There is no mandative clause — ✗ *admonished that he be present* — and no gerund complement: ✗ *admonished interrupting*.
**Reports:** a warning delivered from a position of authority, mixed with mild reproach — unlike [caution](#caution), which implies no fault on the hearer's part, *admonish* says the hearer has already gone wrong or is about to.

> "Counselor, do not interrupt the witness again."  →  The judge **admonished him not to interrupt** the witness again.

1. The judge *admonished* the jury to disregard the remark.
2. Her editor *admonished* her for missing a second deadline.
3. We were *admonished* against discussing the case outside the room.
4. He *admonished* the team that another lapse would mean suspension.
5. The coach *admonished* the players to stop arguing with the referee.
6. She *admonished* me gently, and I took the point.

**See also:** [caution](#caution), [warn](#warn), [counsel](#counsel), [urge](#urge), [command](06-order-request.md#command), [charge](06-order-request.md#charge)

### advise

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈvaɪz/ &middot; *ad-VYZE* &middot; the noun *advice* is /ədˈvaɪs/, *ad-VYSE*, with an /s/ and no plural
**Type:** reporting verb — advice &middot; **Register:** neutral, tilting formal
**Patterns:** *advise* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *advise* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *advise* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *advise* + **-ing** &middot; *advise* + **sb** + *against* + **-ing** &middot; *advise* + (**sb**) + **wh**-clause &middot; *advise* + noun phrase &middot; *advise* + **sb** + *of* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***advise to do something*** with no object — this is the quiet error inside an otherwise well-behaved verb: ✗ *I advise to book early* → ✓ *I advise booking early* / *I advise you to book early*. The infinitive needs somebody to attach to. Also ***advise someone from doing something*** — the preposition is *against*: *advised us against selling*. And do not write the noun: ***I adviced him*** is not a word.
**Spelling:** *advise* is spelled *-ise* in American English too. It is not one of the *-ize/-ise* pairs, because the ending is not the Greek suffix; ***advize*** is wrong everywhere. The noun *advice* is uncountable — ✗ *an advice*, ✗ *some advices* → ✓ *a piece of advice*, *some advice*.
**Subjunctive:** *advise* takes the bare mandative form — *advised that she **be** notified*, not *that she **is** notified*. Of the four subjunctive verbs in this file it is the one most tolerant of *should*, but the bare form is still standard American.
**Reports:** the general-purpose advice verb, and the only common one that works in all three frames — object plus infinitive, gerund, and subjunctive clause. Weaker than [urge](#urge), more authoritative than [suggest](#suggest). The *advise someone of something* pattern is a separate, bureaucratic sense meaning *formally inform*: *Please advise us of any change of address.*

> "You should see a doctor about that," she said.  →  She **advised me to see** a doctor about it.
> "I wouldn't sell right now."  →  He **advised against selling** right now.

1. Her lawyer *advised* her to say nothing until the hearing.
2. I'd *advise* waiting until the prices come down.
3. The doctor *advised* that he stay off the ankle for two weeks.
4. We *advise* against booking through a third party.
5. The manual *advises* what to do if the light stays red.
6. Please *advise* us of any change in your billing address.
7. She *advised* me that the deadline had already passed.
8. Her agent *advised* her what to say if anyone asked about the delay.

**See also:** [counsel](#counsel), [recommend](#recommend), [suggest](#suggest), [urge](#urge), [caution](#caution), [inform](01-neutral.md#inform)

### advocate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈædvəkeɪt/ &middot; *AD-vuh-kayt* &middot; the noun is /ˈædvəkət/, *AD-vuh-kut*, with a reduced last syllable — the two are spelled alike and said differently
**Type:** reporting verb — public recommendation &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *advocate* + noun phrase &middot; *advocate* + **-ing** &middot; *advocate* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *advocate for* + noun phrase or **-ing** &middot; *advocate against* + noun phrase or **-ing**
**Never:** ***advocate someone to do something***, and ***advocate to do something*** — the verb takes no infinitive at all, with or without an object: ✗ *advocates to build more housing*. Repairs: *advocated that they be released*, *advocated releasing them*, *advocated their release*.
**Reports:** open, public support for a policy or course of action — a recommendation made to an audience rather than to an individual. Unlike [suggest](#suggest), it implies the speaker has taken a side and will defend it.
**Contested:** ***advocate for*** was condemned for a century as a redundancy, on the grounds that *advocate* is already transitive. That battle is over in American English: *advocate for a cause*, *advocate for patients* is now standard, and it is the only natural option when the object is a person or group rather than a proposal. Keep the bare transitive for policies (*advocates a flat tax*) and use *for* for constituencies (*advocates for veterans*).

> "The city needs to build more housing, and I'll keep saying so."  →  She **advocated building** more housing.

1. The report *advocates* a phased rollout rather than a single launch.
2. He has *advocated* for years that the deposit be refundable.
3. Nobody at the meeting *advocated* cutting the program entirely.
4. She *advocates for* families going through the appeals process.
5. The senator *advocated* an immediate freeze on new permits.
6. They *advocate* replacing the whole system, which strikes me as expensive.
7. Two members *advocated against* holding the vote before the audit.

**See also:** [endorse](#endorse), [propose](#propose), [recommend](#recommend), [push](#push), [argue](02-assert.md#argue)

### alert

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɜrt/ &middot; *uh-LURT*
**Type:** reporting verb — warning, notification &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *alert* + **sb** + *to* + noun phrase &middot; *alert* + **sb** + *to the fact* + **that**-clause &middot; *alert* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *alert* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***alert someone to do something*** — there is no infinitive frame, and the trap is that *to* is already the verb's preposition, so the wrong sentence looks right. ✗ *alerted us to check the logs* → ✓ *alerted us that the logs needed checking*, or simply *told us to check the logs*. And no bare *alert that*: an object is required, ✗ *alerted that the servers were down*. There is no subjunctive either: the *that*-clause states a fact.
**Reports:** giving someone notice of something they need to know about now, usually a risk or an anomaly. Neutral about blame and silent about what to do next, which is what separates it from [warn](#warn) — you alert someone to a fact, you warn them about a consequence.

> "Heads up — two of the servers are down."  →  He **alerted us that** two of the servers were down.

1. A neighbor *alerted* the fire department.
2. She *alerted* me to a typo on the first page.
3. The bank *alerted* him that his card had been used overseas.
4. Nobody *alerted* the night shift to the change in procedure.
5. The app *alerts* you when your balance drops below fifty dollars.
6. We should *alert* legal before this goes any further.

**See also:** [tip off](#tip-off), [warn](#warn), [forewarn](#forewarn), [flag](#flag), [notify](01-neutral.md#notify)

### caution

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɔʃən/ &middot; *KAW-shun*
**Type:** reporting verb — warning &middot; **Register:** formal, common in journalism
**Patterns:** *caution* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *caution* + **sb** + *against* + **-ing** &middot; *caution* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *caution* + **that**-clause &middot; *caution* + **sb** + *about* + noun phrase or **-ing**
**Never:** ***caution someone from doing something*** — it is *against*, like [warn](#warn) and [advise](#advise): *cautioned investors against reading too much into one quarter*. And ***caution that*** + subjunctive: despite the family resemblance to [advise](#advise) and [urge](#urge), *caution* is not a mandative trigger, and its *that*-clause is an ordinary indicative statement. ✗ *cautioned that the fee be waived* → ✓ *cautioned against waiving the fee* / *cautioned them not to waive the fee*.
**British:** in British usage *caution* has a police sense with no American equivalent — to *caution* a suspect is to read them the formal warning before questioning, and *a caution* is a recorded reprimand issued instead of a prosecution. *He was cautioned* therefore means something much more specific in Britain than in the United States.
**Reports:** a measured, unalarmed warning — the register of press releases and analysts. [Warn](#warn) says danger; *caution* says be careful. Note that the objectless *caution that* is common in reporting (*Officials cautioned that the figures were preliminary*), where the equivalent *warn that* would sound more dire.

> "Don't read too much into one quarter," the analyst said.  →  She **cautioned investors against reading** too much into one quarter.

1. The mayor *cautioned* residents to stay off the roads until morning.
2. Economists *cautioned* that the drop might be seasonal.
3. He *cautioned* me against making an offer on the first day.
4. The label *cautions* users about mixing the two solutions.
5. She *cautioned* that the numbers had not been audited.
6. I'd *caution* you to get that in writing.

**See also:** [warn](#warn), [admonish](#admonish), [advise](#advise), [alert](#alert)

### convince

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈvɪns/ &middot; *kuhn-VINSS*
**Type:** reporting verb — persuasion (belief) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *convince* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *convince* + **sb** + *of* + noun phrase &middot; *convince* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive
**Never:** ***convince that*** with no object — someone has to be convinced: ✗ *He convinced that the plan would work* → ✓ *He convinced us that it would work*. And ***convince someone into doing something*** is not idiomatic; that frame belongs to [talk into](#talk-into) and [persuade](#persuade).
**Reports:** a change of belief that actually took hold. The verb is a success verb — you cannot convince someone who remains unconvinced — which distinguishes it from [urge](#urge) and [press](#press), both of which report only the attempt.
**Contested:** the traditional rule says *convince* takes belief (*convince that*, *convince of*) and *persuade* takes action (*persuade to do*), so ***convince her to resign*** was long treated as an error. In current American English it is entirely standard, appears throughout edited prose, and no ordinary reader notices. Use it. The distinction still does real work in the other direction: *I convinced him that he was wrong* and *I persuaded him to admit it* are not the same claim.

> "Look at the numbers — this can work."  →  She **convinced me that** it could work.

1. It took an hour, but she *convinced* the committee to reconsider.
2. Nothing I said *convinced* him that the check had cleared.
3. The evidence *convinced* the jury of his innocence.
4. I'm not *convinced* the second option is any cheaper.
5. He *convinced* his parents to let him take a year off.
6. She *convinced* herself that nobody had noticed.

**See also:** [persuade](#persuade), [talk into](#talk-into), [win over](#win-over), [prevail on](#prevail-on)

### counsel

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaʊnsəl/ &middot; *KOWN-suhl* &middot; identical in sound to *council*, which is a body of people and never a verb
**Type:** reporting verb — advice &middot; **Register:** formal, professional
**Patterns:** *counsel* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *counsel* + **sb** + *against* + **-ing** &middot; *counsel* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *counsel* + noun phrase &middot; *counsel* + **sb** + *on/about* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***counsel someone from doing something***, and ***counseled that he should be removed*** is British-flavored — American practice takes the bare subjunctive, *counseled that he be removed*. The bare gerund is not the frame either: prefer a noun, *counsel delay*, over ✗ *counsel delaying*.
**British:** the past and the *-ing* form double the *l* in British spelling — *counselled*, *counselling*, *counsellor* — against American *counseled*, *counseling*, *counselor*. Every example below uses the American form.
**Reports:** advice given in a professional or pastoral capacity, or advice with unusual gravity. *Counsel patience*, *counsel restraint*, *counsel delay* — the bare noun-phrase frame — are the set collocations of this verb, the formal counterpart of *advise caution*. Reach for the noun rather than a gerund: ✓ *counseled delay*, ✗ *counseled delaying*.

> "I'd wait before you respond to any of this."  →  Her attorney **counseled her to wait** before responding.

1. His advisor *counseled* him to defer the semester rather than withdraw.
2. The elders *counseled* patience.
3. We were *counseled* against putting anything in writing.
4. She *counsels* students on financial aid appeals.
5. The report *counsels* that the review be conducted by an outside firm.
6. He *counseled* restraint, and for once everyone listened.

### deter

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈtɜr/ &middot; *dih-TUR* &middot; the past is *deterred*, with a doubled *r*
**Type:** reporting verb — discouragement (result) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *deter* + **sb** + *from* + **-ing** &middot; *deter* + **sb** + *from* + noun phrase &middot; *deter* + noun phrase (a person or an activity: *deter casual thieves*, *deter theft*)
**Never:** ***deter someone to do something***, ***deter someone doing something***, and ***deter someone against doing something***. The only preposition is *from*. There is no *that*-clause: ✗ *deterred him that it was risky*.
**Reports:** the effect rather than the words — that something stopped a person from acting, whether or not anyone spoke. Its subject is more often a circumstance than a person (*the price deterred us*), which is what separates it from [dissuade](#dissuade), where somebody argued you out of it.

4. Higher premiums have *deterred* younger drivers from buying coverage.
5. The lock is mostly there to *deter* casual thieves.
6. She was not *deterred* by the first rejection.
7. The new fee has *deterred* a number of families from the program.

### discourage

**Pronunciation:** /dɪsˈkɜrɪdʒ/ &middot; *dis-KUR-ij*
**Type:** reporting verb — discouragement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *discourage* + **sb** + *from* + **-ing** &middot; *discourage* + **sb** + *from* + noun phrase &middot; *discourage* + **-ing** &middot; *discourage* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***discourage someone to do something*** and ***discourage someone against doing something***. No *that*-clause either: ✗ *discouraged us that we should apply*. And note the asymmetry with its opposite: [encourage](#encourage) takes an object plus an infinitive, *discourage* never does — ✓ *encouraged me to apply* but ✗ *discouraged me to apply* → ✓ *discouraged me from applying*.
**Reports:** the exact opposite number of [encourage](#encourage), and the mildest of the *from* + *-ing* group — it reports advice or a policy that pushes against an action without forbidding it. The bare gerund frame is the natural way to state a blanket policy: *The city discourages idling.*

> "I really wouldn't apply for that one," she said.  →  She **discouraged me from applying**.

1. The building *discourages* deliveries after nine.
2. My advisor *discouraged* me from taking two labs in the same term.
3. Rain *discouraged* most of the crowd.
4. The policy is meant to *discourage* speculative bidding.
5. Nothing anybody said *discouraged* her from trying again.

### dissuade

**Patterns:** *dissuade* + **sb** + *from* + **-ing** &middot; *dissuade* + **sb** + *from* + noun phrase &middot; *dissuade* + **sb** (+ *from it*)
**Never:** ***dissuade someone to do something***, ***dissuade someone of doing something***, ***dissuade someone against doing***. Only *from*. There is no positive verb ✗ *suade* — the opposite of *dissuade* is [persuade](#persuade).
**Reports:** successful argument against a course of action — the person had intended to do it and, after the conversation, did not. That success condition is what separates it from [discourage](#discourage), which reports only the push. The near-synonym in ordinary speech is [talk out of](#talk-out-of); *dissuade* is what you write, *talked her out of it* is what you say.

**Patterns:** *egg* + **sb** + *on* &middot; *egg* + **sb** + *on* + **to**-infinitive (less common)
**Never:** ***egg on him*** — with a pronoun object the particle must follow it: *egged him on*, *egged them on*, never ✗ *egged on him*. ### encourage

**Never:** ***encourage that*** with a subjunctive clause — ✗ *encouraged that he apply* → ✓ *encouraged him to apply*. And ***encourage someone that***: it takes no *that*-clause of any kind. The infinitive also needs an object — ✗ *The policy encourages to recycle* → ✓ *encourages recycling* / *encourages residents to recycle*.
**Reports:** positive pressure toward an action the speaker considers good for the hearer. Softer than [urge](#urge), warmer than [advise](#advise), and unlike either it says nothing about the speaker's expertise — you encourage a friend, you advise a client.

### endorse

**Never:** ***endorse someone to do something***, ***endorse to do something***, and ***endorse that the plan go forward***. *Endorse* takes a noun phrase and nothing else: ✓ *endorsed the proposal*, ✗ *endorsed postponing the vote* is at best marginal — write *endorsed a postponement*. ### enjoin

**Pronunciation:** /ɛnˈdʒɔɪn/ &middot; *en-JOYN*
**Type:** reporting verb — solemn urging, or legal prohibition &middot; **Register:** formal, literary or legal
**Patterns:** *enjoin* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *enjoin* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *enjoin* + **sb** + *from* + **-ing** &middot; *enjoin* + **sb** + *to* + noun phrase (*enjoined us to silence*) &middot; *enjoin* + noun phrase (*enjoin silence*)
**Never:** ***enjoin someone against doing something*** — the legal preposition is *from*.
**Two opposite senses:** this verb means both *urge strongly* and *forbid by court order*, and the frame tells you which. With **to**-infinitive it urges: *enjoined us to be patient*. With *from* + **-ing** it prohibits: *enjoined the company from selling the data*. Nothing else disambiguates them, so keep the frames clean.
**Reports:** in the urging sense, a solemn charge laid on someone; in the legal sense, an injunction. Both are markedly formal — in ordinary speech use [urge](#urge) or *ban*.

> "You must all be patient with one another."  →  He **enjoined them to be** patient with one another.

1. The letter *enjoins* members to attend in person.

4. The order *enjoined* that the records be preserved.

6. The court *enjoined* further construction pending review.

### persuade

**Pronunciation:** /pərˈsweɪd/ &middot; *per-SWAYD*

**Patterns:** *persuade* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *persuade* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *persuade* + **sb** + *of* + noun phrase &middot; *persuade* + **sb** + *into* + **-ing** &middot; *persuade* + **sb** + *out of* + **-ing**
**Never:** ***persuade to do something*** with no object and ***persuade that*** with no object — the person persuaded is obligatory: ✗ *She persuaded that we should wait* → ✓ *She persuaded us to wait*. And ***persuade someone doing something***.

| deter | not necessarily — the subject may be a price or a rule | **✓** |
| discourage | not necessarily | ✗ |

4. They *persuaded* her into buying the extended warranty.
5. I couldn't *persuade* anyone to come with me.
6. The evidence *persuaded* the judge of his good faith.

### prescribe

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈskraɪb/ &middot; *prih-SKRYBE*
**Type:** reporting verb — authoritative recommendation &middot; **Register:** formal, medical or legal
**Patterns:** *prescribe* + noun phrase &middot; *prescribe* + noun phrase + *for* + **sb** &middot; *prescribe* + **that**-clause (subjunctive)
**Never:** ***prescribe someone to do something*** — like the rest of Group B it takes no object plus infinitive: ✗ *prescribed me to rest* → ✓ *prescribed rest*, *prescribed that I rest for a week*. And ***prescribe someone something*** as a double object is shaky in careful writing; prefer *prescribed an antibiotic for her*.
**Confusable:** *prescribe* and **proscribe** are opposites. *Prescribe* means order or recommend; *proscribe* means forbid. *A regimen the doctor prescribed* and *a practice the code proscribes* say contradictory things about the same activity.
**Reports:** a recommendation that carries institutional authority — a doctor's order, a statute's requirement. Where [recommend](#recommend) leaves the choice open, *prescribe* does not.

> "Take one of these twice a day for ten days."  →  The doctor **prescribed** a ten-day course of antibiotics.

1. Her doctor *prescribed* a lower dose.
2. The statute *prescribes* a thirty-day notice period.
3. He *prescribed* rest and a great deal of water.
4. Nothing in the bylaws *prescribes* how the chair is chosen.
5. The regulation *prescribes* that each unit be inspected annually.
6. She was *prescribed* physical therapy instead of surgery.

### press

**Pronunciation:** /prɛs/ &middot; *PRESS*
**Type:** reporting verb — insistent urging &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *press* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *press* + **sb** + *for* + noun phrase &middot; *press* + **sb** + *on/about* + noun phrase &middot; *press for* + noun phrase &middot; *press* + **sb** + *into* + **-ing** (usually passive: *was pressed into helping*) &middot; *press* + noun phrase (*press the point*)
**Never:** ***press someone that*** — no *that*-clause follows it, and no subjunctive. And ***press someone into to do something***: after *into* comes a gerund or a noun, never an infinitive. The set phrase is *press someone into service*; with a verb it is *pressed into serving*.
**Reports:** urging kept up past the point of comfort. It reports repetition and pressure, not success — you can press someone all week and get nowhere, which is exactly what separates it from [persuade](#persuade).

> "I need an answer today. Today. Can you give me one?"  →  She **pressed me to give** her an answer that day.

1. Reporters *pressed* the governor for a timeline.
2. He *pressed* her to reconsider, and she refused.
3. They *pressed* him on what he had known and when.
4. The union is *pressing for* a shorter workweek.
5. She didn't *press* the point.
6. I was *pressed* to explain where the money had gone.
7. He was *pressed into* chairing the session when the moderator canceled.

**See also:** [urge](#urge), [push](#push), [persuade](#persuade), [exhort](#exhort), [nag](06-order-request.md#nag)

### prevail on

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈveɪl ɑn/ &middot; *prih-VAYL-on* &middot; also *prevail upon*, /prɪˈveɪl əˌpɑn/, which is even more formal
**Type:** reporting verb — successful persuasion &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *prevail on/upon* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive
**Never:** ***prevail someone to do something*** — the preposition is obligatory. And ***prevail on someone that***, ***prevail on someone into doing***. This verb has exactly one frame.
**Reports:** persuading a reluctant person to do something, usually as a favor and usually after some effort. It is a success verb like [persuade](#persuade), but it carries the extra information that the person had to be brought around. Distinctly formal — in speech, *talked her into it*.

> "Please, would you consider chairing the committee this year?"  →  They **prevailed on her to chair** the committee.

1. We finally *prevailed on* him to take a few days off.
2. She was *prevailed upon* to say a few words.
3. They *prevailed on* the landlord to extend the lease by a month.
4. I could not *prevail on* anyone to trade shifts.
5. His colleagues *prevailed upon* him to withdraw the letter.
6. The board *prevailed on* the founder to stay through the transition.

**See also:** [persuade](#persuade), [talk into](#talk-into), [win over](#win-over), [press](#press)

### prompt

**Pronunciation:** /prɑmpt/ &middot; *PRAHMPT*
**Type:** reporting verb — causing someone to act or speak &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *prompt* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *prompt* + noun phrase &middot; *prompt* + **sb** + *for* + noun phrase &middot; *prompt* + **sb** + *with* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***prompt someone that*** and ***prompt that***. No clause follows it.
**Reports:** supplying the trigger for an action, often without any advice being given at all — the subject is more often an event than a person (*the outage prompted a review*). When the subject is a person, it means a small verbal push or a supplied cue, as with an actor who has forgotten a line.

> "You know, you might want to check the warranty on that."  →  His remark **prompted me to check** the warranty.

1. The audit *prompted* a full review of the vendor list.
2. She *prompted* him with the first two words and he took it from there.
3. What *prompted* you to call?
4. The system *prompts* you for a second password.
5. Complaints *prompted* the store to change its return policy.
6. A single question *prompted* an hour of explanation.

### propose

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈpoʊz/ &middot; *pruh-POHZ*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal proposal &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *propose* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *propose* + **-ing** &middot; *propose* + **to**-infinitive (= intend) &middot; *propose* + noun phrase &middot; *propose* + noun phrase + *to* + **sb** &middot; *propose* + **sb** + *for/as* + noun phrase (nominate: *proposed her for the chair*)
**Never:** ***propose someone to do something*** and ***propose to someone to do something***. Repairs: *proposed that we meet*, *proposed meeting*, *proposed a meeting*. The one place a person may follow *propose* directly is the nominating sense, where the person is the thing proposed, not the person spoken to: ✓ *proposed him as treasurer*, still ✗ *proposed him to serve as treasurer*.
**Subjunctive:** bare base form — *proposed that the deadline **be** extended*, *proposed that the rule **not apply** to renewals*. The *should* version is British; the indicative, ✗ *proposed that the deadline **is** extended*, is an error in both dialects.
**Two frames, two meanings:** *propose to do* means the subject intends to do it; *propose doing* means the subject suggests it be done. *He proposed to sell the building* is his own plan; *He proposed selling the building* is a recommendation to the group. A third sense, *propose to someone*, is an offer of marriage and takes no complement at all.
**Reports:** a suggestion put forward formally enough to be acted on — more procedural than [suggest](#suggest), less binding than [move](#move).

> "I think we should extend the deadline by a week," she said.  →  She **proposed that** the deadline **be extended** by a week.

1. The committee *proposed* a two-year pilot.
2. He *proposed* meeting halfway, in Denver.
3. She *proposed* that the fee be waived for first-time applicants.
4. What do you *propose* we do about it?
5. The city *proposes* to widen the intersection next spring.
6. Nobody *proposed* an alternative.
7. The department *proposed* her as interim chair.

**See also:** [suggest](#suggest), [recommend](#recommend), [move](#move), [put forward](#put-forward), [float](#float)

### push

**Pronunciation:** /pʊʃ/ &middot; *PUUSH* &middot; the vowel is /ʊ/ as in *put*, not /ʌ/ as in *hush*
**Type:** reporting verb — forceful urging &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Patterns:** *push* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *push for* + noun phrase &middot; *push* + **sb** + *into* + **-ing** &middot; *push* + noun phrase (*push a proposal*)
**Never:** ***push someone that***. No *that*-clause. And ***push someone to doing something*** — after the object it is a plain infinitive, or *into* plus a gerund.
**Reports:** sustained pressure toward an action, with a hint that the person being pushed would rather not. *Push for* reports campaigning for an outcome rather than pressuring a person, and is the neutral option in news writing.

> "You need to ask for a raise. This week. I mean it."  →  She **pushed me to ask** for a raise.

1. His parents *pushed* him to apply to law school.
2. Advocates are *pushing for* stricter labeling rules.
3. Don't let anyone *push* you into signing today.
4. She *pushed* the proposal through in two meetings.
5. The board *pushed* management to cut costs before the quarter closed.
6. I'm not going to *push* it.

**See also:** [press](#press), [urge](#urge), [nudge](#nudge), [advocate](#advocate), [demand](06-order-request.md#demand)

### put forward

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpʊt ˈfɔrwərd/ &middot; *puut-FOR-werd*
**Type:** reporting verb — offering a proposal or view &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *put forward* + noun phrase &middot; *put* + noun phrase + *forward* &middot; *put forward the idea/view/argument* + **that**-clause &middot; *put* + **sb** + *forward* (as a candidate)
**Never:** ***put forward someone to do something*** and ***put forward to do something***. The clause frame requires a noun to hang on: ✗ *put forward that we postpone* → ✓ *put forward the idea that we postpone*, or just *proposed postponing*.
**Separable:** with a pronoun object the particle must follow it — *put it forward*, never ***put forward it***. With a full noun phrase either order works: *put forward a proposal* / *put a proposal forward*.
**Reports:** offering something for consideration — a name, a theory, a plan. More tentative than [propose](#propose) and much more tentative than [move](#move); it says the idea is now on the table, not that the speaker is fighting for it.

> "Here's one option: we could run the pilot in two cities instead of five."  →  She **put forward** a two-city pilot.

1. He *put forward* three names for the vacancy.
2. The paper *puts forward* a theory nobody had considered.
3. She *put* the amendment *forward* on the last day of the session.
4. The idea was *put forward* years ago and quietly dropped.
5. They *put* him *forward* as a compromise candidate.
6. Nobody *put forward* a serious objection.

### recommend

**Pronunciation:** /ˌrɛkəˈmɛnd/ &middot; *rek-uh-MEND* &middot; one *c*, two *m*'s
**Type:** reporting verb — recommendation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *recommend* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *recommend* + **-ing** &middot; *recommend* + noun phrase &middot; *recommend* + noun phrase + *to* + **sb** &middot; *recommend* + **sb** + *for/as* + noun phrase &middot; *recommend against* + **-ing** &middot; *recommend* + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***recommend someone to do something*** and ***recommend someone something***. Both are extremely common learner errors and both have easy repairs: ✗ *recommended me to apply* → ✓ *recommended that I apply* / *recommended applying*; ✗ *recommended me a hotel* → ✓ *recommended a hotel to me*. A person may follow *recommend* only when the person is what is being recommended — ✓ *recommended her for the job*, ✓ *recommended him as a tutor* — never when the person is the one being advised.
**Subjunctive:** bare base form — *recommended that he **see** a specialist*, *recommended that the fee **be** waived*, *recommended that she **not travel**.* British writing often prefers *recommended that he **should see** a specialist*; American writing takes the bare form, and ✗ *recommended that he **sees*** is wrong in both.
**Contested:** ***recommend someone to do*** is not quite dead in British English, where it turns up in older writing and still passes with some readers. In American English it is simply wrong, and it is the second most common version of the [suggest](#suggest) error. Avoid it in both dialects.
**Reports:** advice offered as the speaker's considered opinion, usually with some claim to knowledge behind it — stronger than [suggest](#suggest), less personal than [advise](#advise), and unlike [prescribe](#prescribe) it leaves the decision with the hearer.

> "You really should try the branzino," he said.  →  He **recommended trying** the branzino. / He **recommended that I try** the branzino.

1. She *recommended* a mechanic on Third Street.
2. The panel *recommended* that the policy be rewritten from scratch.
3. I'd *recommend* getting there half an hour early.
4. He *recommended* the book to everyone he met that summer.
5. The report *recommends* against expanding the program.
6. Can you *recommend* somewhere quiet to work?
7. Her advisor *recommended* what to take in the spring.
8. Two of her professors *recommended* her for the fellowship.

**See also:** [suggest](#suggest), [advise](#advise), [propose](#propose), [prescribe](#prescribe), [endorse](#endorse)

### steer

**Pronunciation:** /stɪr/ &middot; *STEER*
**Type:** reporting verb — guidance toward or away from &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Patterns:** *steer* + **sb** + *toward/to* + noun phrase or **-ing** &middot; *steer* + **sb** + *away from* + noun phrase or **-ing** &middot; *steer* + **sb** + *through* + noun phrase &middot; *steer clear of* + noun phrase or **-ing**
**Never:** ***steer someone to do something*** and ***steer someone that***. It is a preposition verb throughout; the infinitive frame does not exist, and neither does a clause of any kind — *steer* cannot carry what was actually said, only the direction it pushed.
**Reports:** guiding someone's choice without ever stating the advice outright — the word implies indirection, which is why it is the natural verb for a salesperson or a diplomat. *Steer clear of* is a fixed idiom meaning avoid entirely.

> "You might be happier with the smaller model, honestly."  →  He **steered me toward** the smaller model.

1. The agent *steered* us toward properties well above our budget.
2. She *steered* the conversation away from politics.
3. He *steered* me away from taking the night shift.
4. A good editor *steers* a writer without rewriting them.
5. They *steered* the company through two recessions.
6. I'd *steer clear of* that intersection at rush hour.
7. Her advisor *steered* her toward applying a year early.

### suggest

**Pronunciation:** /səɡˈdʒɛst/ &middot; *suhg-JEST* &middot; the /ɡ/ is pronounced in American English; British English usually drops it, /səˈdʒɛst/
**Type:** reporting verb — suggestion &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *suggest* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *suggest* + **-ing** &middot; *suggest* + **wh**-clause &middot; *suggest* + noun phrase &middot; *suggest* + noun phrase + *to* + **sb** &middot; *suggest to* + **sb** + **that**-clause
**Never:** ***suggest someone to do something*** — this is the single most common structural error in reported speech. *Suggest* takes no personal object at all before its complement: not with a bare object (✗ *suggested me to leave*), not with a preposition (✗ *suggested to me to leave*), not with a bare infinitive (✗ *suggested me leave*). Repairs: *suggested that I leave*, *suggested leaving*, *suggested it to me*. A second error rides alongside it: ✗ *suggested us a restaurant* → ✓ *suggested a restaurant to us*.
**Subjunctive:** American English takes the bare mandative form — *suggested that he **be** there by nine*, not *that he **should** be*, which is the British preference and reads as either stiff or oddly permissive to an American ear. The indicative means something entirely different: *suggested that he **was** there by nine* is a report of evidence, not a suggestion. That second sense is real and common — *The data suggest that the drug works* — and it is the only context in which the indicative after *suggest* is correct.
**Reports:** a proposal or recommendation offered without the authority implied by *tell* or the urgency of [urge](#urge). In the evidential sense it reports what facts imply rather than what a person said.

> "Why don't we meet on Thursday?" she said.  →  She **suggested that we meet** on Thursday.
> "You should apply," he said.  →  He **suggested applying** / **suggested that I apply**.

1. She *suggested* that we split the check.
2. He *suggested* meeting at the office instead.
3. The report *suggests* a link between the two events.
4. Can I *suggest* something?
5. Nobody *suggested* that the delay was deliberate.
6. I'd *suggest* leaving early if you want a seat.
7. She *suggested* to the committee that the vote be postponed.
8. He *suggested* where we might look next.

**See also:** [recommend](#recommend), [propose](#propose), [advise](#advise), [float](#float), [put forward](#put-forward), [insist](03-agree-refuse.md#insist), [demand](06-order-request.md#demand)

### talk into

**Type:** reporting verb — successful persuasion &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *talk* + **sb** + *into* + **-ing** &middot; *talk* + **sb** + *into* + noun phrase &middot; *talk* + oneself + *into* + **-ing**
**Never:** ***talk someone into to do something*** and ***talk someone to do something***. After *into* comes a gerund or a noun, never an infinitive. The object goes between *talk* and *into*: ✗ *talked into it me*.
**Reports:** persuasion that worked, in the register of ordinary speech, and usually with a shade of regret or amusement — *he talked me into it* rarely introduces a decision the speaker is proud of. The formal equivalents are [persuade](#persuade) and [prevail on](#prevail-on).

> "Come on, one more round — it's early."  →  They **talked me into staying** for one more round.

### talk out of

**Patterns:** *talk* + **sb** + *out of* + **-ing** &middot; *talk* + **sb** + *out of* + noun phrase &middot; *talk* + oneself + *out of* + **-ing**
**Never:** ***talk someone out of to do something***, ***talk someone from doing something***, and ***talk someone out to do***. The full preposition is *out of*, followed by a gerund or noun.
**Reports:** the everyday counterpart of [dissuade](#dissuade) — an argument that stopped somebody from doing what they had planned. Like *dissuade*, it is a success verb: the plan was abandoned.

### tip off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtɪp ˈɔf/ &middot; *tip-AWF* &middot; the noun is *a tip-off*, /ˈtɪp ɔf/, *TIP-awf*, with the stress moved forward and a hyphen
**Type:** reporting verb — confidential warning &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *tip* + **sb** + *off* &middot; *tip* + **sb** + *off* + **that**-clause &middot; *tip* + **sb** + *off* + *about/to* + noun phrase &middot; *tip off* + noun phrase (unsplit, with a full noun object: *tipped off investigators*)
**Never:** ***tip off him*** — a pronoun object must sit inside the phrasal verb: *tipped him off*, never ✗ *tipped off him*. (A full noun phrase may go either side: *tipped off investigators* / *tipped investigators off*.) And ***tip off someone to do something***: there is no infinitive frame, and no subjunctive — the *that*-clause states a fact.
**Reports:** passing on information privately, usually about something the hearer was not supposed to know or something illicit. The word carries a whiff of the informant — police are tipped off, reporters are tipped off — and it presupposes the information turned out to be worth having.

### urge

**Pronunciation:** /ɜrdʒ/ &middot; *URJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — strong recommendation &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *urge* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *urge* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *urge* + noun phrase (*urge caution*) &middot; *urge* + **sb** + *on* &middot; *urge* + noun phrase + *on/upon* + **sb** (formal)
**Never:** ***urge someone doing something*** and ***urge someone from doing something***. The negative is *not to*: *urged us not to wait*.
**Subjunctive:** *urge* is a full mandative trigger — *urged that the report **be** released*, *urged that she **not resign**.* British writing often has *urged that he should be released*; American writing does not.
**Reports:** recommending with pressure and often with urgency, and saying nothing about whether it worked. *Urge* is the standard verb of press releases and official statements (*officials urged residents to evacuate*), which is why it reads as slightly formal in conversation.

> "Please, you have to leave now — the road will close within the hour."  →  They **urged us to leave** immediately.

### warn

**Pronunciation:** /wɔrn/ &middot; *WORN* &middot; a homophone of *worn*
**Type:** reporting verb — warning &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *warn* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *warn* + **sb** + *not to*-infinitive &middot; *warn* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *warn* + **that**-clause &middot; *warn* + **sb** + *of/about* + noun phrase or **-ing** &middot; *warn* + (**sb**) + *against* + **-ing** &middot; *warn* + **sb** + *off*
**Never:** ***warn someone from doing something*** — it is *against*: ✗ *warned us from swimming there* → ✓ *warned us against swimming there*. And ***warn someone to not do something***; the negative goes before *to*. There is no subjunctive: ✗ *warned that he be careful* → ✓ *warned him to be careful*. Note that *warn* is the one warning verb in this file that takes both an infinitive and a clause, so both repairs are always open to it.
**Reports:** notice of a danger or a bad consequence, with the implication that the hearer can still avoid it. Stronger and more alarmed than [caution](#caution), more consequential than [alert](#alert) — you alert someone to a fact, you warn them what the fact will cost. Note the objectless *warn that*, standard in news writing: *Forecasters warned that the storm could stall.*

> "Don't sign anything until a lawyer has seen it," she said.  →  She **warned me not to sign** anything until a lawyer had seen it.

1. The ranger *warned* us about the ice on the upper trail.
2. Forecasters *warned* that the storm could stall over the valley.
3. He *warned* her that the offer would expire at midnight.
4. Signs *warn* swimmers against going out past the buoys.
5. The guidebook *warns* against hiking the ridge after noon.
6. She *warned* me off the seafood, and she was right.
7. I *warned* him, and he did it anyway.

### win over

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪn ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *win-OH-ver*
**Type:** reporting verb — successful persuasion of an opponent &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Patterns:** *win* + **sb** + *over* &middot; *win over* + noun phrase &middot; *win* + **sb** + *over to* + noun phrase
**Never:** ***win someone over to do something*** and ***win someone over that***. No infinitive, no clause — this verb reports a result and cannot carry the content of what was said. With a pronoun the particle follows: *won her over*, never ***won over her***.
**Reports:** turning a skeptic or an opponent into a supporter. It says nothing about method and everything about the starting point — you can only win over somebody who was against you, which distinguishes it from [persuade](#persuade), where the hearer may simply have been undecided.

> "All right. I still have doubts, but I'm with you."  →  She **won him over**.

1. Her presentation *won over* the two holdouts on the board.
2. It took a year to *win* the neighborhood *over*.
3. He *won* me *over* with the second draft.
4. The candidate is trying to *win over* voters who backed her opponent.
5. Nobody was *won over* by the apology.
6. She *won* them *over to* the idea of a shorter season.

| offer an idea formally, for a vote | [propose](#propose), [move](#move) | *that* + subjunctive |
| push hard, outcome unknown | [urge](#urge), [press](#press), [push](#push), [exhort](#exhort) | object + *to*-infinitive |
| push gently | [encourage](#encourage), [nudge](#nudge), [prompt](#prompt) | object + *to*-infinitive |
| push and succeed | [persuade](#persuade), [convince](#convince), [talk into](#talk-into), [prevail on](#prevail-on), [win over](#win-over) | object + *to*-infinitive (*persuade*, *convince*, *prevail on* — after *on*), or *into* + *-ing* (*talk into*); *win over* takes the object and nothing else |
| point out a danger | [warn](#warn), [caution](#caution), [alert](#alert), [forewarn](#forewarn), [flag](#flag), [tip off](#tip-off) | *warn* takes both an infinitive and a clause; *alert* and *tip off* take no infinitive at all; *flag* takes no personal object |
| argue somebody out of something | [dissuade](#dissuade), [talk out of](#talk-out-of), [discourage](#discourage), [deter](#deter) | *from* or *out of* + *-ing* |
| campaign for something publicly | [advocate](#advocate), [endorse](#endorse), [push](#push) | noun phrase; *advocate* also takes *-ing* and a subjunctive clause, *endorse* neither |
| order it with authority | [prescribe](#prescribe), [enjoin](#enjoin), [admonish](#admonish) | see entries |
