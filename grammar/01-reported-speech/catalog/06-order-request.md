# Ordering, requesting, and forbidding

> The verbs that report one person telling another what to do — or what not to do.

[← Reporting verbs](README.md) &middot; [Reported speech](../README.md)

These are the verbs that report a **directive**: an utterance whose whole point was to get somebody to act. Almost all of them run one frame — *verb + person + to-infinitive*, negated as *not to* — so what separates them is not structure but force, from *begged me to* at one end, through *asked me to* in the middle, to *ordered me to* and *commanded me to* at the other. The exceptions are worth learning as a block: *demand*, *decree*, *mandate*, and *stipulate* refuse a personal object and take a *that*-clause with the bare subjunctive (*demanded that he **leave***, never ✗ *demanded him to leave*); the prohibition verbs *prohibit*, *ban*, and *bar* take *from* plus an *-ing* form and refuse the infinitive outright; and *let* — with literary *bid* beside it — takes a **bare** infinitive with no *to*. Everything else in the group is a question of tone, and choosing the tone accurately is most of the work.

## The force scale

The frame is identical down the whole column. Only the verb changes, and the verb carries everything.

| Force | Verbs | Original sounds like | Report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desperate plea | beg, implore, plead with, beseech, entreat | "Please — I'm asking you." | She **begged me to stay**. |
| Formal petition | petition, appeal to, request | "We respectfully ask that you reconsider." | They **petitioned the board to reconsider**. |
| Neutral request | ask | "Could you stay?" | She **asked me to stay**. |
| Reminder, nudge | remind, prompt | "Don't forget to stay for the vote." | She **reminded me to stay**. |
| Persistent pressure | press, push, nag, urge, exhort, call on | "Come on, stay." | She **pressed me to stay**. |
| Non-negotiable | demand, insist on, require | "You're staying." | She **demanded that I stay**. |
| Instruction with authority | tell, instruct, direct, charge, summon | "Stay." | She **told me to stay**. |
| Absolute authority | order, command, decree, mandate, bid, adjure | "You will stay." | She **ordered me to stay**. |
| Permission | let, allow, permit, authorize | "You can stay." | She **let me stay**. |
| Prohibition | forbid, prohibit, ban, bar, proscribe, enjoin | "You may not stay." | She **forbade me to stay**. |

## The four frames

Every verb in this file takes one or more of these. The **Patterns** line on each entry says which.

| Frame | Notation | Verbs that take it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person plus infinitive | *V + sb + to*-infinitive | the majority — order, tell, ask, beg, urge, remind, allow… | He **told me to wait**. |
| Bare subjunctive clause | *V + that*-clause | demand, require, request, order, decree, mandate, stipulate, insist | He **demanded that I wait**. |
| Preposition plus gerund | *V + sb + from + -ing* | prohibit, ban, bar, forbid, enjoin (in its negative sense) | The rule **bars me from waiting** there. |
| Bare infinitive | *V + sb +* bare infinitive | **let**, and literary **bid** | He **let me wait**. |

Two of these are where errors cluster. Verbs in the second row take **no personal object at all** before the clause — ✗ *demanded me to wait* — and verbs in the third row take **no infinitive at all** — ✗ *prohibited me to wait*. The subjunctive in row two is the bare base form for every subject, and it never backshifts: *He demanded that she **be** there*, not *was there*, even under a past reporting verb. Full grammar in [Reported commands and requests](../06-commands-requests.md).

**British English puts *should* in that clause, and it is not an error.** *They demanded that he **should** resign*, *the court directed that the exhibits **should** be returned*, and *she insisted that I **should** see it* are ordinary British usage, common in British newspapers and in older writing on both sides of the Atlantic. American English drops the *should* and leaves the bare form. Every subjunctive-taking verb in this file — *demand*, *require*, *request*, *order*, *command*, *decree*, *mandate*, *stipulate*, *insist*, *urge*, *ask*, *petition*, *direct*, *instruct*, *enjoin*, *exhort*, *beg*, *beseech*, *implore*, *entreat*, *forbid* — behaves the same way. Read *should* as British, not as wrong; write the bare form in American prose. What is wrong in both dialects is the plain indicative in a directive clause: ✗ *demanded that he **resigns***.

### adjure

**Pronunciation:** /əˈdʒʊr/ &middot; *uh-JOOR*
**Type:** reporting verb — solemn command &middot; **Register:** formal, archaic; legal and biblical
**Patterns:** *adjure* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *adjure* + **sb** + **that**-clause (archaic) &middot; *adjure* + **sb** + **by** + noun phrase (*adjured him by all he held sacred*)
**Never:** ***adjure someone from doing something*** — the *from* + *-ing* frame belongs to [prohibit](#prohibit) and [bar](#bar). ***adjure to do something*** with no object is also impossible: somebody is always being sworn.
**Reports:** a command laid on someone under oath or in the name of something sacred — it sits between [command](#command) and [implore](#implore) and carries an appeal to conscience that neither of its neighbors has. Unlike a plain order, it presumes the hearer wants to do right.

> "In the name of everything you believe in, tell me the truth."  →  He **adjured me to tell** the truth.
> "Swear you will not repeat this."  →  She **adjured us not to repeat** it.

1. The witness was *adjured* to answer truthfully or not at all.
2. He *adjured* them to remember what the founders had risked.
3. The high priest *adjured* him to say whether the charge was true.
4. In the last paragraph she *adjures* her readers to check the primary sources themselves.
5. Nobody had *adjured* me to secrecy; I simply kept quiet.
6. She *adjured* him by the memory of his mother not to sell the farm.

**See also:** [beseech](#beseech), [command](#command), [entreat](#entreat), [charge](#charge), [implore](#implore)

### allow

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlaʊ/ &middot; *uh-LOW*
**Type:** reporting verb — permission &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *allow* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *allow* + **-ing** (no person named: *allows smoking*) &middot; *allow* + **noun phrase** &middot; *allow* + **sb** + **noun phrase** (*allowed us ten minutes*) &middot; passive *be allowed to* (very common) &middot; *allow for* + noun phrase (= make room for, a different verb: *allow for traffic*) &middot; *allow* + **that**-clause (a different verb — see below)
**Never:** ***allow that you park here*** as a grant of permission. Permission never takes a *that*-clause in English. Repairs: *allows you to park here*, *allows parking here*. And ***allow someone doing something*** — the gerund frame drops the person entirely.
**Two verbs:** *allow* + *that*-clause is a concession, not a permission — *I'll allow that he had a point* means "I concede." It reports agreement, not authorization, and it does not take the subjunctive: *allow that he **had** a point*, not *have*.
**Reports:** permission as the withdrawal of an objection — weaker than [permit](#permit), which is institutional, and than [authorize](#authorize), which requires someone with formal power. Its negative is the everyday way English forbids things: *They don't allow dogs in here.*

> "You can use my car."  →  She **allowed me to use** her car.
> "Dogs are fine on the patio."  →  The restaurant **allows dogs** on the patio.

1. My parents never *allowed* us to eat in the living room.
2. The airline *allows* one carry-on and one personal item.
3. Her visa *allowed* her to stay another ninety days.
4. The building doesn't *allow* subletting under any circumstances.
5. The judge *allowed* the question, over the objection.
6. I'll *allow* that the timing looks bad, but nothing was hidden.

**See also:** [permit](#permit), [let](#let), [authorize](#authorize), [forbid](#forbid), [prohibit](#prohibit)

### appeal to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpil tə/ &middot; *uh-PEEL tuh*
**Type:** reporting verb — urgent public request &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; journalistic
**Patterns:** *appeal to* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *appeal to* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *appeal for* + noun phrase (no addressee named) &middot; *appeal to* + **sb** (bare: *appealed to the crowd*)
**Never:** ***appeal someone to do something*** — the preposition *to* is not optional before the person. Repairs: *appealed to him to come forward*, or switch verbs: *urged him to come forward*.
**American note:** in the courtroom sense, American English makes *appeal* transitive — *she appealed the ruling*. British English keeps the preposition — *appealed against the ruling*. In American writing, ✗ *appealed against the verdict* reads as an import.
**Reports:** an urgent request broadcast to people who are under no obligation to comply — the verb of press conferences, open letters, and relief campaigns. It has no authority behind it at all, which is exactly what distinguishes it from [demand](#demand) and [call on](#call-on).

> "If anyone saw anything that night, please come forward."  →  Police **appealed to witnesses to come** forward.
> "We need volunteers, urgently."  →  The shelter **appealed for volunteers**.

1. The mayor *appealed to* residents to conserve water through the weekend.
2. Aid groups have *appealed for* another forty million dollars.
3. She *appealed to* her brother for help with the mortgage.
4. Both sides *appealed to* the union to return to the table.
5. He *appealed to* the crowd's better instincts and got nowhere.
6. The family *appealed to* anyone with information to call the tip line.

**See also:** [call on](#call-on), [urge](#urge), [plead](#plead), [beg](#beg), [petition](#petition)

### ask

**Pronunciation:** /æsk/ &middot; *ASK*
**Type:** reporting verb — request (also the neutral question verb) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *ask* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *ask* + **to**-infinitive (the subject acts) &middot; *ask* + (**sb**) + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *ask* + **that**-clause (subjunctive; formal) &middot; *ask* + **sb** + **wh**-clause &middot; *ask* + **sb** + **if/whether**-clause &middot; *ask* + **sb** + **noun phrase** (*asked me a question*) &middot; *ask* + **sb** + **about** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***ask to someone to do something*** — the addressee takes no preposition. And ***ask for to do something*** — *for* introduces a noun, never an infinitive: *asked for the check*, not ✗ *asked for to see the check*.
**The object flips the subject:** this is the one thing about *ask* that must be right. *She asked to leave* means **she** leaves; *She asked me to leave* means **I** do. No other verb in this file makes the distinction so cheaply.
**Reports:** the unmarked request — neutral in force, neutral in register, and silent about how politely the original was phrased. "Close the window," "Could you close the window?" and "I hate to ask, but would it be possible to close the window?" all come out as *She asked me to close the window*; if the manner mattered, use an adverb or a stronger verb.

> "Could you close the window?"  →  She **asked me to close** the window.
> "Can I leave early today?"  →  She **asked to leave** early **that day**.

1. He *asked* me to water his plants while he was in Tulsa.
2. She *asked* to speak to whoever was in charge.
3. I *asked* the waiter for a glass of tap water.
4. We *ask* that all phones be silenced during the performance.
5. Nobody *asked* him to do any of this.
6. She *asked* whether the position was still open.

**See also:** [request](#request), [beg](#beg), [tell](#tell), [order](#order), [demand](#demand)

### authorize

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔθəraɪz/ &middot; *AW-thuh-ryze*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal permission &middot; **Register:** formal, institutional
**Patterns:** *authorize* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *authorize* + **noun phrase** (*authorized the payment*) &middot; passive *be authorized to* (very common)
**Never:** ***authorize to release the funds*** with nothing in between — *authorize* always takes an object, either the person empowered or the thing approved. And ***authorize someone doing something*** — no gerund frame.
**Spelling:** American English writes *authorize*, always. *Authorise* is the British and Commonwealth spelling — a dialect variant rather than an error, and note that Oxford British style prefers *-ize* too. In American prose, write *authorize*.
**Reports:** permission conferred by someone whose office gives them the power to confer it. [allow](#allow) removes an objection; *authorize* creates a right, and it implies a record — a signature, a resolution, a memo. Its passive is where it lives: *You are not authorized to be in this room.*

> "You may release the funds."  →  The board **authorized us to release** the funds.
> "The transfer is approved."  →  She **authorized the transfer**.

1. Congress *authorized* the agency to issue subpoenas.
2. Only two people are *authorized* to sign checks over ten thousand dollars.
3. Her supervisor *authorized* the overtime in writing.
4. Nobody *authorized* this purchase, and now it's on the audit.
5. The warrant *authorized* them to search the garage but not the house.
6. I'm not *authorized* to discuss an open investigation.

**See also:** [permit](#permit), [allow](#allow), [let](#let), [mandate](#mandate), [direct](#direct)

### ban

**Pronunciation:** /bæn/ &middot; *BAN*
**Type:** reporting verb — official prohibition &middot; **Register:** neutral; journalistic
**Patterns:** *ban* + **noun phrase** &middot; *ban* + **-ing** &middot; *ban* + **sb** + **from** + **-ing** &middot; *ban* + **sb** + **from** + noun phrase (*banned him from the building*)
**Never:** ***ban someone to do something*** — there is no infinitive frame at all. Repairs: *banned him from driving*, *banned driving*, *banned drivers from using phones*.
**Reports:** the suppression of an activity by a government, an institution, or a governing body — the activity is the target, and the people affected are whoever happens to be doing it. Contrast [bar](#bar), which targets a person, and [forbid](#forbid), which is what one person says to another.

> "No one may fly drones in the park."  →  The city **banned drones** from the park.
> "You are not to set foot in this store again."  →  The manager **banned him from the store**.

1. Several states have *banned* the sale of flavored vaping products.
2. The league *banned* him for the rest of the season.
3. Our building *banned* smoking on the balconies in 2019.
4. The country *banned* imports of beef from three provinces.
5. She was *banned* from the online forum for a week.
6. You can't *ban* a book from a public library that easily.

**See also:** [bar](#bar), [prohibit](#prohibit), [forbid](#forbid), [proscribe](#proscribe), [enjoin](#enjoin)

### bar

**Pronunciation:** /bɑr/ &middot; *BAR*
**Type:** reporting verb — prohibition by exclusion &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; legal
**Patterns:** *bar* + **sb** + **from** + **-ing** &middot; *bar* + **sb** + **from** + noun phrase &middot; *bar* + **noun phrase** (legal: *the statute bars recovery*)
**Never:** ***bar someone to do something***, and ***bar someone doing something*** without *from*. The preposition is obligatory whenever a person is named.
**Reports:** prohibition achieved by shutting someone out — of a place, an office, a profession, a remedy. [ban](#ban) abolishes an activity; *bar* excludes a person from it. In legal writing it is also the verb for a rule that forecloses a claim: *The statute of limitations bars the suit.*

> "You are not to enter the courtroom."  →  The judge **barred him from entering** the courtroom.
> "Former employees may not bid on these contracts."  →  The rule **bars former employees from bidding**.

1. The state *barred* him from practicing medicine for five years.
2. Security *barred* us from the loading dock without a badge.
3. A conflict of interest *bars* her from voting on the measure.
4. Nothing in the lease *bars* tenants from keeping a cat.
5. The court held that the settlement *barred* any further claims.
6. He was *barred* from the building the same afternoon he was fired.

**See also:** [ban](#ban), [prohibit](#prohibit), [forbid](#forbid), [enjoin](#enjoin), [proscribe](#proscribe)

### beg

**Pronunciation:** /bɛɡ/ &middot; *BEG*
**Type:** reporting verb — desperate request &middot; **Register:** neutral, emotionally marked
**Patterns:** *beg* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *beg* + **to**-infinitive (the subject acts) &middot; *beg* + (**sb**) + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *beg* + **of** + **sb** (formal and dated: *I beg of you*) &middot; *beg* + **that**-clause (subjunctive; literary)
**Never:** ***beg to someone to do something*** — no preposition before the person. Compare [plead](#plead), which requires one.
**The object flips the subject,** exactly as with [ask](#ask): *He begged to differ* — he differs. *He begged me to reconsider* — I reconsider.
**Reports:** a request made from need, with the speaker's dignity spent — the bottom of the force scale. Reporting a request as *begged* is a strong editorial claim about the original: it says the speaker had no leverage.

> "Please, please don't tell him."  →  She **begged me not to tell** him.
> "I'm asking you — one more week."  →  He **begged for another week**.

1. She *begged* her landlord to hold the apartment until Friday.
2. He *begged* to be excused from the table.
3. The dog *begs* for scraps every single night.
4. I *begged* him not to put it in writing.
5. They *begged* the committee for one more hearing.
6. I *beg* to differ with almost everything in that paragraph.

**See also:** [implore](#implore), [plead](#plead), [beseech](#beseech), [entreat](#entreat), [ask](#ask)

### beseech

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsitʃ/ &middot; *bih-SEECH*
**Type:** reporting verb — humble plea &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, liturgical
**Patterns:** *beseech* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *beseech* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *beseech* + **that**-clause (archaic)
**Never:** ***beseech to someone*** — like [beg](#beg) and unlike [plead](#plead), it takes a bare object. And ***beseech someone doing something***.
**Past tense:** *besought* and *beseeched* are both correct. *Besought* is the older form and still the one in scripture and poetry; *beseeched* is more common in modern American prose and is what to write if you are not aiming for an archaic effect.
**Reports:** an urgent, humbled plea — [beg](#beg) transposed into a liturgical or high-literary key. Almost nobody uses it of an ordinary conversation; using it of one is a deliberate exaggeration.

> "I implore you — spare the city."  →  He **besought them to spare** the city.
> "Hear our prayer."  →  The congregation **beseeches God to hear** their prayer.

1. She *beseeched* the governor to grant a stay of execution.
2. In the letter he *besought* his brother for forgiveness.
3. We *beseech* thee, O Lord, to look upon this house.
4. The ambassador *beseeched* both capitals to keep the line open.
5. I *beseeched* him — half joking, half not — to stop sending me drafts at midnight.
6. The petition *beseeches* the council to reconsider the closure.

**See also:** [implore](#implore), [entreat](#entreat), [beg](#beg), [adjure](#adjure), [plead](#plead)

### bid

**Pronunciation:** /bɪd/ &middot; *BID*
**Type:** reporting verb — command &middot; **Register:** literary, archaic
**Patterns:** *bid* + **sb** + **bare infinitive** (*bade her enter*) &middot; *bid* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive (older and rarer) &middot; *bid* + **sb** + **noun phrase** (*bade them farewell*)
**Never:** ***bid that she leave*** — in its command sense, modern *bid* takes no *that*-clause. And ***bid to someone to do something***.
**Forms:** past *bade*, pronounced /bæd/ &middot; *BAD* in traditional American usage, though /beɪd/ &middot; *BAYD* is widespread and unremarkable. Past participle *bidden*. The auction and card-game verb is a different word with a regular past — *bid*, *bid*.
**Bare infinitive:** *bid* is one of the very few directive verbs to drop *to* — *He bade her sit down*, not ✗ *He bade her to sit down* in current literary use. The only other verb in this file that does it is [let](#let).
**Reports:** a command in a raised, old-fashioned register — the verb of scripture, fairy tales, and historical fiction. In everyday American English it survives mainly in *bid farewell* and *do as you're bidden*.

> "Come in."  →  He **bade her enter**.
> "Say nothing to anyone."  →  She **bade us hold** our tongues.

1. The king *bade* the messenger rise.
2. She *bade* us farewell at the platform and did not look back.
3. He did as he was *bidden* and asked no questions.
4. The innkeeper *bade* them come in out of the rain.
5. Custom *bids* us wait until the eldest is seated.
6. I *bid* you good night.

**See also:** [command](#command), [order](#order), [charge](#charge), [tell](#tell), [let](#let)

### call on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɔl ɑn/ &middot; *KAWL awn* (also *call upon* /ˈkɔl əˈpɑn/ &middot; *KAWL uh-PAHN*)
**Type:** reporting verb — public demand &middot; **Register:** formal; journalistic and diplomatic
**Patterns:** *call on* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *call on* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *call for* + noun phrase (no addressee: *called for calm*) &middot; *call for* + **-ing** (*called for tightening the rules*)
**Never:** ***call someone to resign*** — without *on*, the verb means "telephone," and the sentence changes meaning entirely. ***call on someone doing something*** is impossible too.
**Reports:** a demand made in public, addressed to someone with the power to act, by someone with no power to compel them. It is the standard verb of resolutions, statements, and editorials. *Call upon* is the same verb, one register higher.
**Second sense:** *call on someone* also means to pay a visit, or to invite a student to speak — *The professor called on me first.* Context separates them without difficulty.

> "The minister must resign."  →  The opposition **called on the minister to resign**.
> "There should be an immediate ceasefire."  →  The resolution **called for an immediate ceasefire**.

1. Three senators *called on* the agency to release the full report.
2. The union *called for* a strike vote by the end of the month.
3. She *called upon* her colleagues to put the vote off a week.
4. The editorial *calls on* the mayor to apologize publicly.
5. Nobody *called for* his resignation, but nobody defended him either.
6. He *called on* us for a decision before the market opened.

**See also:** [appeal to](#appeal-to), [urge](#urge), [demand](#demand), [press](#press), [exhort](#exhort)

### charge

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɑrdʒ/ &middot; *CHARJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal instruction assigning a duty &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and ceremonial
**Patterns:** *charge* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *charge* + **sb** + **with** + **-ing** (*charged him with organizing the drive*) &middot; *charge* + **sb** + **with** + noun phrase &middot; passive *be charged with* (the commonest form) &middot; *charge* + **that**-clause (a different verb — see below)
**Never:** ***charge someone that they do something*** — the directive verb takes no *that*-clause, and no personal object may stand in front of one. Repairs: *charged him to finish it*, *charged him with finishing it*. And ***charge someone to do something*** when you mean *accuse*: the infinitive frame only ever assigns a duty.
**Three verbs wearing one spelling.** The complement decides which one you have written, and they are not near neighbors:

| Frame | Reports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *charge* + sb + **to**-infinitive | a solemn instruction | The judge **charged the jury to weigh** it carefully. |
| *charge* + sb + **with** + *-ing* / noun | a duty assigned | She **charged him with running** the fundraiser. |
| *charge* + **that**-clause | an accusation | The complaint **charges that the company knew**. |

The middle row is ambiguous outside context — *charged him with theft* accuses, *charged him with the accounts* assigns — and only the third row is the accusation verb proper, which never takes a personal object before its clause: ✗ *charged him that the company knew*.
**Reports:** a solemn instruction that hands somebody a responsibility — a judge to a jury, an outgoing officer to a successor, a commencement speaker to a graduating class. It is [instruct](#instruct) with ceremony attached.

> "Consider only the evidence you heard in this room."  →  The judge **charged the jury to consider** only the evidence.
> "You'll be running the fundraiser this year."  →  She **charged him with running** the fundraiser.

1. The judge *charged* the jurors to disregard the last exchange entirely.
2. The commission was *charged* with reviewing every permit issued since 2020.
3. He *charged* his successor to finish what the department had started.
4. She *charged* us to keep the news to ourselves until Monday.
5. The task force is *charged* with producing recommendations by spring.
6. In his last letter he *charged* them to look after their mother.

**See also:** [instruct](#instruct), [direct](#direct), [command](#command), [enjoin](#enjoin), [adjure](#adjure)

### command

**Pronunciation:** /kəˈmænd/ &middot; *kuh-MAND*
**Type:** reporting verb — absolute order &middot; **Register:** formal; military and literary
**Patterns:** *command* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *command* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *command* + **noun phrase** (*commanded silence*)
**Never:** ***command to advance*** with no object — somebody must be commanded. And ***command that he advances*** — the *that*-clause takes the bare subjunctive, not the indicative.
**Subjunctive:** *command that* takes the base form for every subject and never backshifts: *He commanded that the gates **be** shut*, never *were shut*.
**Second sense:** *command* also means "elicit without asking" — *commands respect*, *commands a high price*, *commands a view of the harbor*. That verb reports nothing at all; it takes only a noun phrase.
**Reports:** the strongest directive in the language — an order from someone whose authority makes refusal a breach of duty. Where [order](#order) is administrative and can come from a doctor or a court, *command* is military, royal, or divine, and in ordinary American office English it always sounds like an exaggeration.

> "Fall back to the ridge."  →  The general **commanded them to fall back** to the ridge.
> "The gates are to be shut at sundown."  →  He **commanded that the gates be shut** at sundown.

1. The captain *commanded* the crew to stand down.
2. She *commanded* him to leave the house and never come back.
3. The statute *commands* that every ballot be preserved for twenty-two months.
4. He does not so much ask as *command*.
5. Her work *commands* respect even from people who disagree with it.
6. The officer *commanded* us not to move until the all-clear.

**See also:** [order](#order), [direct](#direct), [instruct](#instruct), [decree](#decree), [bid](#bid)

### decree

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈkri/ &middot; *dih-KREE*
**Type:** reporting verb — sovereign order &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, historical, often ironic
**Patterns:** *decree* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *decree* + **noun phrase** (*decreed a general amnesty*)
**Never:** ***decree someone to do something*** — *decree* takes no personal object before a complement. Repairs: *decreed that he step down*, *decreed his removal*.
**Subjunctive:** the base form throughout, and no backshift: *The emperor decreed that the temple **be** rebuilt*, not *was rebuilt*.
**Reports:** a command that becomes law the instant it is uttered, issued by an authority answerable to no one. Applied to an ordinary person it is heavily ironic — *My sister has decreed that we're doing Thanksgiving at her place.*

> "All shops will close on Sunday."  →  The governor **decreed that all shops close** on Sunday.
> "There will be a general amnesty."  →  The new government **decreed a general amnesty**.

1. The czar *decreed* that the calendar be changed.
2. The court *decreed* that the estate be divided equally among the three children.
3. Some committee somewhere has *decreed* that we all use the new template.
4. In 1685 the king *decreed* the revocation of the edict.
5. Nature has not *decreed* that offices must be cold.
6. The board *decreed* that no exceptions be made for anyone.

**See also:** [order](#order), [command](#command), [mandate](#mandate), [dictate](#dictate), [proscribe](#proscribe)

### demand

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈmænd/ &middot; *dih-MAND*
**Type:** reporting verb — non-negotiable request &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *demand* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *demand* + **to**-infinitive (the subject acts: *demanded to see the manager*) &middot; *demand* + **noun phrase** &middot; *demand* + **noun phrase** + **of** + **sb** (*demanded an explanation of him*) &middot; *demand* + **of** + **sb** + **that**-clause (formal)
**Never:** ***demand someone to do something*** — one of the highest-frequency errors in this whole file, because *demand* looks exactly like [order](#order) and [require](#require), both of which accept the object plus infinitive. It does not. Repairs: *demanded that he resign*, *demanded his resignation*, *demanded to see him*.
**Subjunctive:** bare base form, all persons, no backshift. *She demanded that he **be** present*, not *was present*; *He demanded that the invoice **be paid***; negative with plain *not* — *demanded that we **not tell** the press*, never ✗ *that we don't tell*. British writing has *demanded that he **should** be present*, which is a dialect difference and not an error.
**The mirror-image error:** *demand*'s object-plus-infinitive gap has an exact counterpart worth knowing — *want* and *expect* take the object plus infinitive and refuse the *that*-clause. ✗ *I want that you come* and ✗ *I demanded him to come* are the same learner making opposite errors.
**Reports:** a request presented as an entitlement, with no room left for a no. It differs from [require](#require) in that a person demands and a rule requires, and from [insist on](#insist-on) in that *demand* is a single act while *insist* implies repetition against resistance.

> "I want to see the manager, right now."  →  She **demanded to see** the manager.
> "He has to resign. Today."  →  They **demanded that he resign** that day.

1. The union *demanded* that the layoffs be rescinded.
2. He *demanded* to know who had authorized the transfer.
3. Protesters *demanded* the release of the detained journalists.
4. She *demanded* of him an explanation he was in no position to give.
5. The tenants *demanded* that the heat be fixed before the weekend.
6. Nobody is *demanding* perfection; we're asking for a deadline.

**See also:** [require](#require), [insist on](#insist-on), [order](#order), [request](#request), [ask](#ask)

### dictate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪkteɪt/ &middot; *DIK-tayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — imposed terms &middot; **Register:** neutral, usually disapproving
**Patterns:** *dictate* + **that**-clause &middot; *dictate* + **noun phrase** (*dictated the terms*) &middot; *dictate* + **noun phrase** + **to** + **sb** (*dictated the terms to them*) &middot; *dictate* + **wh**-clause (*dictates who gets in*) &middot; *dictate to* + **sb** (intransitive: *nobody dictates to me*)
**Never:** ***dictate someone to do something*** — no object plus infinitive. Repairs: *dictated that he step down*, *dictated the terms of his exit*.
**Mood:** the *that*-clause goes subjunctive when *dictate* imposes a rule (*policy dictates that the form **be** notarized*) and indicative when it merely predicts or explains (*common sense dictates that this **will** take longer*). Both are current; the second is far commoner in American writing.
**Second sense:** *dictate a letter* — speak words for someone else to transcribe. That verb takes only a noun phrase and reports nothing.
**Reports:** terms laid down by whoever is in a position to lay them down, with an unmistakable note of resentment on the reporter's part. Nobody describes their own reasonable instruction as *dictating*.

> "You'll hire whoever I tell you to hire."  →  He **dictated who** they would hire.
> "These are the terms. There's no negotiation."  →  She **dictated the terms** and left.

1. The larger firm *dictated* every clause of the merger agreement.
2. Policy *dictates* that all incidents be logged within twenty-four hours.
3. He isn't going to let a subcontractor *dictate to* him.
4. Weather *dictated* the schedule more than anything on the whiteboard did.
5. The budget *dictates* how many people we can bring on this year.
6. She *dictated* two letters and a memo before nine.

**See also:** [decree](#decree), [mandate](#mandate), [order](#order), [stipulate](#stipulate), [require](#require)

### direct

**Pronunciation:** /dəˈrɛkt/ &middot; *duh-REKT* (also /daɪˈrɛkt/ &middot; *dye-REKT*)
**Type:** reporting verb — official instruction &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and corporate
**Patterns:** *direct* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *direct* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *direct* + **sb** + **to** + noun phrase (a different sense: *directed us to the elevators*)
**Never:** ***direct someone doing something*** — no gerund frame. ***direct to do something*** with no object is impossible in the directive sense.
**Subjunctive:** *direct that* takes the base form and does not backshift — *The court directed that the exhibits **be** returned*.
**Reports:** an instruction issued through a chain of authority — the verb of court orders, agency letters, and corporate memoranda. It has the force of [order](#order) without its edge: nobody is being shouted at, but nobody is being asked either.

> "Produce the documents by Friday."  →  The court **directed the company to produce** the documents by Friday.
> "The exhibits are to be returned."  →  The judge **directed that the exhibits be returned**.

1. The inspector *directed* us to shut the line down until the guard was replaced.
2. Her will *directs* that the proceeds go to the county library.
3. The memo *directed* all staff to complete the training by the fifteenth.
4. The referee *directed* both coaches to their benches.
5. He was *directed* to appear at ten and showed up at noon.
6. The statute *directs* the agency to publish its findings annually.

**See also:** [instruct](#instruct), [order](#order), [command](#command), [charge](#charge), [enjoin](#enjoin)

### enjoin

**Pronunciation:** /ɛnˈdʒɔɪn/ &middot; *en-JOYN*
**Type:** reporting verb — order **or** prohibition &middot; **Register:** formal; legal
**Patterns:** *enjoin* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive (= order them to) &middot; *enjoin* + **sb** + **from** + **-ing** (= forbid them from) &middot; *enjoin* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *enjoin* + **noun phrase** (*enjoined silence*, *enjoined the merger*)
**Never:** ***enjoin someone doing something*** without *from*, and ***enjoin someone against doing something*** — the preposition is *from*.
**The preposition carries the meaning.** This is the only verb in the file that points in both directions, and nothing but the preposition tells you which:

| Frame | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *enjoin* + sb + **to** + infinitive | commands the action | The bishop **enjoined them to** forgive. |
| *enjoin* + sb + **from** + *-ing* | forbids the action | The court **enjoined them from** selling. |

In modern American English the *from* sense dominates so completely — it is the verb behind the noun *injunction* — that a reader meeting *enjoined to* outside a religious or literary text often has to reread.
**Reports:** in law, a binding order of a court, most often one that stops something. In older moral and religious writing, a duty solemnly urged.

> "Stop selling the product immediately."  →  The court **enjoined the company from selling** the product.
> "Be patient with one another."  →  The letter **enjoins its readers to be** patient with one another.

1. A federal judge *enjoined* the state from enforcing the new rule.
2. The rule of the order *enjoined* silence between compline and dawn.
3. Plaintiffs asked the court to *enjoin* the merger pending review.
4. He was *enjoined* from contacting any former client for two years.
5. Scripture *enjoins* believers to care for the widow and the orphan.
6. The injunction *enjoining* construction was lifted in March.

**See also:** [forbid](#forbid), [prohibit](#prohibit), [bar](#bar), [direct](#direct), [charge](#charge)

### entreat

**Pronunciation:** /ɛnˈtrit/ &middot; *en-TREET*
**Type:** reporting verb — earnest plea &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Patterns:** *entreat* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *entreat* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *entreat* + **that**-clause (formal, rare)
**Never:** ***entreat to someone to do something*** — bare object, no preposition. And ***entreat someone from doing something***.
**Reports:** an earnest and courteous plea — [beg](#beg) with the desperation removed and the manners left in. Where *beg* implies the speaker has nothing left, *entreat* implies only that they care very much. It is a written word; almost no American says it aloud.

> "Please, I'm asking you to reconsider."  →  She **entreated him to reconsider**.
> "Do not go tonight."  →  He **entreated her not to go** that night.

1. The letter *entreats* the governor to commute the sentence.
2. She *entreated* her son to write, even a postcard.
3. He *entreated* them for a few more days to find the money.
4. I *entreat* you to read the second half before you decide.
5. The delegation *entreated* both sides to keep talking.
6. His mother had *entreated* him not to enlist, and he enlisted anyway.

**See also:** [implore](#implore), [beseech](#beseech), [beg](#beg), [plead](#plead), [adjure](#adjure)

### exhort

**Pronunciation:** /ɪɡˈzɔrt/ &middot; *ig-ZORT* (the *h* is silent)
**Type:** reporting verb — strong encouragement &middot; **Register:** formal; rhetorical
**Patterns:** *exhort* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *exhort* + **sb** + **to** + noun phrase (*exhorted them to greater effort*) &middot; *exhort* + **that**-clause (subjunctive; rare)
**Never:** ***exhort someone doing something***, and ***exhort to someone***. The object is bare and the complement is an infinitive.
**Reports:** urging delivered as rhetoric, to an audience rather than to a person — the verb of sermons, locker rooms, and commencement addresses. It is [urge](#urge) with an intended crowd, and unlike [order](#order) it carries no power to compel: an exhortation can simply be ignored.

> "Leave nothing on the field out there."  →  The coach **exhorted them to leave** nothing on the field.
> "Give generously, all of you."  →  The pastor **exhorted the congregation to give** generously.

1. He *exhorted* the volunteers to knock on one more door before dark.
2. The general *exhorted* his troops to hold the line one more hour.
3. Her columns *exhort* readers to vote in local elections nobody follows.
4. The letter *exhorts* the churches to stand firm.
5. She *exhorted* us to greater discipline and, less usefully, to greater cheerfulness.
6. Coaches can *exhort* all they want; the roster is the roster.

**See also:** [urge](#urge), [call on](#call-on), [press](#press), [appeal to](#appeal-to), [push](#push)

### forbid

**Pronunciation:** /fərˈbɪd/ &middot; *fer-BID*
**Type:** reporting verb — personal prohibition &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *forbid* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *forbid* + **sb** + **from** + **-ing** &middot; *forbid* + **-ing** (no person named: *forbids smoking*) &middot; *forbid* + **noun phrase** &middot; *forbid* + **that**-clause (rare, formal)
**Never:** ***forbid someone doing something*** with no preposition. Either put *from* in or switch to the infinitive: *forbade us from entering* / *forbade us to enter*.
**Forms:** past *forbade*, traditionally /fərˈbæd/ &middot; *fer-BAD*, though /fərˈbeɪd/ &middot; *fer-BAYD* is now at least as common in American speech. Past participle *forbidden*. *Forbid* is also the one verb here whose *God forbid* survives as a fixed subjunctive.
**Contested — and the textbook rule has lost.** Handbooks used to insist on *forbid someone **to** do something* and reject *forbid someone **from** doing something* as a confusion with [prohibit](#prohibit). In current American English both are standard, *from* is if anything the more common in speech, and no mainstream usage guide still calls it an error. Write whichever reads better.
**Reports:** a prohibition spoken by one person to another whom they have authority over — a parent, a boss, a commanding officer. Compare the institutional verbs:

| Verb | Typical source | Frame | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **forbid** | a person | sb + *to* / sb + *from* + *-ing* | She **forbade** us **to enter** / **from entering**. |
| **prohibit** | a rule or statute | sb + *from* + *-ing* only | The policy **prohibits** us **from entering**. |
| **ban** | a government or body | activity, or sb + *from* | The city **banned** drones. |
| **bar** | a rule, excluding a person | sb + *from* + *-ing* | The order **barred** him **from entering**. |

> "You may not enter the lab."  →  She **forbade us to enter** the lab. / She **forbade us from entering** the lab.
> "No phones at the table."  →  He **forbids phones** at the table.

1. Her father *forbade* her to see him again, which settled the matter in the other direction.
2. The lease *forbids* subletting for periods under thirty days.
3. Doctrine *forbids* members from taking an oath of any kind.
4. The coach *forbade* us to talk to reporters before the hearing.
5. Nothing in the bylaws *forbids* a board member from also being a vendor.
6. God *forbid* anyone actually reads the terms of service.

**See also:** [prohibit](#prohibit), [ban](#ban), [bar](#bar), [proscribe](#proscribe), [permit](#permit)

### implore

**Pronunciation:** /ɪmˈplɔr/ &middot; *im-PLOR*
**Type:** reporting verb — intense plea &middot; **Register:** formal to literary
**Patterns:** *implore* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *implore* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *implore* + **that**-clause (rare, formal)
**Never:** ***implore to someone to do something***, and ***implore someone from doing something***.
**Reports:** the most emotionally charged of the pleading verbs — more intense than [beg](#beg), less ritualized than [beseech](#beseech). It reports desperation without reporting loss of dignity, which is why journalists reach for it when quoting families of the missing.

> "Please — please don't do this."  →  He **implored her not to do** it.
> "Come home. Whatever's happened, come home."  →  They **implored him to come** home.

1. She *implored* the committee to hear one more witness.
2. His parents *implored* anyone who had seen the car to call the police.
3. I *implore* you to read the fine print this one time.
4. The doctor *implored* him to stop driving.
5. He *implored* her for a second chance and did not get one.
6. The letter *implores* the company not to close the last plant in the county.

**See also:** [beg](#beg), [beseech](#beseech), [entreat](#entreat), [plead](#plead), [appeal to](#appeal-to)

### insist on

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈsɪst ɑn/ &middot; *in-SIST awn*
**Type:** reporting verb — unyielding demand &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *insist on* + **-ing** &middot; *insist on* + noun phrase &middot; *insist on* + **sb's** + **-ing** (*insisted on my paying*) &middot; *insist on* + **sb** + **-ing** (*insisted on me paying* — commoner in speech, still flagged by some editors) &middot; bare *insist* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; bare *insist* (intransitive: *I insisted*)
**Never:** ***insist someone to do something*** and ***insist on someone to do something*** — there is no infinitive frame in either shape. Repairs: *insisted that I stay*, *insisted on my staying*, *insisted on paying*.
**Two verbs, split by mood.** *Insist* leads a double life, and the mood of the *that*-clause is the only thing that separates a demand from an assertion:

| Sentence | Mood | Means |
|---|---|---|
| She insisted that he **be** present. | subjunctive | She demanded his attendance. |
| She insisted that he **was** present. | indicative, backshifted | She claimed, against objection, that he had been there. |

The directive sense never backshifts; the assertive sense backshifts normally, per [Backshift](../02-backshift.md), and is catalogued separately as [insist](03-agree-refuse.md#insist). British writing renders the directive sense with *should* — *insisted that he **should** be present* — which is dialect, not error.
**Reports:** a demand repeated against resistance. [demand](#demand) is a single act; *insist* implies the speaker has already been told no at least once and is not accepting it.

> "No — I'm paying. I'm paying."  →  He **insisted on paying**.
> "You have to see it for yourself."  →  She **insisted that I see** it for myself.

1. She *insisted on* driving me to the airport at four in the morning.
2. He *insisted on* an itemized bill before he'd sign anything.
3. My grandmother *insisted* that everyone eat before the photographs.
4. They *insisted on* our being there an hour early, for no reason anyone explained.
5. The bank *insists on* two forms of identification.
6. He *insisted* that he had never met her, and the photographs said otherwise.

**See also:** [demand](#demand), [require](#require), [press](#press), [urge](#urge), [stipulate](#stipulate)

### instruct

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈstrʌkt/ &middot; *in-STRUKT*
**Type:** reporting verb — procedural directive &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *instruct* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *instruct* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *instruct* + **sb** + **that**-clause (informational) &middot; *instruct* + **sb** + **wh**-clause (*instructed us how to file it*) &middot; *instruct* + **sb** + **in** + noun phrase (= teach)
**Never:** ***instruct to fill out the form*** with no object, in the directive sense. Somebody is always instructed. And ***instruct someone doing something*** — there is no gerund frame.
**Reports:** a directive given as part of a procedure or a job — authority without emotion. A clerk instructs, a supervisor instructs, a lawyer instructs a client; none of them is angry. Compare [tell](#tell), which is neutral but personal, and [direct](#direct), which is one step more official.

> "Fill out the form in blue ink."  →  The clerk **instructed us to fill out** the form in blue ink.
> "Nobody talks to the press."  →  We were **instructed not to talk** to the press.

1. The flight attendant *instructed* passengers to stow all loose items.
2. Her attorney *instructed* her to answer only the question asked.
3. The manual *instructs* you to disconnect the battery first.
4. He *instructed* the desk that no calls were to be put through.
5. She *instructed* us in how the filing system had been organized, which took an hour.
6. The pharmacist *instructed* him not to take it on an empty stomach.

**See also:** [direct](#direct), [order](#order), [tell](#tell), [command](#command), [charge](#charge)

### let

**Pronunciation:** /lɛt/ &middot; *LET*
**Type:** reporting verb — permission &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Patterns:** *let* + **sb** + **bare infinitive** &middot; *let* + **sb** + adverbial particle (*let him in*, *let the dog out*) &middot; *let's* + **bare infinitive** (the first-person imperative)
**Never:** ***let someone to do something*** — no *to*, ever, in any tense. This is the single most reliable *let* error, and it comes from the fact that every neighboring verb ([allow](#allow), [permit](#permit), [authorize](#authorize)) requires the *to*.
**No passive:** ✗ *He was let to leave* and ✗ *He was let leave* are both impossible. Use *He was allowed to leave*. The only surviving passive is the fixed idiom *be let go* (= be fired), which is a different sense entirely.
**Forms:** *let* — *let* — *let*. No *-ed* anywhere.
**Reporting *let's*:** *Let's leave* is a proposal that includes the speaker, so it is reported with [suggest](05-advise-suggest.md#suggest) or *propose*, never with *tell*: *He **suggested that we leave*** — never ✗ *He suggested us to leave*, and never ✗ *He told us to leave*, which reports an order instead.
**Reports:** permission in its most ordinary, domestic form — no institution, no paperwork, often no explicit request.

> "You can borrow the car."  →  She **let me borrow** the car.
> "Let's take a break."  →  He **suggested that we take** a break.

1. My parents never *let* me stay out past eleven.
2. *Let* me know when the car's out front.
3. She wouldn't *let* him pay for dinner.
4. They *let* the dog out at six every morning without fail.
5. He *let* the lease run out rather than argue about the deposit.
6. Don't *let* anyone tell you this was inevitable.

**See also:** [allow](#allow), [permit](#permit), [authorize](#authorize), [forbid](#forbid), [suggest](05-advise-suggest.md#suggest)

### mandate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmændeɪt/ &middot; *MAN-dayt*
**Type:** reporting verb — legal or institutional requirement &middot; **Register:** formal; policy and journalism
**Patterns:** *mandate* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *mandate* + **noun phrase** (*mandates helmets*) &middot; passive *be mandated to* + infinitive (institutional)
**Never:** ***the law mandates employers to provide coverage*** — the active object plus infinitive is disfavored in edited American prose and flagged by usage panels. Repairs: *mandates that employers provide coverage*, *mandates coverage*. Note that the **passive** *is mandated to* is well established and unobjectionable: *The agency is mandated to review every complaint.*
**Subjunctive:** base form, no backshift — *The ordinance mandated that every unit **be** inspected*.
**Reports:** a requirement imposed by law, ordinance, or a governing body, on a whole class of people at once. Nobody mandates anything to an individual in conversation. Compare [require](#require), which is the same idea one register down and which happily takes the object plus infinitive.

> "Every employee must complete the training."  →  The policy **mandates that every employee complete** the training.
> "All new buildings will have sprinklers."  →  The code **mandates sprinklers** in all new construction.

1. Twenty-nine states *mandate* some form of paid sick leave.
2. The settlement *mandates* that the department publish quarterly data.
3. Federal law *mandates* a ninety-day notice period for plant closings.
4. The commission is *mandated* to report to the legislature each January.
5. Nothing in the charter *mandates* that meetings be held in person.
6. The district *mandated* masks for two weeks and then rescinded the rule.

**See also:** [require](#require), [decree](#decree), [stipulate](#stipulate), [dictate](#dictate), [direct](#direct)

### nag

**Pronunciation:** /næɡ/ &middot; *NAG*
**Type:** reporting verb — repeated irritating request &middot; **Register:** informal
**Patterns:** *nag* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *nag* + **sb** + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *nag* + **sb** + **into** + **-ing** &middot; *nag at* + **sb** &middot; *nag* (intransitive)
**Never:** ***nag someone that they should do something*** — no *that*-clause. And ***nag someone doing something*** without *into*.
**Reports:** requesting so often that the repetition itself has become the problem — the only verb in this group whose meaning includes the **listener's** reaction rather than the speaker's force. Reporting a request as *nagging* is an editorial verdict, and one that has historically been aimed at women far more than at men, which is worth knowing before you choose it in writing about a real person.

> "Have you called the dentist? Have you called the dentist yet?"  →  She **nagged me to call** the dentist.
> "You said you'd fix the gutter. That was in April."  →  He **nagged me about the gutter** for months.

1. My roommate *nagged* me to return the library books until I finally did.
2. She *nagged* him about the smoke detector for a year, and then it went off at 3 a.m.
3. He *nagged* his way into a corner office and a reputation.
4. Stop *nagging* — I'll do it after lunch.
5. Something about the second paragraph kept *nagging at* me.
6. They *nagged* the city into repaving the block.

**See also:** [press](#press), [push](#push), [prompt](#prompt), [remind](#remind), [urge](#urge)

### order

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔrdər/ &middot; *OR-der*
**Type:** reporting verb — command &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *order* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *order* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *order* + **noun phrase** (*ordered a recount*) &middot; *order* + **sb** + adverbial particle (*ordered him out*)
**Never:** ***order to someone to do something*** — the addressee is a bare object. And ***order that he leaves*** — the *that*-clause takes the bare subjunctive, not the indicative.
**Subjunctive:** base form for every subject, and no backshift — *The judge ordered that the records **be** sealed*, not *were sealed*. The negative is plain *not*: *ordered that the file **not be** released*.
**Negative infinitive:** *not* goes in front of *to* — *ordered us **not to** move*, never ✗ *ordered us to not move* in edited prose.
**Reports:** the default word for an authoritative directive. Broader than [command](#command), which is military or absolute, and colder than [tell](#tell), which is the same act reported without ceremony. A court, a doctor, a captain, and a parent can all order; only the first three sound natural doing it in a report.

> "Evacuate the building."  →  The chief **ordered us to evacuate** the building.
> "The records are to be sealed."  →  The judge **ordered that the records be sealed**.

1. The captain *ordered* the crew to abandon ship.
2. Her doctor *ordered* her to stay off the ankle for six weeks.
3. The court *ordered* that the company turn over its internal emails.
4. He *ordered* us out of the kitchen before the smoke alarm went off.
5. The agency *ordered* a recall of every lot from that plant.
6. Nobody *ordered* me to be here; I volunteered.

**See also:** [command](#command), [direct](#direct), [instruct](#instruct), [tell](#tell), [summon](#summon)

### permit

**Pronunciation:** /pərˈmɪt/ &middot; *per-MIT* (the noun is /ˈpɜrmɪt/ &middot; *PER-mit*)
**Type:** reporting verb — formal permission &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *permit* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *permit* + **-ing** (no person named: *permits smoking*) &middot; *permit* + **noun phrase** &middot; passive *be permitted to* (very common) &middot; *permit of* + noun phrase (formal: *permits of no exception*)
**Never:** ***permit that employees park here*** — permission takes no *that*-clause. Repairs: *permits employees to park here*, *permits parking here*.
**Stress:** the verb stresses the second syllable and the noun the first — *the city won't per**MIT** it* / *you'll need a **PER**mit*. This is the ordinary English noun–verb stress shift, and *permit* is one of its clearest cases.
**Reports:** institutional permission, stated in policies and posted on signs. Between [let](#let) (domestic), [allow](#allow) (general), and [authorize](#authorize) (a conferred power), *permit* is the one that belongs to rules.

> "Employees may park here after six."  →  The building **permits employees to park** there after six.
> "Smoking is fine on the terrace."  →  The hotel **permits smoking** on the terrace.

1. The permit *permits* excavation only between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m.
2. Her schedule doesn't *permit* a trip that long.
3. The museum does not *permit* flash photography in the print rooms.
4. Guests are *permitted* to use the pool until ten.
5. Weather *permitting*, the ceremony will be outdoors.
6. The evidence *permits of* no other reading.

**See also:** [allow](#allow), [let](#let), [authorize](#authorize), [forbid](#forbid), [prohibit](#prohibit)

### petition

**Pronunciation:** /pəˈtɪʃən/ &middot; *puh-TISH-un*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal written request &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and civic
**Patterns:** *petition* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *petition* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *petition for* + noun phrase &middot; *petition* + **that**-clause (subjunctive; legal)
**Never:** ***petition to the court to reopen the case*** — the addressee is a direct object, not the object of *to*. Repair: *petitioned the court to reopen the case*. (The **noun** does take *to*: *a petition to the court*.)
**Reports:** a request put to an authority in the form the authority requires — signatures, a filing, a formal letter. It reports the document as much as the utterance, which is why it is nearly always in the past tense and about groups.

> "We ask the court to reopen the case."  →  They **petitioned the court to reopen** the case.
> "We want a recount."  →  The campaign **petitioned for a recount**.

1. Residents *petitioned* the council to put a stoplight at the intersection.
2. He *petitioned* the state for reinstatement of his license.
3. Nineteen faculty members *petitioned* the dean to reverse the decision.
4. She *petitioned* the court to seal the juvenile records.
5. The union *petitioned* that the vote be conducted by secret ballot.
6. You can *petition* for a hardship waiver, but almost nobody gets one.

**See also:** [request](#request), [appeal to](#appeal-to), [ask](#ask), [call on](#call-on), [demand](#demand)

### plead

**Pronunciation:** /plid/ &middot; *PLEED*
**Type:** reporting verb — sustained emotional appeal &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *plead with* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *plead for* + noun phrase &middot; *plead with* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *plead* + **noun phrase** (*pleaded ignorance*, *pleaded guilty* — different senses) &middot; *plead* + **that**-clause (= argue, a different sense)
**Never:** ***plead someone to do something*** — the preposition *with* is obligatory, and dropping it is one of the commonest errors in this file, because every neighbor ([beg](#beg), [implore](#implore), [entreat](#entreat), [beseech](#beseech)) takes a bare object. Repair: *pleaded with me to stay*.
**Past tense:** *pleaded* is the form to write. *Pled* is extremely common in American speech and in courthouse usage, and no one will misunderstand it, but AP and most American style guides still print *pleaded*, including in *pleaded guilty*.
**Reports:** an appeal made over time and with visible emotion — longer than a [beg](#beg), less formal than an [appeal to](#appeal-to). Note that the courtroom senses (*plead guilty*, *plead the Fifth*, *plead a case*) are separate verbs that report an entry on the record, not a request.

> "Please, just hear me out."  →  He **pleaded with me to hear** him out.
> "Give us more time — please."  →  They **pleaded for more time**.

1. She *pleaded with* her brother to see a doctor.
2. The family *pleaded for* privacy after the verdict.
3. He *pleaded with* the officer not to write the ticket.
4. Neighbors *pleaded with* the city to slow traffic on the street for years.
5. He *pleaded* guilty to two counts and got probation.
6. She *pleaded* ignorance, unconvincingly.

**See also:** [beg](#beg), [implore](#implore), [entreat](#entreat), [beseech](#beseech), [appeal to](#appeal-to)

### press

**Pronunciation:** /prɛs/ &middot; *PRESS*
**Type:** reporting verb — persistent pressure &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *press* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *press* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase &middot; *press* + **sb** + **on** + noun phrase (*pressed him on the timeline*) &middot; *press for* + noun phrase &middot; *press* + **noun phrase** (*pressed the point*)
**Never:** ***press that someone do something*** — no *that*-clause of any kind. And ***press someone doing something***. Repairs: *pressed him to answer*, *pressed him for an answer*.
**Reports:** persistent pushing toward an action or an answer, without the authority of [order](#order) or the irritation of [nag](#nag). It is the standard verb for a journalist who will not let a question go: *Pressed on why the memo was never sent, she declined to answer.*

> "Come on — are you taking the job or not?"  →  She **pressed me to take** the job.
> "But who signed off on it? Who signed off on it?"  →  He **pressed her on who** had signed off.

1. My advisor *pressed* me to submit before the fall deadline.
2. Reporters *pressed* the spokesman for a number and never got one.
3. The board is *pressing for* an outside audit.
4. She *pressed* him on the timeline until he admitted it had slipped.
5. They *pressed* us to sign that afternoon, which was itself a warning.
6. Nobody *pressed* the point, and the meeting moved on.

**See also:** [push](#push), [urge](#urge), [nag](#nag), [prompt](#prompt), [demand](#demand)

### prohibit

**Pronunciation:** /proʊˈhɪbɪt/ &middot; *proh-HIB-it*
**Type:** reporting verb — institutional prohibition &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *prohibit* + **noun phrase** &middot; *prohibit* + **-ing** &middot; *prohibit* + **sb** + **from** + **-ing** &middot; *prohibit* + **sb** + **from** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***prohibit someone to do something*** — and unlike [forbid](#forbid), *prohibit* has no infinitive escape hatch at all. This is the sharpest structural line in the file: *forbid* takes either frame, *prohibit* takes only *from* + *-ing*. Repair: *prohibits employees from accepting gifts*.
**Reports:** prohibition written into a rule, a statute, a policy, or a lease — the prohibiting is done by the document, and the person who wrote it has usually vanished from the sentence. Compare [forbid](#forbid), where a person forbids another person.

> "Employees may not accept gifts."  →  The policy **prohibits employees from accepting** gifts.
> "No open containers on the beach."  →  The ordinance **prohibits open containers** on the beach.

1. Federal law *prohibits* airlines from denying boarding on those grounds.
2. The lease *prohibits* subletting without written consent.
3. Our code of conduct *prohibits* accepting anything worth more than twenty-five dollars.
4. The injunction *prohibited* him from coming within five hundred feet of the school.
5. Nothing *prohibits* a nonprofit from lobbying; it just limits how much.
6. The rule *prohibits* the use of personal email for agency business.

**See also:** [forbid](#forbid), [ban](#ban), [bar](#bar), [proscribe](#proscribe), [enjoin](#enjoin)

### prompt

**Pronunciation:** /prɑmpt/ &middot; *PRAHMPT*
**Type:** reporting verb — verbal nudge &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *prompt* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *prompt* + **noun phrase** (*prompted an investigation*) &middot; *prompt* + **sb** + **for** + noun phrase (*prompted him for the password*) &middot; *prompt* + **sb** + **with** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***prompt someone that they should do something*** — no *that*-clause. And ***prompt someone doing something***.
**Often not a speech act at all.** In most of its uses, the subject of *prompt* is an event rather than a speaker — *The leak prompted an internal review*. Only *prompt someone to do something*, with a human subject, reports an utterance; the rest reports causation.
**Reports:** the smallest directive in the file — one line, once, and only because the listener had stalled. An actor who forgets a line is *prompted*; so is a witness who needs the question repeated.

> "Go on — tell them what you told me."  →  She **prompted me to repeat** what I had told her.
> "And your date of birth?"  →  The clerk **prompted him for** his date of birth.

1. A whisper from the wings *prompted* him to pick up the line.
2. Her question *prompted* me to look at the numbers again.
3. The audit *prompted* a complete rewrite of the approval process.
4. The system *prompts* you for a second factor after ninety days.
5. He had to be *prompted* twice before he thanked anyone.
6. Nothing in the email *prompted* us to think it was urgent.

**See also:** [remind](#remind), [nag](#nag), [press](#press), [urge](#urge), [push](#push)

### proscribe

**Pronunciation:** /proʊˈskraɪb/ &middot; *proh-SKRYBE*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal condemnation and prohibition &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly
**Patterns:** *proscribe* + **noun phrase** &middot; *proscribe* + **-ing**
**Never:** ***proscribe someone to do something***, and ***proscribe someone from doing something*** — the *from* frame that works so naturally with [prohibit](#prohibit) is not established here. *Proscribe* takes the forbidden thing as its object, not the person.
**Not *prescribe*.** These two are near-opposites, they differ by one letter, and confusing them reverses the sentence:

| Verb | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **proscribe** | forbids, condemns | The style guide **proscribes** the passive voice. |
| **prescribe** | orders, lays down as required | The style guide **prescribes** the serial comma. |

**Reports:** a formal denunciation that also forbids — historically, outlawing a person; now, ruling a practice out of bounds. It is the verb of style guides, professional codes, and academic writing about norms.

> "The passive voice is not to be used in these documents."  →  The manual **proscribes the passive voice**.
> "Members may not accept outside money."  →  The charter **proscribes accepting outside money**.

1. Most professional codes *proscribe* undisclosed conflicts of interest.
2. The regime *proscribed* the party and jailed its leadership.
3. Nothing in the guidelines *proscribes* citing preprints; they just discourage it.
4. Church law *proscribed* the practice for four centuries.
5. Grammar handbooks once *proscribed* the split infinitive, and nobody listened.
6. The treaty *proscribes* an entire class of weapon.

**See also:** [prohibit](#prohibit), [forbid](#forbid), [ban](#ban), [bar](#bar), [decree](#decree)

### push

**Pronunciation:** /pʊʃ/ &middot; *PUUSH*
**Type:** reporting verb — sustained pressure &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Patterns:** *push* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *push* + **sb** + **into** + **-ing** &middot; *push for* + noun phrase &middot; *push* + **sb** + **on** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***push someone doing something*** without *into*, and ***push that someone do something*** — no *that*-clause.
**The *into* frame entails success:** *He pushed me into signing* means I signed. *He pushed me to sign* leaves the outcome open. The same split runs through *talk into*, *pressure into*, and *bully into*.
**Reports:** repeated pressure toward a decision, with a strong hint that the person pushed was reluctant and the person pushing had an interest. It is [press](#press) with the speaker's motives in view.

> "You should really apply. Apply. Have you applied?"  →  My advisor **pushed me to apply**.
> "Sign it. Everyone else has signed it."  →  They **pushed her into signing**.

1. Her parents *pushed* her to take the safer offer.
2. Two members of the board have been *pushing for* a change at the top since March.
3. He *pushed* me into buying the extended warranty and I've regretted it since.
4. Don't *push* him on it tonight; wait until he's slept.
5. The agency is *pushing* utilities to publish outage data.
6. Nobody *pushed* me; I wanted to go.

**See also:** [press](#press), [urge](#urge), [nag](#nag), [prompt](#prompt), [exhort](#exhort)

### remind

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈmaɪnd/ &middot; *rih-MYND*
**Type:** reporting verb — directive resting on prior intent &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *remind* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *remind* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *remind* + **sb** + **of** + noun phrase &middot; *remind* + **sb** + **about** + noun phrase &middot; *remind* + **sb** + **wh**-clause
**Never:** ***remind to bring your passport*** with no object — *remind*, like [tell](#tell), always names the person. And ***remind someone doing something***.
**Two frames, two speech acts.** The infinitive reports a directive; the *that*-clause reports information. They are not interchangeable:

| Frame | Reports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *remind* + sb + **to**-infinitive | a directive | She reminded me **to lock** the door. (Lock it.) |
| *remind* + sb + **that**-clause | a fact | She reminded me **that the door locks itself**. (It does.) |

**Reports:** a directive whose whole force comes from the listener's own earlier intention — you can only be reminded to do something you had already meant to do. That presupposition is what separates it from [tell](#tell): *She told me to lock the door* implies she decided; *She reminded me to lock the door* implies I had.

> "Don't forget your passport."  →  She **reminded me to bring** my passport.
> "The deposit's due Friday, remember."  →  He **reminded me that the deposit was** due Friday.

1. Please *remind* me to move the car before street cleaning.
2. The dentist's office *reminds* you twice, by text and by phone.
3. She *reminded* the committee that the vote had already been taken once.
4. He *reminded* us of the last time somebody tried this.
5. Nobody *reminded* me the clocks changed, and I sat in an empty room for an hour.
6. Let me *remind* you who signed the check.

**See also:** [tell](#tell), [prompt](#prompt), [nag](#nag), [instruct](#instruct), [press](#press)

### request

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈkwɛst/ &middot; *rih-KWEST*
**Type:** reporting verb — formal request &middot; **Register:** formal, written
**Patterns:** *request* + **noun phrase** &middot; *request* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *request* + **to**-infinitive (the subject acts: *requested to be reassigned*) &middot; *request* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive (formal and stiff; the passive is the natural home)
**Never:** ***request for a copy*** as a verb — *for* belongs to the noun (*a request for a copy*), not the verb. Repair: *requested a copy*. And ***request someone something*** — ✗ *requested him a copy*.
**Subjunctive:** *request that* takes the base form and does not backshift: *They requested that the session **not be** recorded*, never ✗ *that the session isn't recorded*.
**The active object plus infinitive is stiff.** *We request you to complete the form* is grammatical and reads like a form letter from 1955. American English strongly prefers the passive — *Passengers are requested to remain seated* — or the *that*-clause.
**Reports:** a request lodged in a formal register, usually in writing. It is [ask](#ask) with the temperature dropped: nobody requests a glass of water from a friend.

> "Please turn off all phones."  →  The theater **requested that all phones be** turned off.
> "Could I be moved to a different team?"  →  She **requested to be reassigned**.

1. He *requested* a copy of the inspection report in writing.
2. The court *requested* that both parties submit briefs by the tenth.
3. Guests are *requested* to keep noise down after ten.
4. She *requested* to be excused from the panel.
5. We *request* your presence at a reception following the ceremony.
6. The family has *requested* that donations go to the food bank instead of flowers.

**See also:** [ask](#ask), [demand](#demand), [require](#require), [petition](#petition), [call on](#call-on)

### require

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈkwaɪər/ &middot; *rih-KWYRE*
**Type:** reporting verb — imposed obligation &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *require* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *require* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *require* + **noun phrase** &middot; *require* + **-ing** (*requires wearing a badge*) &middot; passive *be required to* (the commonest form of all)
**Never:** ***require that every visitor signs in*** in American prose — the plain indicative in a directive *that*-clause is a British pattern and reads as an error in American writing. Repair: *require that every visitor **sign** in*. British English also has *require that every visitor **should** sign in*. Both British forms are dialect rather than error; neither belongs in American prose. What is impossible in either dialect is ***require someone doing something***.
**The contrast with *demand*.** These two mean nearly the same thing and behave differently, which is why the error migrates between them:

| | + sb + *to*-infinitive | + *that* + subjunctive | + noun phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| **require** | ✓ *requires visitors to sign in* | ✓ *requires that visitors sign in* | ✓ *requires a signature* |
| **demand** | ✗ *demands visitors to sign in* | ✓ *demands that visitors sign in* | ✓ *demands a signature* |

**Reports:** an obligation that comes from a rule, a policy, or the nature of the task rather than from a person's will. A person demands; a regulation requires. This is why *require* so often appears with an inanimate subject and in the passive: *You are required to disclose it.*

> "Every visitor has to sign in."  →  Policy **requires that every visitor sign** in. / Policy **requires every visitor to sign** in.
> "You'll need two forms of ID."  →  The bank **requires two forms** of ID.

1. The job *requires* you to be on call one weekend a month.
2. State law *requires* that landlords return deposits within twenty-one days.
3. The recipe *requires* buttermilk, and nothing else works the same way.
4. Employees are *required* to report outside income over five thousand dollars.
5. The grant *requires* filing a progress report every six months.
6. Nothing *requires* you to answer that question.

**See also:** [demand](#demand), [mandate](#mandate), [stipulate](#stipulate), [order](#order), [instruct](#instruct)

### stipulate

**Pronunciation:** /ˈstɪpjəleɪt/ &middot; *STIP-yuh-layt*
**Type:** reporting verb — written condition &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and contractual
**Patterns:** *stipulate* + **that**-clause &middot; *stipulate* + **noun phrase** &middot; *stipulate* + **wh**-clause (*stipulates how the money may be spent*) &middot; *stipulate to* + noun phrase (courtroom sense — see below)
**Never:** ***stipulate someone to do something*** — no personal object before the complement. Repair: *stipulated that he give thirty days' notice*.
**Mood tells you what it is doing.** *Stipulate* imposes a condition with the **subjunctive** and merely records a term with the **indicative**, and both are correct:

| Sentence | Mood | Reports |
|---|---|---|
| The contract stipulates that the work **be** finished first. | subjunctive | a condition being imposed |
| The contract stipulates that payment **is** due in thirty days. | indicative | a term being described |

**Second sense:** in American courtrooms, *stipulate* also means to agree that a fact need not be proved — *The parties stipulated to the authenticity of the emails.* That sense takes *to*.
**Reports:** a requirement written into an agreement as a precondition of it — the difference from [require](#require) is that a stipulation is negotiated, part of a bargain, and usually on paper.

> "The work has to be finished before we pay."  →  The contract **stipulates that the work be finished** before payment.
> "Thirty days' notice, in writing."  →  The lease **stipulates thirty days' written notice**.

1. The will *stipulates* that the house not be sold for ten years.
2. Her contract *stipulates* a minimum of four weeks' notice.
3. The grant *stipulates* how much can go to overhead.
4. They *stipulated* that neither side would discuss the settlement publicly.
5. The rules *stipulate* that entries be submitted electronically.
6. Both parties *stipulated* to the timeline, which saved a day of testimony.

**See also:** [require](#require), [mandate](#mandate), [decree](#decree), [dictate](#dictate), [insist on](#insist-on)

### summon

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmən/ &middot; *SUM-un*
**Type:** reporting verb — order to appear &middot; **Register:** formal
**Patterns:** *summon* + **sb** &middot; *summon* + **sb** + **to** + noun phrase (*summoned him to her office*) &middot; *summon* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive (*summoned to appear*) &middot; *summon* + **noun phrase** (*summoned help*)
**Never:** ***summon someone that they come*** — no *that*-clause. And ***summon to someone*** — the person summoned is a direct object.
**Not *summons*.** The noun is *a summons* (plural *summonses*). American legal usage also has a verb *summons* meaning "serve with a summons" — *He was summonsed for failing to appear* — which is a different word from *summon* and best left to lawyers.
**Reports:** an order whose entire content is "come here" — the only verb in this group about location rather than action. The person summoned has no discretion; that is what distinguishes it from *invite*.

> "Come to my office. Now."  →  She **summoned me to her office**.
> "You are to appear before the committee on the ninth."  →  He **was summoned to appear** before the committee.

1. The principal *summoned* both students to her office before lunch.
2. He was *summoned* to testify and took the Fifth.
3. She *summoned* the waiter with a look that took about a second.
4. The board was *summoned* into an emergency session on a Sunday.
5. Someone finally *summoned* help from the road above.
6. Being *summoned* to headquarters is never good news.

**See also:** [order](#order), [direct](#direct), [command](#command), [call on](#call-on), [instruct](#instruct)

### tell

**Pronunciation:** /tɛl/ &middot; *TEL*
**Type:** reporting verb — command (also the general information verb) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Patterns:** *tell* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **that**-clause &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **wh**-clause &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **noun phrase** (*told me a story*) &middot; *tell* + **sb** + **about** + noun phrase
**Never:** ***tell to sit down*** and ***tell that he had left*** — *tell* always requires an object, in every one of its uses. And ***say someone to do something*** — its partner verb *say* refuses an object entirely. The object requirement is the sharpest line between the two.
**Two verbs in one.** *Tell* reports a command with an infinitive and a statement with a *that*-clause, and switching the frame switches the speech act:

| Frame | Reports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *tell* + sb + **to**-infinitive | a command | He told me **to go** home. (an order) |
| *tell* + sb + **that**-clause | information | He told me **that he had gone** home. (a fact) |

**No subjunctive:** unlike [order](#order) and [demand](#demand), *tell* does not license a mandative clause. ✗ *He told that I leave* is not English; the *that*-clause after *tell* is always an ordinary statement, backshifted per [Backshift](../02-backshift.md).
**And *say to* is fine without an object.** Textbooks say *say* cannot report a command; in real American speech *He said to wait* and *She said not to bother* are entirely normal. What is impossible is *say* with an object. To name the addressee, you must switch to *tell*.
**Reports:** the plainest directive verb in the language — force-neutral, register-neutral, and by far the most common. Everything more specific in this file is *tell* plus an attitude.

> "Go home."  →  He **told me to go** home.
> "Don't worry about it."  →  She **told me not to worry** about it.

1. My boss *told* me to take the rest of the week off.
2. She *told* the kids not to open the door for anyone.
3. He *told* us that the flight had already been canceled.
4. *Tell* me what actually happened, not what's in the report.
5. Nobody *told* me the meeting had moved.
6. The sign *tells* you to stay on the trail, and people ignore it.

**See also:** [order](#order), [instruct](#instruct), [ask](#ask), [remind](#remind), [direct](#direct)

### urge

**Pronunciation:** /ɜrdʒ/ &middot; *URJ*
**Type:** reporting verb — strong recommendation &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Patterns:** *urge* + **sb** + **to**-infinitive &middot; *urge* + **that**-clause (subjunctive) &middot; *urge* + **noun phrase** (*urged caution*) &middot; *urge* + **sb** + **on** (a different sense: *urged the horse on*)
**Never:** ***urge to someone to do something***, and ***urge someone doing something***. The complement is an infinitive or a clause, never a gerund.
**Subjunctive:** *urge that* takes the base form and does not backshift — *He urged that the vote **be** delayed*.
**Reports:** a recommendation delivered with urgency by someone with no power to compel. It is the exact midpoint of this file: stronger than [advise](05-advise-suggest.md#advise), weaker than [demand](#demand), and unlike either, it says the speaker cared about the outcome. Officials and editorial boards urge; that is nearly all they can do.

> "You really need to see a doctor."  →  She **urged me to see** a doctor.
> "The vote should be put off."  →  He **urged that the vote be delayed**.

1. Her attorney *urged* her not to say anything publicly.
2. The Fed *urged* caution in a statement that moved nothing.
3. I'd *urge* you to read the dissent before making up your mind.
4. Public health officials *urged* residents to stay off the roads.
5. He *urged* that the matter be referred back to committee.
6. She *urged* me to apply, and I'm glad now that she did.

**See also:** [press](#press), [push](#push), [exhort](#exhort), [call on](#call-on), [advise](05-advise-suggest.md#advise)
