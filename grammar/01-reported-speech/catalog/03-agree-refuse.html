Agreeing, refusing, promising, and threatening#

The verbs that report what a speaker committed to, went along with, or shut the door on.

← Reporting verbs · Reported speech

Every verb in this file reports a commissive — an utterance that binds the speaker to a future course of action, or refuses to be bound. He agreed, she promised, they refused, he threatened: in each case the original words were about what would happen next, and the reporting verb absorbs the whole of it, which is why will and won't so often vanish from the report. He said he would pay me back and He promised to pay me back carry the same content; the second lets the verb do the work the modal was doing.

The pattern that runs through the group is the to-infinitive with no object. Because the person who will act is almost always the subject, these verbs take the infinitive directly — agreed to help, offered to drive, promised to call, refused to answer, threatened to sue — and inserting a person breaks them: offered me to drive*, **refused him to enter*, threatened her to leave* are all impossible. Some of the same verbs also take a that-clause (agreed that the price was fair, promised that he would be there), and a few take only a noun (rejected the offer, vetoed the bill). Refuse is the strictest member of the set: among clause complements it takes the to-infinitive and nothing else — no that-clause, no gerund, no personal object.

Which frames each verb allows#

Read the columns strictly. + to-inf means the subject of the reporting verb is also the subject of the infinitive. + obj + to-inf means a person comes between the verb and to. + that covers any that-clause, indicative or subjunctive. + prep + -ing means a gerund after an obligatory preposition, never a bare one. + NP means a plain noun-phrase object taken with no preposition — whether it names the thing refused (refused the offer), the thing promised (promised a refund), or the person addressed (assured her, warned him). A noun reached through a preposition is not this column, which is why opt for the smaller model and consent to the search are marked ✗ with the preposition shown. A bare gerund used as a noun (accepted losing the deposit) does count as an NP here, not as + prep + -ing.

accede#

Pronunciation: /əkˈsid/ · uhk-SEED Type: reporting verb — agreement, yielding · Register: formal, legal and diplomatic Patterns: accede + to + noun phrase · accede + to + -ing · accede (intransitive) Never: accede to do something and accede that — accede is never followed by an infinitive or a that-clause, and it never takes a direct object. The to is a preposition, so what follows is a noun or an -ing form: acceded to the request, acceded to releasing the records. Watch out: the same verb also means take office (acceded to the throne) and join a treaty (acceded to the convention). All three senses share the to. Reports: formal agreement to a demand or request made by someone with a claim on you — where agree is neutral and consent grants permission, accede concedes ground.

"Very well, we will release the files," the ministry said. → The ministry acceded to the request.

  1. The board finally acceded to the union's demand for a wage review.
  2. She acceded to his request that the letter be read aloud.
  3. The city acceded to publishing the inspection reports online.
  4. After three days of talks, the company acceded.
  5. The government has never acceded to a single one of those conditions.
  6. He acceded to the plan only because refusing would have cost him the contract.

See also: agree, assent, consent, acquiesce, concur

accept#

Pronunciation: /əkˈsɛpt/ · uhk-SEPT Type: reporting verb — agreement, acceptance · Register: neutral Patterns: accept + noun phrase · accept + that-clause · accept + noun phrase + as + noun phrase · accept + -ing? (see below) Never: accept to do something — one of the most persistent errors made by speakers of French, Italian, Spanish, and Portuguese, whose equivalents all take the infinitive. English accept has no infinitive frame at all. Repairs: He agreed to help, He accepted the job, He accepted an invitation to speak. Nor accept someone to do something: **They accepted him to join the board* → They accepted him as a board member, or They agreed to let him join the board. The -ing question: accept takes a gerund only when the gerund is a genuine noun phrase naming a thing accepted — She accepted losing the deposit (she came to terms with it). It cannot mean agreed to do it: **He accepted driving us to the airport* is wrong for He offered to drive us. Two speech acts: accept an offer is agreement; accept that something is true is concession. Only the second takes a that-clause: I accept that I was wrong. Reports: taking what is offered, or conceding a point — unlike agree, it presupposes something already on the table to be taken or granted.

"Yes, I'll take the position," she said. → She accepted the position. "All right, I was wrong about the date," he said. → He accepted that he had been wrong about the date.

  1. She accepted the offer without asking about the salary.
  2. I accept that the numbers don't support my original estimate.
  3. The committee accepted his resignation the same afternoon.
  4. He never accepted the verdict as fair.
  5. They accepted our apology and the matter ended there.
  6. Nobody at the table accepted that the delay was anyone's fault.

See also: agree, consent, decline, refuse, reject, turn down

acquiesce#

Pronunciation: /ˌækwiˈɛs/ · ak-wee-ES Type: reporting verb — reluctant or silent agreement · Register: formal, literary Patterns: acquiesce (intransitive) · acquiesce + in + noun phrase / -ing · acquiesce + to + noun phrase / -ing Never: acquiesce to do something and acquiesce that — no infinitive, no that-clause, and no direct object. Repairs: acquiesced in the decision, acquiesced to the new terms, acquiesced in being reassigned. in vs. to: traditional and legal usage prefers acquiesce in; acquiesce to is now at least as common in American writing and is not an error. In is the safer choice in formal prose. Reports: agreement given by not objecting — it reports compliance without endorsement, which no other verb in this group carries.

"I don't like it, but fine," he said. → He acquiesced in the decision.

  1. The minority shareholders acquiesced rather than fund another lawsuit.
  2. She acquiesced in the reorganization but made her objections part of the record.
  3. Congress acquiesced to the transfer of funds for two full budget cycles.
  4. By saying nothing for six months, the landlord had acquiesced in the sublet.
  5. He acquiesced to being interviewed only after his attorney approved the questions.
  6. Nobody acquiesces to a rule they were never told about.

See also: accede, assent, consent, agree, demur

agree#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡri/ · uh-GREE Type: reporting verb — agreement, acceptance of a proposal · Register: neutral Patterns: agree + to-infinitive · agree + that-clause · agree + to + noun phrase · agree + with + person / statement · agree + on/about + noun phrase / -ing · agree + wh-clause · agree + noun phrase (a price, a date, a text) — British English only · passive: it was agreed that… Never: agree someone to do something — agree never takes a personal object before its complement. Repairs: agreed to let her go, agreed that she could go, agreed with her. Also agree doing — a bare gerund needs a preposition first: agreed on meeting at noon, not **agreed meeting at noon*. A British frame, not an American one: transitive agree with no preposition — We agreed a price, The parties agreed the wording, They agreed a deal — is standard in British and Irish English and in international commercial writing, but it is not ordinary American English. In American prose write agreed on a price or agreed to the wording. The passive the terms were agreed carries the same British flavor; the terms were agreed on is the American form. to, with, on — the three prepositions: agree to a proposal (you accept it), agree with a person or an opinion (you share it), agree on a matter still being settled (you reach a common position). I agreed to the plan means I went along with it; I agree with the plan means I think it is right; we agreed on a plan means we produced one. Who does the action: with the infinitive, the subject of agree is the one who acts: She agreed to drive — she drives. To report agreement about somebody else, use the that-clause: She agreed that Marcus would drive. Reports: acceptance of a proposal or a statement — the plain, unmarked member of this group, with none of the reluctance of acquiesce, the formality of assent, or the permission-granting of consent.

"Fine, I'll cover your shift," he said. → He agreed to cover my shift. "You're right, the second draft is better." → She agreed that the second draft was better.

  1. He agreed to look at the engine before the weekend.
  2. Everyone at the meeting agreed that the deadline was unrealistic.
  3. She agreed to the terms but asked for the payment schedule in writing.
  4. I don't agree with him about much, but he was right about the roof.
  5. We finally agreed on a date in early October.
  6. They couldn't agree where to hold the reception.
  7. It was agreed that the vote would be taken by secret ballot.

See also: accept, consent, concur, assent, accede, refuse, promise

arrange#

Pronunciation: /əˈreɪndʒ/ · uh-RAYNJ Type: reporting verb — undertaking, settled plan · Register: neutral Patterns: arrange + to-infinitive · arrange + for + person + to-infinitive · arrange + that-clause (subjunctive) · arrange + wh-clause · arrange + noun phrase Never: arrange someone to do something — arrange cannot take a bare personal object before the infinitive; it needs for. **I arranged him to meet you at the gate* → I arranged for him to meet you at the gate, or I arranged that he meet you at the gate. Subjunctive: the that-clause after arrange is mandative, so American English uses the bare base form: arranged that a car be waiting, not that a car was waiting. See the mandative subjunctive. Reports: a commitment that has been settled with somebody else — it reports the outcome of a negotiation, where promise reports a one-sided pledge and agree reports the moment of assent.

"I'll be there at four, then." → She arranged to be there at four. "Someone from the office will pick you up." → He arranged for someone to pick me up.

  1. We arranged to meet in the lobby half an hour before the talk.
  2. She arranged for the piano to be moved while we were out of town.
  3. The clerk arranged that the deed be recorded the same day.
  4. They still haven't arranged who is covering the front desk.
  5. He arranged the whole trip in a single afternoon.
  6. I've arranged for a neighbor to feed the cat.

See also: agree, promise, undertake, decide, offer

assent#

Pronunciation: /əˈsɛnt/ · uh-SENT Type: reporting verb — formal agreement · Register: formal, written Patterns: assent (intransitive) · assent + to + noun phrase · assent + to + -ing Never: assent to do something and assent that — no infinitive complement and no that-clause, and no direct object. Repairs: assented to the proposal, assented to having the room searched. Homophone: assent and ascent are pronounced identically. The verb of agreement is the one with two s's in the middle; ascent is a climb and is only ever a noun. Mostly a noun: in ordinary American English the noun is far commoner than the verb: she nodded her assent, royal assent, by common assent. The verb belongs to formal narrative and legal prose. Reports: official or public agreement, usually to something formally put — where agree is a position, assent is an act performed on the record.

"Agreed," said the chairman. → The chairman assented.

  1. The trustees assented to the sale without discussion.
  2. She assented with a single nod and said nothing more.
  3. Both parties assented to having the hearing moved to Thursday.
  4. He would not assent to any version of the text that named him.
  5. The governor assented to the bill on the last day of the session.
  6. Nobody in the room assented, and the motion died.

See also: agree, accede, consent, concur, endorse, second

assure#

Pronunciation: /əˈʃʊr/ · uh-SHOOR Type: reporting verb — reassurance, guarantee of truth · Register: neutral to formal Patterns: assure + person + that-clause · assure + person + of + noun phrase · assure + person (alone, in I assure you) Never: assure that… with no object — assure always names the person reassured. **He assured that the figures were correct* → He assured the committee that the figures were correct, or He guaranteed that the figures were correct. Also assure someone to do something: **She assured me to come* → She assured me that she would come, or She promised to come. assure / ensure / insure: assure takes a person and removes their doubt; ensure takes a fact and makes it certain (ensure that the door is locked); insure is about insurance policies. Only assure requires a personal object. Reports: a statement made to remove somebody's doubt — it reports confidence offered rather than a commitment undertaken, which is what separates it from promise.

"Don't worry, the check went out on Monday." → He assured me that the check had gone out on Monday.

  1. The mechanic assured us that the noise was nothing serious.
  2. She assured the family of her complete discretion.
  3. I assure you, nobody outside this room has seen the draft.
  4. He assured the board that the audit would be finished before the annual meeting.
  5. They assured her of a full refund and then stopped answering her calls.
  6. Let me assure you that we take every one of these reports seriously.

See also: promise, guarantee, swear, pledge, vow

choose#

Pronunciation: /tʃuz/ · CHOOZ Type: reporting verb — decision, deliberate option · Register: neutral Patterns: choose + to-infinitive · choose + not to-infinitive · choose + noun phrase · choose + person + to-infinitive (= select) · choose + wh-clause · choose + between/among + noun phrase Never: choose that and choose doing — no that-clause and no gerund. **He chose that he would stay* → He chose to stay. **She chose staying* → She chose to stay. Irregular: choose / chose / chosen. The present is /tʃuz/ and the past is /tʃoʊz/ — the vowel is the only thing that separates them in speech, and the spelling choosed does not exist. Reporting a refusal politely: chose not to is the standard neutral way to report a declined action without the flat force of refused: He chose not to comment is journalistically careful; He refused to comment is not. Reports: a decision taken between real alternatives, with the emphasis on the option not taken — decide names the moment of resolution, choose names the selection.

"No, I'd rather not say anything." → He chose not to comment.

  1. She chose to finish the degree part-time rather than take a year off.
  2. The witness chose not to answer the last three questions.
  3. They chose the smaller apartment because of the light.
  4. The department chose her to lead the review.
  5. He couldn't choose which of the two offers to take.
  6. You may choose between a refund and a replacement.

See also: opt, elect, decide, decline, refuse

commit#

Pronunciation: /kəˈmɪt/ · kuh-MIT Type: reporting verb — undertaking · Register: neutral, common in business and political writing Patterns: commit + to + -ing · commit + to + noun phrase · commit + oneself + to + -ing / noun phrase · commit + person / organization + to + -ing / noun phrase (committed the company to a full audit) · passive: be committed to + -ing Never: commit that… — no that-clause: **They committed that they would hire locally* → They committed to hiring locally, or They promised that they would hire locally. And commit someone to do something — when an object is present, to is still the preposition, so what follows is a noun or an -ing: **The deal commits the buyer to pay the fees* → The deal commits the buyer to paying the fees. There is no frame in which commit is followed by a bare to-infinitive clause of its own. commit to do or commit to doing — contested: the textbook rule is that to here is a preposition, so a gerund must follow: committed to reducing emissions. Corporate and political English breaks this constantly — committed to reduce emissions appears in press releases every day, and reads as unremarkable to most Americans. It is still the form editors change, so write the -ing in anything that will be edited. The other commit: commit a crime, commit funds, commit to memory are unrelated senses with a plain direct object. Doubling: committed, committing, commitment — but commitment keeps a single t before -ment. Reports: a binding undertaking, usually institutional and usually public — promise is what a person says to another person; commit is what an organization puts on the record.

"We will cut delivery times by half," the company said. → The company committed to cutting delivery times by half.

  1. The city committed to replacing every lead service line within ten years.
  2. She committed herself to the project before she saw the budget.
  3. Neither side would commit to a date.
  4. They've committed to a full audit, and the letter says so in writing.
  5. He was committed to finishing the trail before the first snow.
  6. Don't commit to anything until you've read the second page.

See also: promise, pledge, undertake, guarantee, vow

concur#

Pronunciation: /kənˈkɜr/ · kuhn-KUR Type: reporting verb — agreement with a statement or judgment · Register: formal, academic and legal Patterns: concur (intransitive) · concur + that-clause · concur + with + person / opinion · concur + in + noun phrase (legal) · concur + on + noun phrase Never: concur to do something — no infinitive, because concur reports shared opinion, never a commitment to act. **The two agencies concurred to release the data* → The two agencies agreed to release the data. It also takes no direct object: **I concur your assessment* → I concur with your assessment. Spelling: the final consonant doubles: concurred, concurring. The noun is concurrence. Legal sense: in American appellate opinions, a judge who concurs in the judgment agrees with the outcome but not necessarily the reasoning — a technical distinction that the everyday agree cannot make. Reports: agreement with an assessment or a judgment already stated — it never reports agreement to do anything, which is precisely what separates it from agree.

"Yes, I read the data the same way." → She concurred that the data supported the finding.

  1. The second reviewer concurred that the methodology was sound.
  2. Two of the three judges concurred in the judgment but wrote separately.
  3. I concur with everything my colleague has just said.
  4. The panel concurred on the diagnosis within an hour.
  5. She read the report twice and concurred.
  6. The auditors did not concur that the reserve was adequate.

See also: agree, assent, accept, endorse, second

consent#

Pronunciation: /kənˈsɛnt/ · kuhn-SENT Type: reporting verb — permission, agreement to something affecting oneself · Register: neutral to formal; the standard verb in legal and medical contexts Patterns: consent + to-infinitive · consent + to + noun phrase · consent + to + -ing · consent (intransitive) Never: consent someone to do something — no personal object. **The judge consented her to travel* → The judge allowed her to travel or The judge consented to her traveling. Consent that… survives only in old legal formulas (consents that judgment be entered); in ordinary modern English write agreed that. Two kinds of to: consent takes both an infinitive to and a prepositional to, with almost the same meaning: consented to be interviewed and consented to being interviewed are both standard. Very few verbs allow this pair — compare refuse, which allows only the infinitive. Stress: the verb is kuhn-SENT; there is no noun/verb stress shift, unlike reject and refuse. Reports: permission granted for something that will be done to you or in your name — where agree accepts a proposal, consent authorizes an act.

"Yes, you may search the car." → He consented to the search. "All right, I'll be recorded." → She consented to be recorded.

  1. The patient consented to the procedure after reading the risks.
  2. Her parents consented to her taking the internship abroad.
  3. He consented to testify only under subpoena.
  4. No landowner along the route has consented.
  5. She consented to the sale of the property but not to the terms of the lease.
  6. Both parties consented to having the dispute heard by an arbitrator.

See also: agree, accede, assent, acquiesce, refuse

decide#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈsaɪd/ · dih-SIDE Type: reporting verb — decision · Register: neutral Patterns: decide + to-infinitive · decide + not to-infinitive · decide + that-clause · decide + wh-clause · decide + on/against + noun phrase / -ing · decide + noun phrase (a case, a question) Never: decide someone to do something — no personal object before the infinitive. **The letter decided me to go* → The letter decided me (object alone, fine) or The letter made me decide to go. Also decide doing: a gerund needs a preposition — decided on selling the house, not **decided selling the house*. The negative: decided not to is a decision against acting; didn't decide to is the absence of a decision. She decided not to call and She didn't decide to call are not paraphrases of each other. Two senses: decide also means settle a question and takes a plain object: the court decided the case, one goal decided the match. In that sense the subject is often not a speaker at all. Reports: the moment a course of action is fixed — where choose highlights the alternatives and resolve the determination, decide is the neutral, ordinary word.

"We're going to take the northern route." → They decided to take the northern route. "The building is safe," the inspector said. → The inspector decided that the building was safe.

  1. We decided to leave before the traffic started.
  2. He decided that the whole thing had been a misunderstanding.
  3. They still haven't decided where to hold the ceremony.
  4. She decided against saying anything until she had the paperwork.
  5. The panel decided the appeal in under a week.
  6. I decided not to renew the lease.

See also: resolve, determine, choose, opt, elect, arrange

decline#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈklaɪn/ · dih-KLINE Type: reporting verb — polite refusal · Register: formal, polite; standard in journalism Patterns: decline + to-infinitive · decline + noun phrase · decline (intransitive) Never: decline doing and decline that — no gerund and no that-clause. He declined commenting* → He declined to comment. **She declined that she would attend* → She declined the invitation or She said she would not attend. It also cannot take two objects: They declined him the loan* → They refused him the loan or They denied him the loan. And no personal object before the infinitive — decline someone to do something: **She declined me to speak* → She declined to let me speak. The newsroom verb: declined to comment is the fixed formula because refused to comment implies the reporter was entitled to an answer. The choice between the two verbs is an editorial judgment, not a stylistic one. The other decline: decline also means fall (sales declined, a declining population), which is intransitive and unrelated to speech. Reports: refusal offered courteously, with no suggestion of defiance — refuse reports a blocked demand, decline reports an offer not taken up.

"Thank you, but I'd rather not." → She declined. "I have nothing to say about that." → He declined to comment.

  1. The senator declined to say whether she had spoken to the mayor.
  2. He declined the invitation but sent flowers.
  3. Both witnesses declined to be photographed.
  4. She was offered the chairmanship twice and declined both times.
  5. The company declined to confirm the number of layoffs.
  6. I declined, as politely as I knew how.

See also: refuse, reject, turn down, demur, accept, spurn

demur#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈmɜr/ · dih-MUR Type: reporting verb — polite objection, hesitation · Register: formal, literary Patterns: demur (intransitive) · demur + at + noun phrase / -ing · demur + to + noun phrase · direct speech attribution: "…," he demurred. Never: demur to do something and demur that — no infinitive and no that-clause; the objection is never spelled out by a complement clause. **She demurred that it was too expensive* → She demurred at the price or She objected that it was too expensive. Spelling and stress: stress on the second syllable throughout, and the r doubles: demurred, demurring, demurral. Not to be confused with the adjective demure /dɪˈmjʊr/, which means modest and is a different word entirely. As a speech tag: demurred is one of the few verbs in this group used to attribute direct speech: "I'm not sure that's fair," she demurred. It signals a soft, hedged objection. Reports: a mild, hesitant objection rather than a refusal — the speaker balks but has not yet said no, which is exactly what refuse and decline have already done.

"Well… I'm not sure about that." → He demurred.

  1. When I suggested splitting the cost, he demurred.
  2. She demurred at the suggestion that the delay was her doing.
  3. The committee demurred to the chair's reading of the bylaws.
  4. "That seems a little steep," she demurred.
  5. He demurred at paying in advance and asked for an invoice instead.
  6. Nobody demurred, so the motion carried.

See also: decline, refuse, acquiesce, assent, reject

determine#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈtɜrmɪn/ · dih-TUR-min Type: reporting verb — firm decision; also a finding · Register: formal Patterns: determine + to-infinitive · determine + that-clause · determine + wh-clause · determine + noun phrase · determine + on/upon + noun phrase · passive: be determined to + infinitive Never: determine someone to do something — no personal object before the infinitive. **The evidence determined the jury to acquit* → The evidence led the jury to acquit. Also determine doing: determined on selling, not **determined selling*. Two speech acts in one verb: determine reports a decision (He determined to see it through) and, far more often in modern American English, a finding (The lab determined that the sample was contaminated). Only the first belongs in this group; the second is a verb of establishing fact, and the that-clause is what tells you which is meant. The passive is commoner than the active: in the resolve sense, be determined to has almost replaced the plain active verb: She was determined to finish is ordinary English; She determined to finish reads as literary or nineteenth-century. Reports: a decision arrived at and held to — stronger than decide, and unlike resolve it doubles as the verb for reaching a factual conclusion.

"I am going to see this through, whatever it costs." → She determined to see it through. "The valve failed before the fire started," the investigators said. → Investigators determined that the valve had failed before the fire started.

  1. He determined to learn the language before the posting began.
  2. The inspector determined that the wiring had never been permitted.
  3. We could not determine who had signed the form.
  4. The court will determine the amount of damages next month.
  5. She had determined on a complete change of career.
  6. They were determined to have the roof finished before the rain.

See also: decide, resolve, choose, vow, pledge

elect#

Pronunciation: /ɪˈlɛkt/ · ih-LEKT Type: reporting verb — deliberate choice · Register: formal, common in legal, financial, and medical writing Patterns: elect + to-infinitive · elect + not to-infinitive · elect + person (+ as / to) + noun phrase (= vote in) Never: elect that and elect doing — no that-clause and no gerund. They elected that the case be reheard* → They decided that the case be reheard. **He elected taking the buyout* → He elected to take the buyout. Nor elect for on the model of opt for: She elected for the lump sum* → She opted for the lump sum or She elected to take the lump sum. Of the three choice verbs, only opt has the for frame. Not the same as voting: the two senses share a spelling and nothing else. The board elected to defer the dividend is a decision by the board; The board elected a new chair is a vote. Only the first is a reporting verb. Reports: a formal choice between options that were both genuinely available — the register of pension paperwork and surgical consent forms, where choose would be too plain.

"I'll take the lump sum rather than the annuity." → She elected to take the lump sum.

  1. Employees who elect to enroll must do so before the fifteenth.
  2. He elected not to appeal the ruling.
  3. She elected to have the surgery in January rather than wait.
  4. Most of the partners elected to defer their distributions.
  5. The taxpayer may elect to spread the gain over four years.
  6. Two of the three defendants elected to testify.

See also: opt, choose, decide, decline, determine

endorse#

Pronunciation: /ɛnˈdɔrs/ · en-DORSS Type: reporting verb — public agreement, backing · Register: neutral to formal; standard in politics and journalism Patterns: endorse + noun phrase · endorse + noun phrase + for + office · passive: be endorsed by + person Never: endorse that and endorse to do something — endorse takes only a noun phrase. **The union endorsed that the strike continue* → The union endorsed the strike or The union agreed that the strike should continue. To endorse a statement, name it: endorsed the claim that…, endorsed her account of the meeting. Spelling: endorse is the standard American spelling in every sense; indorse survives only in some legal and banking documents. The noun is endorsement. He vowed to appeal implies determination against opposition; He promised to appeal implies only an obligation. Reports: a promise made solemnly and usually in public, with feeling behind it — pledge is institutional, swear invokes an oath, and vow carries the speaker's resolve.

"I will never work for that company again." → He vowed never to work for the company again. "We'll be back, and next time we'll win." → She vowed that they would be back.

  1. The mayor vowed to appeal the ruling within the week.
  2. She vowed that she would never lend him money again.
  3. He vowed revenge on everyone who had testified.
  4. They vowed to rebuild on the same lot.
  5. After the second flood, she vowed to sell the house.
  6. The candidate vowed to serve out the full term.

See also: swear, pledge, promise, resolve, threaten, determine

warn#

Pronunciation: /wɔrn/ · WORN Type: reporting verb — warning; a threat presented as a prediction · Register: neutral Patterns: warn + person + that-clause · warn + that-clause (no object) · warn + person + to-infinitive · warn + person + not to-infinitive · warn + person + of/about + noun phrase · warn + person + against + noun phrase / -ing · warn + person (alone) Never: warn to do something with no object — the infinitive frame requires the person: He warned to be careful* → He warned us to be careful. The that-clause is different: there the object may be dropped, and routinely is in news writing — Forecasters warned that the storm would stall. Also warn someone something: **She warned me the deadline* → She warned me about the deadline; and warn someone for doing: He warned me for being late* → He warned me against being late. The object rule, compared with tell: tell can never drop its personal object; warn can, but only before a that-clause. **He told that the road was closed* is wrong, while He warned that the road was closed is perfectly standard. Warning or threat: warn reports a threat when the speaker controls the harm — He warned me that he would go to the police — and an ordinary caution when they do not — He warned me that the steps were icy. Only the first belongs to this group; for the caution and advice sense see Reported commands, requests, and advice. Negative infinitive: not goes before to: warned us not to touch it, never **warned us to not touch it* in careful writing. Reports: notice of a bad consequence — the same content as threaten when the speaker will cause the harm, but framed as information rather than as a demand, which is what makes it the more menacing of the two in print.

"Do that again and I'll call your supervisor." → He warned me that he would call my supervisor. "Don't touch those wires." → The electrician warned us not to touch those wires.

  1. The landlord warned them that the next late payment would start eviction.
  2. She warned me not to sign anything before the lawyer had read it.
  3. Regulators warned that the bank was undercapitalized.
  4. He warned us about the ice on the bottom step.
  5. Her attorney warned her against speaking to reporters.
  6. I warned him twice, and after that I stopped.

See also: threaten, promise, insist, swear, advise
