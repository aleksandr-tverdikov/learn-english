# Backshift: the tense changes

> What happens to the verb when you move someone's words into a past-tense report — the full table of shifts, the one-step-back logic that generates them, and the cases where the shift never happens at all.

[← Reported speech](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

When you report what somebody said and you frame that report in the past — *she said*, *he told me*, *they explained* — the verb inside the reported clause usually moves one step further into the past. That movement is called **backshift**, and it is the single mechanical operation at the center of reported speech.

> "I **work** downtown," he said.  →  He said he **worked** downtown.

Nothing about the meaning changed. He did not stop working downtown; the sentence is not saying that his job is over. The past tense on *worked* is not doing its ordinary job of putting an event in past time — it is **agreeing** with the past tense on *said*. That is the whole idea, and it is the part most learners never get told: backshift is grammatical agreement, not a statement about when something happened.

There are thirteen or so rows in the table below, but you do not have to memorize thirteen rows. There is one rule that generates all of them, and the section [Why backshift exists](#why-backshift-exists) walks through it. Learn the rule and the table becomes something you can rebuild from scratch instead of something you have to recall.

## The master table

Every tense and future form in English, with what it becomes after a past-tense reporting verb. Verb: *work* / assorted. Read the middle column as "one step further back."

| # | Direct speech | Reported after *said* | Example pair |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | present simple — *works* | past simple — *worked* | *"I **live** in Boston."* → *She said she **lived** in Boston.* |
| 2 | present continuous — *is working* | past continuous — *was working* | *"I**'m waiting** outside."* → *He said he **was waiting** outside.* |
| 3 | present perfect — *has worked* | past perfect — *had worked* | *"I**'ve finished** the report."* → *She said she **had finished** the report.* |
| 4 | present perfect continuous — *has been working* | past perfect continuous — *had been working* | *"I**'ve been trying** to reach you."* → *He said he **had been trying** to reach me.* |
| 5 | past simple — *worked* | past perfect — *had worked* | *"I **saw** the email."* → *She said she **had seen** the email.* |
| 6 | past continuous — *was working* | past perfect continuous — *had been working* | *"I **was driving** at the time."* → *He said he **had been driving** at the time.* |
| 7 | past perfect — *had worked* | past perfect — *had worked* (no change) | *"I **had left** by then."* → *She said she **had left** by then.* |
| 8 | past perfect continuous — *had been working* | past perfect continuous (no change) | *"I **had been waiting** for an hour."* → *He said he **had been waiting** for an hour.* |
| 9 | future *will* — *will work* | *would work* | *"I**'ll call** you."* → *She said she **would call** me.* |
| 10 | *be going to* — *am/is/are going to work* | *was/were going to work* | *"I**'m going to quit**."* → *He said he **was going to quit**.* |
| 11 | future continuous — *will be working* | *would be working* | *"I**'ll be waiting** at the gate."* → *She said she **would be waiting** at the gate.* |
| 12 | future perfect — *will have worked* | *would have worked* | *"I**'ll have finished** by Friday."* → *He said he **would have finished** by Friday.* |
| 13 | future perfect continuous — *will have been working* | *would have been working* | *"By June I**'ll have been teaching** for ten years."* → *She said that by June she **would have been teaching** for ten years.* |

Two rows do nothing, and that is not an oversight — rows 7 and 8 are already as far back as English can go. See [Why the past perfect is the floor](#why-the-past-perfect-is-the-floor).

The tense-and-aspect names in the left column are laid out in full, with one example use each, in [the verbs reference](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md).

## Why backshift exists

### The reporting verb resets the clock

An ordinary sentence measures time from **now**. *I am tired* means tired at this moment; *I was tired* means tired before this moment.

A reported clause does not measure from now. It measures from **the moment the words were spoken**, and the reporting verb is what places that moment on the timeline. Once you write *she said*, you have planted a flag in the past, and everything in the clause that follows is timed against that flag rather than against the present.

So the reported clause has to answer a different question. Not "when did this happen relative to now?" but "when did this happen relative to the moment she opened her mouth?" There are only three possible answers — before, at the same time, or after — and English marks all three with tense.

### The three relations: before, at the same time, after

| Relation to the moment of speaking | What the speaker used | What the report uses | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **At the same time** — true while she was speaking | present | past | *"I**'m** cold."* → *She said she **was** cold.* |
| **Before** — already over when she spoke | past or present perfect | past perfect | *"I **called** him."* → *She said she **had called** him.* |
| **After** — still to come when she spoke | *will* / *be going to* | *would* / *was going to* | *"I**'ll** call him."* → *She said she **would** call him.* |

That is the entire system in three lines. The past tense in *she was cold* is not claiming that the coldness is over — it is claiming that the coldness lined up with the speech. The past perfect in *she had called him* is not adding some extra layer of pastness — it is saying the call came earlier than the speech. And *would* is not a conditional here; it is simply the past-tense form of *will*, doing for a past anchor exactly what *will* does for a present one.

### The one-step-back ladder

Because the anchor moved back, every form moves back with it. English tense forms sit on a short ladder, and backshift walks each form down exactly one rung:

```
        the speaker's form            the reported form
        -----------------------------------------------------
   1    will / be going to      →     would / was going to
   2    present                 →     past
   3    past                    →     past perfect
   3'   present perfect         →     past perfect
   4    past perfect            →     past perfect   (floor)
```

Rungs 3 and 3' both land on rung 4, and rung 4 cannot move. That is why the thirteen source forms in the master table produce only **nine** distinct reported forms: *worked*, *was working*, *had worked*, *had been working*, *was/were going to work*, *would work*, *would be working*, *would have worked*, *would have been working*. Three different originals — past simple, present perfect, and past perfect — all come out as *had worked*, and their three continuous counterparts all come out as *had been working*.

### Only the first verb moves

Here is the rule that makes the table unnecessary. **Backshift touches exactly one element: the finite verb — the first word of the verb phrase.** Everything after it is left completely alone. The aspect of the clause never changes: a continuous stays continuous, a perfect continuous stays perfect continuous, a passive stays passive.

One wrinkle in the wording, because it trips people up. When the finite verb is an auxiliary — *is*, *has*, *will*, *does*, *was* — the change really is one word swapped for another. When the finite verb is the lexical verb itself and it has to drop to the past perfect, the single step is spelled out as *had* + past participle: *saw* → *had seen*, *worked* → *had worked*. It is still one operation on one slot; it just takes two words to write down.

| Direct verb phrase | Finite verb | Its one-step-back form | Reported verb phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| *works* | *works* | *worked* | *worked* |
| *doesn't work* | *does* | *did* | *didn't work* |
| *is working* | *is* | *was* | *was working* |
| *has worked* | *has* | *had* | *had worked* |
| *has been working* | *has* | *had* | *had been working* |
| *worked* | *worked* | *had worked* | *had worked* |
| *was working* | *was* | *had been* | *had been working* |
| *had worked* | *had* | *had* (floor) | *had worked* |
| *will work* | *will* | *would* | *would work* |
| *is going to work* | *is* | *was* | *was going to work* |
| *will be working* | *will* | *would* | *would be working* |
| *will have worked* | *will* | *would* | *would have worked* |
| *is being reviewed* | *is* | *was* | *was being reviewed* |

Watch it operate on a long verb phrase. In *"I **have been** waiting since noon,"* the finite verb is *have*; it drops to *had*; *been waiting since noon* is untouched:

> "I **have been** waiting since noon," she said.  →  She said she **had been** waiting since noon.

And on a passive, where the finite verb is buried in front of two other verb forms:

> "The apartment **is being** repainted," he said.  →  He said the apartment **was being** repainted.

Learners who try to backshift the whole phrase produce things like ✗ *had been worked* for *has been working*. Move the finite verb; leave the rest.

### Why the past perfect is the floor

English has one past, and one past-of-the-past. It has no past-of-the-past-of-the-past — there is no ✗ *had had left*, no ✗ *had had been waiting*. The ladder runs out.

Two consequences follow, and both are practical.

**First**, the past perfect stays put. When the speaker's own words were already in the past perfect, the report reuses the same form:

> "I **had already eaten** when they called," he said.  →  He said he **had already eaten** when they called.

**Second**, the report loses information. Past simple and present perfect both collapse into the past perfect, so a reported past perfect is genuinely ambiguous — three different originals produce the same report:

| The original words | The report |
|---|---|
| *"I **ate**."* | *He said he **had eaten**.* |
| *"I**'ve eaten**."* | *He said he **had eaten**.* |
| *"I**'d eaten**."* | *He said he **had eaten**.* |

If the difference matters, a reporter does not fix it with tense — tense cannot fix it. The reporter adds an adverb or restructures: *He said he'd eaten **earlier*** points at the plain past, *He said he'd **already** eaten **by then*** points at the past perfect, and *He said he **wasn't hungry** because he'd eaten* sidesteps the whole problem.

### The floor is also why past simple often stays put

Because the past-simple-to-past-perfect step buys so little — the ambiguity above means the reader cannot tell which original it came from anyway — American speakers frequently skip it. *She said she saw him at the game* is completely standard English, not a mistake or a shortcut. This is treated as an option, not an error, in [Backshift is available, not compulsory](#backshift-is-available-not-compulsory).

## The changes one by one

Each subsection gives the rule, at least one transformation pair, and any real-usage note attached to that row.

### Present simple → past simple

The most common shift in the language. The present simple describes what was true while the person was speaking, so in a past-framed report it becomes the past simple.

> "I **live** in Boston," she said.  →  She said she **lived** in Boston.

> "The store **opens** at nine," he told me.  →  He told me the store **opened** at nine.

Negatives carry the shift on the *do*-auxiliary, since that is the finite verb:

> "I **don't know** her," he said.  →  He said he **didn't know** her.

Questions behave differently, and it is worth flagging here so the pattern is not over-generalized. A reported question loses its *do*-support altogether rather than shifting it, so the past tense lands on the lexical verb:

> "**Do** you **have** a minute?" she asked.  →  She asked whether I **had** a minute. (Not ✗ *whether I did have*.)

The verb *be* has its own past forms and takes them: *am* and *is* become *was*, *are* becomes *were*.

> "I **am** exhausted," she said.  →  She said she **was** exhausted.

> "They **are** on their way," he said.  →  He said they **were** on their way.

*There is* and *there are* behave the same way, since *is* and *are* are the finite verbs:

> "**There's** a problem with the invoice," she said.  →  She said **there was** a problem with the invoice.

### Present continuous → past continuous

The finite verb is the form of *be*; the `-ing` participle never changes.

> "I**'m looking** for my keys," he said.  →  He said he **was looking** for his keys.

> "The kids **are playing** outside," she said.  →  She said the kids **were playing** outside.

The present continuous also expresses arranged future plans, and it backshifts identically in that use — the plan simply becomes a plan reported from a past vantage point:

> "We**'re flying** to Denver on Thursday," he said.  →  He said they **were flying** to Denver on Thursday.

### Present perfect → past perfect

*Have* / *has* becomes *had*. The past participle is untouched.

> "I**'ve finished** the report," she said.  →  She said she **had finished** the report.

> "He **hasn't called** me back," she said.  →  She said he **hadn't called** her back.

American participles ride through unchanged, including the ones that differ from British English. *Get* has two participles in American usage, *got* and *gotten*, and *gotten* — the one meaning "obtained" or "became" — is standard in the United States and rare in Britain. Backshift does not touch it:

> "I**'ve gotten** used to the commute," she said.  →  She said she **had gotten** used to the commute.

> "We**'ve gotten** three offers," he said.  →  He said they **had gotten** three offers.

A related American habit shows up on the way in rather than on the way out. With *just*, *already*, and *yet*, American English allows the simple past where British English wants the present perfect — *Did you eat yet?* alongside *Have you eaten yet?* Both originals are reportable, and they do not land in quite the same place:

| The original words | The report |
|---|---|
| *"I**'ve already eaten**."* (both dialects) | *He said he **had already eaten**.* |
| *"I **already ate**."* (American) | *He said he **had already eaten**.* — or, just as normally, *He said he **already ate**.* |

Contraction warning: *he'd* is ambiguous in writing between *he had* and *he would*. In a reported clause it is almost always *had* when a past participle follows (*he'd finished*) and *would* when a bare infinitive follows (*he'd finish*). The following word tells you which.

### Present perfect continuous → past perfect continuous

Again only *have* / *has* moves.

> "I**'ve been trying** to reach you all morning," he said.  →  He said he **had been trying** to reach me all morning.

> "She**'s been living** in Austin since 2021," he said.  →  He said she **had been living** in Austin since 2021.

A *since* or *for* phrase built on an absolute reference — *since 2021*, *for ten years*, *since the merger* — stays exactly as it is. It measures a stretch of time from a fixed point, the fixed point did not move, and the phrase still lines up under the new anchor.

A *since* phrase built on a deictic word is a different matter, and it gets re-anchored like any other pointing expression: *"I've been up since **this morning**," he said* → *He said he had been up since **that morning**.*

### Past simple → past perfect

The step down to the floor.

> "I **saw** the email," she said.  →  She said she **had seen** the email.

> "We **moved** here in 2019," he said.  →  He said they **had moved** there in 2019.

> "I **didn't hear** the alarm," she said.  →  She said she **hadn't heard** the alarm.

**This is the row that real American speech breaks most often.** Textbooks present past-to-past-perfect as compulsory; ordinary speakers and professional writers leave the past simple in place all the time, and nobody hears it as wrong:

> "I **saw** the email," she said.  →  She said she **saw** the email.

Both versions are standard. The past perfect earns its keep when the order of two past events actually matters — *He said he had already left when the fire started* is clearer than *He said he already left when the fire started* — and it is strongly preferred after *by then*, *before that*, *until then*, and similar ordering adverbs, and in edited prose generally. Elsewhere it is a stylistic choice, and the plain past is the more natural one in speech. The honest summary: in conversation, keep the past simple unless dropping the past perfect leaves the order of events unclear; in formal writing, shift. [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md) has the situation-by-situation breakdown.

The past simple also stays put when a time expression already fixes the event on the calendar, and with events that cannot be re-ordered against anything. *She said she was born in Ohio* is what everyone says; *She said she had been born in Ohio* is grammatical but fussy, because a birth has no competing past event to be sequenced against. The same goes for *He said he grew up in Tulsa* and *She said she went to Michigan State*.

### Past continuous → past perfect continuous

> "I **was driving** when it happened," he said.  →  He said he **had been driving** when it happened.

> "They **were arguing** about money," she said.  →  She said they **had been arguing** about money.

This row is broken even more often than the last one. The past perfect continuous is a heavy form, and in speech the past continuous simply stays:

> "I **was driving** when it happened," he said.  →  He said he **was driving** when it happened.

That is what most Americans actually say, and it is not an error. Use the past perfect continuous when you need to make clear that the activity preceded some other past event; otherwise leave it.

### Past perfect → past perfect (no change)

Nothing to do. The form is already at the bottom of the ladder.

> "I **had finished** by the time she got there," he said.  →  He said he **had finished** by the time she got there.

> "They **hadn't told** anyone," she said.  →  She said they **hadn't told** anyone.

### Past perfect continuous → past perfect continuous (no change)

Same reasoning.

> "I **had been waiting** for almost an hour," she said.  →  She said she **had been waiting** for almost an hour.

### Future *will* → *would*

*Would* is the past-tense form of *will*, in exactly the sense that *did* is the past-tense form of *do*. It is not a conditional here and carries no hint of unreality.

> "I**'ll call** you tomorrow," she said.  →  She said she **would** call me **the next day**.

> "It **won't take** long," he said.  →  He said it **wouldn't take** long.

> "The package **will arrive** Monday," she said.  →  She said the package **would arrive** Monday.

Some reporting verbs already carry the future inside their meaning, and after those the *would* often disappears into an infinitive instead. Compare *He said he would pay me back* with *He* [*promised*](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise) *to pay me back* — same content, no *would* needed.

*Shall* needs a paragraph of its own, because textbooks written in Britain pair it with *should* and present that pairing as the rule. In everyday American English *shall* has two survivals and nothing else: legal and regulatory prose (*the tenant shall provide written notice*), and first-person offers and suggestions (*Shall I get the door?*, *Shall we?*). Plain future *shall* is not something Americans say, but you will meet it in older writing, and it does not backshift to *should*.

- **Future *shall* → *would*.** *"I **shall** return," he said.* → *He said he **would** return.*
- **Offer or suggestion *shall* → *should*.** *"**Shall** I wait?" he asked.* → *He asked whether he **should** wait.*

Reporting the first kind with *should* is the classic British-textbook slip, and it is a meaning change as well as a dialect one: *He said he should return* reads as advice or obligation, not as a prediction.

### *Be going to* → *was/were going to*

*Be going to* is not a modal — it is the verb *be* plus a fixed phrase — so the shift lands on *be*.

> "I**'m going to quit**," he said.  →  He said he **was going to quit**.

> "They**'re going to raise** the rent," she said.  →  She said they **were going to raise** the rent.

American speakers also freely substitute *would* here, and the two are close to interchangeable in reports:

> "I**'m going to call** the landlord," she said.  →  She said she **would call** the landlord.

There is a small difference worth knowing. *Was going to* leans toward a plan that may not have come off — *He said he was going to fix it* invites the follow-up *but he never did* — while *would* is neutral. If you want to imply the plan failed, *was going to* is the form that carries it.

### Future continuous → *would be* + `-ing`

Only *will* moves; *be* + `-ing` rides along unchanged.

> "I**'ll be waiting** at the gate," she said.  →  She said she **would be waiting** at the gate.

> "This time next week we**'ll be sitting** on a beach," he said.  →  He said that this time **the following week** they **would be sitting** on a beach.

### Future perfect → *would have* + past participle

> "I**'ll have finished** by Friday," he said.  →  He said he **would have finished** by Friday.

> "By December they**'ll have paid off** the loan," she said.  →  She said that by December they **would have paid off** the loan.

Be aware of a real ambiguity here: *would have finished* is also the third-conditional form (*I would have finished if you hadn't interrupted*), so a reader can briefly take a reported future perfect as an unreal past. Writers who care about this recast the sentence — *He said he'd be done by Friday*, or *He said the report would be finished by Friday* — and in speech the future perfect is uncommon enough that the problem rarely comes up.

### Future perfect continuous → *would have been* + `-ing`

The rarest row in the table, and it behaves like all the others: *will* moves, everything after it stays.

> "By June I**'ll have been teaching** for ten years," she said.  →  She said that by June she **would have been teaching** for ten years.

### When the reporting verb absorbs the shift

The whole tense question can sometimes be sidestepped rather than answered. A reporting verb that names the speech act absorbs the content of the shift, and that is often the cleanest repair when the fully backshifted version reads badly.

| The words | Backshifted report | Report with a speech-act verb |
|---|---|---|
| *"I'll pay you back."* | *He said he **would** pay me back.* | *He* [*promised*](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise) *to pay me back.* |
| *"I won't sign it."* | *She said she **wouldn't** sign it.* | *She* [*refused*](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#refuse) *to sign it.* |
| *"I'm sorry I forgot."* | *He said he **was** sorry he **had forgotten**.* | *He **apologized** for forgetting.* |
| *"I can give you a ride."* | *She said she **could** give me a ride.* | *She **offered** me a ride.* |
| *"When does the store open?"* | *She asked when the store **opened**.* | *She **inquired** about the store's hours.* |

Two of those verbs are spelling traps, and in both the British form is often printed as though it were neutral:

- **apologize**, not *apologise*. The *-ize* spelling is the American standard across this whole family — *apologize*, *criticize*, *emphasize*, *organize*, *realize*, *recognize*. British publishers are split between *-ize* and *-ise*; American ones are not. So: *He apologized for forgetting*. The noun is *apology* in both dialects.
- **inquire**, not *enquire*. American English uses *inquire* and *inquiry* for both senses — asking an ordinary question and conducting a formal investigation. The *enquire* / *inquire* split, in which *enquire* means "ask" and *inquire* means "investigate," is British. An American report reads *She inquired whether the room was still available*, never *enquired*.

Neither verb changes anything about backshift itself. When a *that*-clause or a wh-clause follows one of them, it shifts exactly as it would after *said*: *She inquired whether the room **was** still available*; *He apologized and said he **had** forgotten*.

## Modals: the short version

Modal verbs have their own section — [Modal verbs in reported speech](07-modals.md), which covers the semi-modals, the negatives, and the three meanings of *must* — but since they share the ladder, here is the summary so the table above is not left with a hole in it.

| Direct | Reported | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | *would* | *"I **will** help."* → *He said he **would** help.* |
| *can* | *could* | *"I **can** drive."* → *She said she **could** drive.* |
| *may* (possibility) | *might* | *"It **may** rain."* → *He said it **might** rain.* |
| *may* (permission) | *could* / *might* | *"You **may** leave."* → *She said I **could** leave.* |
| *must* (obligation) | *had to* | *"You **must** sign here."* → *He said I **had to** sign there.* |
| *must* (deduction) | *must* (no change) | *"He **must** be joking."* → *She said he **must** be joking.* |
| *have to* / *have got to* | *had to* | *"I**'ve got to** run."* → *She said she **had to** run.* |
| *don't have to* | *didn't have to* | *"You **don't have to** wait."* → *He said I **didn't have to** wait.* |
| *need to* | *needed to* | *"I **need to** get gas."* → *He said he **needed to** get gas.* |
| *shall* (future — rare in AmE) | *would* | *"I **shall** return."* → *He said he **would** return.* |
| *shall* (offer or suggestion) | *should* | *"**Shall** I help?"* → *He asked if he **should** help.* |

The modals that have nowhere left to go never change. Some of them are already past forms — **would, could, should, might**. Others are not past forms at all; they are simply invariant, with no inflection available: **ought to, used to, had better, would rather, needn't, might as well**, plus **must** in its deduction sense and prohibitive **mustn't**. Either way the result is identical, because the ladder has no rung underneath them.

> "I **would** help if I could," he said.  →  He said he **would** help if he could.

> "You**'d better** leave now," she said.  →  She said I**'d better** leave then.

> "I **used to** smoke," he said.  →  He said he **used to** smoke.

A modal followed by *have* + past participle is likewise finished, since the perfect infinitive is already the past of the past:

> "I **should have** taken the earlier flight," he said.  →  He said he **should have** taken the earlier flight.

> "She **might have** missed the exit," he said.  →  He said she **might have** missed the exit.

The single most common modal error in reported speech is turning *would* into *would have*. *Would* is already at the floor; pushing it further changes the meaning from "future in the past" to "unreal past," which is not what the speaker said.

## The reporting verb decides: *said* vs. *says* vs. *has said*

### The rule

**Backshift is triggered by the tense of the reporting verb, and by nothing else.** A past-tense reporting verb makes backshift available. A present-tense or future reporting verb does not — there is no past anchor to agree with, so the reported clause keeps the tense the speaker used.

| Reporting verb | Its time | Backshift? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *says*, *tells me* | present | **No** | *She says she **is** tired.* |
| *is saying*, *is telling us* | present | **No** | *He's saying he **wants** out.* |
| *has said*, *has told me* | present perfect | **No** | *He has said he **will** run again.* |
| *will say*, *is going to say* | future | **No** | *She'll say she **doesn't** remember.* |
| *said*, *told me* | past | **Yes**, by default | *She said she **was** tired.* |
| *was saying* | past | Optional | *She was saying she **wants** / **wanted** out.* |
| *had said*, *had told me* | past perfect | **Yes** | *He had said he **would** be late.* |

Everything in this file up to this point assumed the *said* row. The rest of the table is the reason the assumption has to be stated.

### *Says*: no backshift, ever

> "I**'m** tired," she said.  →  She **says** she **is** tired.

> "I **can't** make it," he said.  →  He **says** he **can't** make it.

> "We **have** three left," they said.  →  They **say** they **have** three left.

American English puts *says* to work in four distinct places, and none of them backshifts.

**Reporting something still current.** The speech may have happened an hour ago, but you are treating the content as live: *The doctor says I need to rest.*

**Reporting a repeated or standing claim.** *He says he's quitting* implies he says it regularly. *He said he's quitting* is a single occasion.

**Reporting what a text says.** Documents speak in the present tense indefinitely: *The label says it contains nuts. The lease says we can't sublet. The sign says no parking after six.*

**Narrating in the vivid present.** Conversational American storytelling switches into the present for immediacy, and *says* — often *goes* or *is like* in casual speech — comes with it: *So I call her back, and she says she never got the message.*

### *Has said*, *will say*, *is saying*

All three are anchored at or after the present, so all three leave the reported clause alone.

> "I**'ll** run again," he said.  →  He **has said** he **will** run again.

> "I **don't** remember," she said.  →  She **will say** she **doesn't** remember.

> "I **want** out," he said.  →  He**'s saying** he **wants** out.

*Has said* is the workhorse of news writing and of any context where the statement is on the record and still stands. Note the difference it makes: *He said he would run again* reports one past moment; *He has said he will run again* reports a position that is currently held.

### The same words, two reports

The choice between *says* and *said* is not a fact about the original utterance. It is a decision the reporter makes about how to frame it, and the identical sentence supports both:

| Framing | Report |
|---|---|
| The speech as a past event | *"I'm not coming," she said.* → *She **said** she **wasn't** coming.* |
| The content as currently true | *"I'm not coming," she said.* → *She **says** she**'s not** coming.* |

Both are correct reports of the same words. The first tells you when she spoke; the second tells you where things stand. Journalists exploit this deliberately — a headline uses *says* to make the claim feel current (`Mayor Says Budget Is Balanced`) while the body copy switches to *said* to timestamp it (`the mayor said Tuesday that the budget was balanced`).

### *Was saying* and *had said*

*Was saying* is a past form, so backshift is technically available, but the continuous aspect frames the speech as recent and ongoing, and speakers usually keep the original tense: *She was saying she wants to move* is at least as common as *wanted*.

*Had said* is a past perfect reporting verb — used when the speech happened before some other past event you have already mentioned. The reported clause backshifts normally, and since the clause verb cannot go below the past perfect either, long chains flatten out:

> "I **finished** it," he said.  →  By the time I got there, he **had said** he **had finished** it.

### Reports inside reports

When one report is embedded in another, backshift does not compound. Each clause walks down to the floor and stops there, so a chain three deep comes out looking like a chain two deep:

> "Dana told me she **needs** the file," he said.  →  He said Dana **had told** him she **needed** the file.

> "Dana told me she **needed** the file," he said.  →  He said Dana **had told** him she **needed** the file.

The second pair is the practical point. The fully mechanical version — *he said Dana had told him she had needed the file* — is defensible, and nobody writes it. Shift the outermost reporting verb, shift the next clause when it clarifies the order of events, and leave the rest alone. A sentence built out of stacked *hads* is a sign the rule is being applied rather than used.

## Backshift is available, not compulsory

The textbook presentation says backshift after *said* is a rule. In real American English it is a **strong default with a real exception zone**. Two corrections to the textbook are needed, and they pull in opposite directions:

- **Backshift is not compulsory.** When the content is still true, still future, or generally true, leaving the tense alone is standard English and not a mistake.
- **Backshift is nonetheless the American default.** British-tradition materials often present the unshifted form as the natural choice for a still-true fact. That is not what American speech or American editing does. *He said he lived in Denver* implies nothing whatever about his having moved, and it is the version most Americans produce most of the time.

So the unshifted form is a choice with a job, not a relaxation of the rule. It puts the reporter's own weight behind the claim and asserts that the claim holds right now.

**Still-true states.**

> "I**'m** allergic to shellfish," she said.  →  She said she **is** allergic to shellfish.

> "I**'m** allergic to shellfish," she said.  →  She said she **was** allergic to shellfish.

Both are correct reports of the same words, and the second does not suggest the allergy ended. The unshifted version is the one to reach for when the currency of the fact is the point — when you are warning the caterer, for instance.

There is one hard constraint on the unshifted form, and it is worth stating as a rule: **it commits you.** If the fact has stopped being true between the speaking and the reporting, the unshifted version is not merely unidiomatic but false. *He said he works at Boeing* is wrong if he was laid off last month; *He said he worked at Boeing* stays silent about the present and is always safe. That asymmetry is the reason backshift is the default.

**Still-future events.**

> "I**'ll be** there at six," he said.  →  He said he**'ll be** there at six.

If six o'clock has not happened yet, *he'll be* is the natural report. *He said he would be there at six* is equally correct and is the only option once six o'clock has passed.

**General truths.**

> "Water **boils** at 212 degrees," the teacher said.  →  The teacher said water **boils** at 212 degrees.

Many textbooks state that general truths *must not* backshift. That is not true — *The teacher said water boiled at 212 degrees* is perfectly grammatical and does not suggest the physics has changed. Both work, and the choice carries a faint difference of stance: the unshifted version reads as the reporter endorsing the claim, the shifted one as merely attributing it. That difference is real and usable: *He said the earth **is** round* sounds like agreement, while *He said the earth **was** flat* keeps the reporter at arm's length.

**When you disagree with the content, backshift.** The unshifted version tends to signal that the reporter accepts the statement. Compare *She said she's a doctor* with *She said she was a doctor* — in context, the second can carry a faint note of doubt.

The full treatment of when backshift is skipped, blocked, or optional is in [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md), which sorts the cases into three groups that behave differently: the impossible, the untriggered, and the genuinely optional.

## What backshift never touches

Some things in a reported clause look like verbs the rule should apply to, and it does not.

**Non-finite forms.** Infinitives, gerunds, and participles have no tense to shift.

> "I want **to leave** early," she said.  →  She said she wanted **to leave** early.

> "I hate **waiting**," he said.  →  He said he hated **waiting**.

**Unreal past.** The past tense in second and third conditionals, in *wish* clauses, in *if only*, and after *it's time* and *would rather* is already doing non-past work. It cannot be shifted again without changing the meaning.

> "If I **had** more time, I**'d** help," he said.  →  He said if he **had** more time he **would** help.

> "I **wish** I **knew** the answer," she said.  →  She said she **wished** she **knew** the answer.

Look carefully at what moved in the second example. *Wish* is not the reporting verb — *said* is. *Wish* is an ordinary present-tense verb sitting inside the reported clause, so it backshifts like any other present tense: *wish* → *wished*. The *knew* in its complement is unreal past rather than real past, and it stays exactly where it is. Two verbs in one clause, and only one of them moves.

**Third conditionals** are already at the floor throughout and come across intact:

> "If I **had known**, I **would have called**," he said.  →  He said that if he **had known** he **would have called**.

**Clauses under an unshifted main verb.** Backshift is all-or-nothing across the report: if the reporting verb never went into the past, nothing inside moves, however many clauses deep it goes.

> "I**'ll** let you know when I **get** there," she said.  →  She **says** she**'ll** let me know when she **gets** there.

The complementary case is not an exception at all, and it belongs here so the pattern above is not over-read. When the main clause *does* shift, the subordinate clauses shift with it — including the present tense that a future time clause requires:

> "I**'ll** call you when I **get** home," he said.  →  He said he **would** call me when he **got** home.

The *get* in *when I get home* was never a real present; it is the tenseless-future present that *when*, *after*, *as soon as*, *before*, and *until* demand in place of *will*. It still moves, because it takes its tense from the clause it hangs on.

## The subjunctive: no tense, nothing to shift

English has a surviving subjunctive, it is considerably more alive in American English than in British English, and it is invisible to backshift. It carries no tense, so there is nothing for a past reporting verb to agree with.

### The mandative subjunctive

After a verb, adjective, or noun of demanding, requiring, urging, or recommending, the verb in the *that*-clause is the **bare base form** — no *-s*, no *to*, no agreement, no tense. It looks identical in the direct version and in the report:

> "I **insist** that he **be** present," she said.  →  She **insisted** that he **be** present.

> "I **recommend** that she **apply** for the grant," he said.  →  He **recommended** that she **apply** for the grant.

> "We **demand** that the fee **be** waived," they said.  →  They **demanded** that the fee **be** waived.

> "It**'s** essential that every member **vote**," he said.  →  He said it **was** essential that every member **vote**.

Watch which verbs moved across those four pairs. *Insist*, *recommend*, *demand*, and *is* are real tensed verbs, and they backshift normally. *Be*, *apply*, and *vote* are subjunctives, and they do not, because English has no past subjunctive for them to shift into.

The negative is a bare *not* in front of the verb, with no *do*-support, and it is equally frozen:

> "I **recommend** that she **not sign** it," the lawyer said.  →  The lawyer **recommended** that she **not sign** it.

Not ✗ *recommended that she doesn't sign* and not ✗ *recommended that she didn't sign*. The passive works the same way, on *be*: *He demanded that the report **be** filed by Friday.*

### American bare form, British *should*

This is the single place in reported speech where a British form is most often handed to American learners as the default.

| | American English | British English |
|---|---|---|
| Form in the *that*-clause | bare subjunctive | *should* + base form |
| Direct | *"I insist that he **be** present."* | *"I insist that he **should be** present."* |
| Reported | *She insisted that he **be** present.* | *She insisted that he **should be** present.* |
| Backshift | none available — the form is tenseless | none available — *should* is already at the floor |

Both are grammatical English; only one is American. Write *She insisted that he be present*. The *should* version is not an error, but in American prose it reads as an import, and it invites a second reading in which the speaker was giving advice rather than issuing a demand.

The rule shows itself in exactly one place — the third-person singular, because that is the only form in which the subjunctive and the indicative are spelled differently:

> ✗ *He insisted that she **goes** to the doctor.*  →  ✓ *He insisted that she **go** to the doctor.*

Everywhere else the bare form and the indicative are spelled the same, which is why learners often get this right by accident and then trip on exactly this one case.

### *Insist*, *suggest*, and *demand* have two meanings each

The same verb can introduce a subjunctive (a demand) or an ordinary indicative clause (an assertion). Only the second backshifts, and picking the wrong one changes what the speaker was doing.

| Sense | Direct | Reported | What shifted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | *"I insist that he **be** present."* | *She insisted that he **be** present.* | *insist* only |
| Assertion | *"I insist that I **paid** the bill."* | *She insisted that she **had paid** the bill.* | both verbs |
| Proposal | *"I suggest that we **wait**."* | *He suggested that we **wait**.* | *suggest* only |
| Claim | *"I suggest that you **are** hiding something."* | *He suggested that I **was** hiding something.* | both verbs |

The full pattern — its triggers, its passive, and the verbs that take it — is in [Reported commands, requests, and advice](06-commands-requests.md#the-mandative-subjunctive), and the tenseless-form argument is worked out in [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md#8-the-subjunctive-does-not-shift).

### The *were* subjunctive and the frozen ones

*Were* after *if*, *wish*, and *as though* is unreal past, already outside the tense system, and it survives reporting intact:

> "If I **were** taller, I**'d** play center," he said.  →  He said that if he **were** taller he **would** play center.

Americans also say *if I was taller* in casual speech, and reporting does not adjudicate that choice — whichever form the speaker used is the form the report keeps.

Frozen formulas do not shift either: *"So be it," he said* → *He said **so be it***. These sit at the boundary where reporting stops working; if a formulaic subjunctive sounds strange inside a *that*-clause, quote it instead of reporting it.

## The other things that change with it

Backshift is one of three simultaneous adjustments. Moving the anchor into the past also moves the person and the place-and-time deixis, and the transformation pairs throughout this file show all three happening at once.

| Dimension | Example of the change |
|---|---|
| Tense (this section) | *am* → *was*, *will* → *would* |
| Person | *I* → *he/she*, *you* → *me*, *my* → *his/her* |
| Time and place | *tomorrow* → *the next day*, *here* → *there*, *now* → *then*, *this* → *that* |

All three in one sentence:

> "I**'ll** meet you **here tomorrow**," she said.  →  She said she **would** meet me **there the next day**.

The person, time, and place shifts have their own section: [Pronouns, time, and place shifts](04-pronouns-time-place.md), which works through the recalculations in detail — including the cases where *here* stays *here* and *today* stays *today* because you are reporting from the same room on the same day. Three structural notes that also interact with backshift:

**Questions** backshift exactly as statements do, but they additionally lose their question word order and their *do*-support: *"Where **do** you **live**?" she asked* → *She asked where I **lived**.* The rest of that transformation is in [Reported questions](05-questions.md).

**Commands** do not backshift at all, because the imperative has no tense. They become infinitives: *"**Wait** here," he said* → *He told me **to wait** there.* See [Reported commands, requests, and advice](06-commands-requests.md).

**The frame around all of it** — the punctuation of direct speech, the shape of the reporting clause, the *that*-clause, and the *say* / *tell* / *ask* split — is in [Direct and indirect speech: the basics](01-basics.md).

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *He said he had **went** home.* | *He said he had **gone** home.* | The past perfect takes the past participle, not the past simple. Backshift does not change which participle the verb uses. |
| *She said she **had had had** enough.* | *She said she **had had** enough.* | The floor is one *had* + the participle. *Have* as a lexical verb gives *had had*, and that is as deep as it gets. |
| *He said he **would have** help me.* | *He said he **would** help me.* | *Would* is already the backshifted form of *will*. Pushing it to *would have* turns a future-in-the-past into an unreal past. |
| *She said she **will** be late, but she wasn't.* | *She said she **would** be late, but she wasn't.* | Once the event is settled and past, the unshifted *will* clashes with the rest of the sentence. |
| *He said he **is** tired **yesterday**.* | *He said he **was** tired yesterday.* | A past time expression forces the past tense; you cannot mix an unshifted verb with a past adverbial. |
| *She said she **has been** working there for years.* | *She said she **had been** working there for years.* | Only *has* moves — but it does have to move if you are backshifting at all. Half-shifting is the error. |
| *He said he **had been worked** all night.* | *He said he **had been working** all night.* | Backshifting the whole phrase instead of the first word turns an active continuous into a passive. |
| *They said they **musted** leave.* | *They said they **had to** leave.* | Modals have no past-tense inflection. Obligation *must* borrows *had to*. |
| *She asked where **did I live**.* | *She asked where **I lived**.* | The reported question keeps statement word order and drops *do*. |
| *He said me he was busy.* | *He **told** me he was busy. / He **said** he was busy.* | *Say* does not take an indirect object without *to*; *tell* requires one. |
| *She insisted that he **was** present.* *(meaning: she demanded it)* | *She insisted that he **be** present.* | The mandative subjunctive is tenseless, so a past reporting verb leaves it alone. The indicative version says she claimed he was there — a different sentence. |
| *He insisted that she **should be** present.* | *He insisted that she **be** present.* | *Should* in a mandative clause is the British pattern. American English uses the bare subjunctive. |
| *He recommended that she **didn't sign** it.* | *He recommended that she **not sign** it.* | The negative subjunctive is bare *not*, with no *do*-support and no backshift. |
| *He said he **should** return.* *(from "I shall return")* | *He said he **would** return.* | Future *shall* backshifts to *would*. The *shall* / *should* pairing is British, and it turns a prediction into advice. |
| *She **enquired** whether the room was available.* | *She **inquired** whether the room was available.* | *Inquire* is the American spelling in both senses. *Enquire* is British. |
| *He said he **was** tired and he **can't** finish.* | *He said he **was** tired and **couldn't** finish.* | Once you commit to backshifting a report, every tensed verb in it shifts. Mixing a shifted and an unshifted verb inside one report is the most common inconsistency there is. |
| *Last week she said she **would** call me **tomorrow**.* | *Last week she said she **would** call me **the next day**.* | The tense and the time word are one decision. A backshifted verb needs a re-anchored time expression to match it. |

## Quick reference

**Derive any backshift in five steps.**

1. **Look at the reporting verb.** Present, present perfect, or future — stop, change nothing. Past — continue.
2. **Find the finite verb** in the reported clause: the first word of the verb phrase, whether that is an auxiliary (*is, has, will, does, was*) or the lexical verb itself (*works, saw*).
3. **Check that it has a tense to move.** A subjunctive (*that he be present*), an unreal past (*if I were*, *I wish I knew*), an infinitive, a gerund, or an imperative has none. Stop.
4. **Move that one verb down one rung** — *will* → *would*, present → past, past or present perfect → past perfect, past perfect → stays. Leave every other word in the phrase alone.
5. **Re-anchor the pronouns and the time and place words to match.** Tense, person, and deixis are one decision, not three.

**The ladder, compressed.**

| Rung | Forms on it | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| Future | *will*, *be going to* | *would*, *was/were going to* |
| Present | *works*, *is working*, *has worked*, *has been working*, *can*, *may*, *must* | *worked*, *was working*, *had worked*, *had been working*, *could*, *might*, *had to* |
| Past | *worked*, *was working* | *had worked*, *had been working* |
| Floor | *had worked*, *had been working*, *would*, *could*, *should*, *might*, *ought to*, *used to*, *had better*, *should have taken* | no change |
| Off the ladder | subjunctive *be* / *apply*, unreal past (*if I were*, *I wish I knew*), infinitives, gerunds, imperatives | no change — no tense to move |

**The three sentences to remember.**

> "I **am** here." → He said he **was** there. (same time as the speech)

> "I **was** here." → He said he **had been** there. (before the speech)

> "I **will be** here." → He said he **would be** there. (after the speech)
