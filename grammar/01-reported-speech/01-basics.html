Direct and indirect speech: the basics#

How to quote someone's exact words and how to convert them into your own — the punctuation of direct speech, the shape of the reporting clause, the that-clause, the say/tell/ask/speak/talk split, and the three things that change in the conversion.

← Reported speech · All grammar topics

There are exactly two ways to put someone else's words into your sentence. You can hand them over untouched, wrapped in quotation marks, exactly as they came out of the speaker's mouth — that is direct speech. Or you can absorb them into your own sentence, rebuilt around your own point of view and your own moment in time — that is indirect speech, more often called reported speech.

Direct: Maria said, "I'll call you tomorrow." Reported: Maria said she would call me the next day.

Everything in this file is about the machinery connecting those two lines. The bolded parts show what the conversion touched — the pronoun, the verb, the time word — which is the whole of reported speech in one example: three kinds of change, applied to a message that otherwise stays exactly what it was.

Learners usually meet reported speech as a list of tense-change rules to memorize. That is the smallest part of it. The larger part is punctuation, the shape of the reporting clause, and knowing which verb takes which complement, because those are the places where a sentence goes wrong in a way an American reader notices immediately. He told that he was late is not a stylistic slip; it is broken. This file covers the whole frame; Backshift covers the tense rules in detail.

1. The two systems#

1.1 Direct speech#

Direct speech reproduces the words as spoken, inside quotation marks, with a reporting clause (also called a speech tag, dialogue tag, or attribution) naming the speaker. Nothing inside the quotation marks belongs to you — the tenses, the pronouns, and the time words all belong to the original speaker, standing at the original moment.

  • Daniel said, "I'm not finishing this tonight."
  • "Where did you park?" she asked.
  • "Don't touch that," the electrician warned us.

Because the words are the speaker's, direct speech can carry things reported speech cannot: interjections, vocatives, hesitations, fragments, dialect spelling, and the rhythm of an actual voice — "Ugh, fine, whatever," he muttered. "Well — I mean — I guess so," she said.

1.2 Indirect (reported) speech#

Reported speech folds the message into your sentence. The quotation marks come off, the message becomes a subordinate clause, and everything that pointed outward from the original speaker is repointed to you. Those same three lines, reported:

  • Daniel said he wasn't finishing it that night.
  • She asked where I had parked.
  • The electrician warned us not to touch it.

You are no longer promising that these were the words; you are promising that this was the content. That difference is legal, journalistic, and moral as well as grammatical — putting words in quotation marks is a claim that they were actually said that way.

1.4 A third option: free indirect speech#

Between the two sits a literary style that drops the quotation marks and the reporting clause but keeps the flavor of the character's voice.

Direct: He looked at the clock. "I'm never going to finish this." Free indirect: He looked at the clock. He was never going to finish this.

The tense and pronoun shifted as in reported speech, but there is no he thought and no that — the narration slides into his head. Notice what did not shift: this. Free indirect speech characteristically keeps the character's own this, here, and now while the verb goes into the past, and that mismatch — a proximal pointing word sitting next to a past tense — is its signature: He was never going to finish this. Not now, not in this office. Novels run on it; ordinary writing does not use it. The full treatment is in Special cases.

2. Punctuating direct speech in American English#

This is where most of the visible errors live. American conventions differ from British ones on two specific points — the shape of the quotation marks and the position of the period and comma — and mixing the two systems in one document is the single most common punctuation fault in student writing.

2.1 The basic frame#

A reporting clause, a comma, an opening quotation mark, a capital letter, the sentence, its terminal punctuation, and a closing quotation mark.

  • She said, "The store closes at nine."
  • He asked, "Are you free on Thursday?"
  • The coach shouted, "Run!"

Three rules are doing the work: the comma goes before the opening quotation mark, the quoted sentence is capitalized even though it sits in the middle of your sentence, and the terminal mark goes inside the closing quotation mark. A colon may replace the comma when the quotation is long, formal, or deliberately weighty — standard in journalism and academic prose: The report closed with a single sentence: "None of this was unforeseeable."

2.2 When the quotation comes first#

The quoted sentence's period becomes a comma, still inside the quotation marks, and the reporting clause that follows starts with a lowercase letter — it is not a new sentence.

  • "The store closes at nine," she said.
  • "I already sent it," Daniel said, "twice."

The lowercase is not optional: "The store closes at nine," She said. is an error.

2.3 Questions and exclamations inside the quotation#

The question mark or exclamation point replaces the comma. It stays inside the quotation marks, and no comma is added — never stack two marks.

Direct speechCorrectWrong
Question, tag after"Where are my keys?" he asked."Where are my keys?," he asked.
Exclamation, tag after"Watch out!" she yelled."Watch out!," she yelled.
Question, tag beforeShe asked, "Where are my keys?"She asked, "Where are my keys?".
Sentence is a question about a quoteDid she really say "I quit"?Did she really say "I quit?"
Both are questionsDid he actually ask, "Where were you?"Did he actually ask, "Where were you?"?

The reporting clause after a question mark or exclamation point is still lowercase: "Watch out!" she yelled.

2.4 Split quotations#

A quotation can be interrupted by the reporting clause. What punctuation you use depends on whether you split inside one sentence or between two.

Split typePatternExample
Inside one sentencecomma … comma, resume lowercase"I know," she said, "but it's late."
Between two sentencescomma … period, resume with a capital"I know," she said. "It's late."
Before a coordinating conjunctioncomma … comma, resume lowercase"I called twice," he said, "and nobody picked up."
Interrupted mid-phrasecomma … comma, resume lowercase"The thing about Chicago," she said, "is the wind."

Getting this wrong produces either a comma splice ("I know," she said, "It's late.") or a fragment ("I know," she said. "but it's late."). Test it by deleting the reporting clause: I know but it's late is one sentence, so commas and a lowercase resumption are right; I know. It's late. is two, so the period and the capital are right.

2.5 Multi-sentence quotations#

Quotation marks open once and close once, around the whole run of speech, however many sentences it contains. Do not re-quote every sentence. The reporting clause normally goes after the first sentence rather than at the end of a long speech — the reader wants to know who is talking early.

  • She said, "The store closes at nine. If you want the sale price, you have to be there by eight."
  • "The store closes at nine," she said. "If you want the sale price, you have to be there by eight."
  • She said, "The store closes at nine." "If you want the sale price, you have to be there by eight."

2.6 Quotations inside quotations#

American English uses double quotation marks for the outer quotation and single marks for a quotation inside it. British practice has traditionally been the reverse, though British publishers increasingly use doubles too. Whichever system you pick, do not mix them.

  • "He actually said, 'I never agreed to that,'" Maria told me.
  • Maria told me, "He actually said, 'I never agreed to that.'"
  • She asked, "Did he really say, 'I quit'?"

Note the pileup at the end: the inner mark closes first, then the outer. In 'I never agreed to that,'" the comma sits inside the single mark, the single mark closes the inner quote, and the double mark closes the outer one — three characters, in that order. A third level goes back to double marks, but at that point rewrite the sentence instead.

Single quotation marks have no other job in American prose. They are not a lighter alternative to doubles and they are not for emphasis. The one common exception is newspaper headlines, which use singles to save space: Mayor calls budget 'a disaster'.

2.7 Periods and commas go inside the closing quote#

This is the American rule, and it is close to absolute: a period or a comma at the end of quoted material goes inside the closing quotation mark, whether or not it belongs to the quoted words.

  • She described the fix as "temporary," which turned out to be generous.
  • He kept using the word "streamlined."

Neither of those marks was in the original wording. They go inside anyway. Colons and semicolons behave in the opposite way — always outside — and question marks and exclamation points go wherever logic puts them.

MarkPosition in American styleExample

SemicolonAlways outsideHe called it "a rounding error"; the auditors disagreed.
ColonAlways outsideHere is what he called "the real problem": staffing.

Exclamation pointInside if the quotation is the exclamationHe shouted, "Stop!"

Where the rule is genuinely breaking down. Outside of edited prose, a large number of Americans now write the "logical" way — He called it "flexible", and left. — because it is the only sane convention when the quoted string is code, a password, a filename, or a keystroke, and because the online writing habits of a generation were formed in technical forums. Chicago itself describes this alternative placement and sanctions it for work in which the exact content of the quoted string matters — textual criticism, linguistics, and computing — where Type "yes", then press Enter beats putting inside the quotation marks a comma the reader is not supposed to type. Two practical rules follow. In anything edited to an American style guide — school, journalism, publishing, business writing — put the period and comma inside; that is what a copyeditor will enforce. In technical writing where a stray comma inside a quoted string is a factual error, put it outside and do it consistently.

2.8 Paragraphing dialogue#

Every change of speaker starts a new paragraph. This is what lets a reader follow a long exchange without a tag on every line.

"Did you send it?" Priya asked.

"Twice," Daniel said.

"Then why is my inbox empty?"

The last line needs no tag at all — the paragraph break assigns it. When one speaker talks for more than one paragraph, open each new paragraph with a quotation mark but close the quotation only at the end of the final paragraph. The missing close-quote is the signal that the same person is still speaking.

"The problem started in March. Nobody flagged it, because the reports were still coming in on schedule.

"By June we were three weeks behind, and by then the client had noticed."

2.9 Quoting a fragment inside your own sentence#

When you weave a phrase rather than a whole sentence into your syntax, there is no comma and no capital letter. The quoted words simply do their normal grammatical job.

Full-sentence quotationFragment woven in
The mayor said, "There will be no new taxes this year."The mayor promised "no new taxes this year."
She said, "It was a disaster."She called the rollout "a disaster."
He said, "I have no comment at this time."He had "no comment at this time."

A comma before a woven-in fragment is an error: The mayor promised, "no new taxes this year." Either quote the sentence whole, or absorb the fragment without a comma. To change anything inside a quotation, use square brackets (She said the proposal "[had] been dead on arrival for weeks."); to cut anything, use an ellipsis ("The committee met … and adjourned without a vote.").

3. The reporting clause#

The reporting clause is the she said part: a subject, a reporting verb, and sometimes an object or an adverbial. It can go in three places.

3.1 Position#

PositionExampleEffect
InitialMaria said, "I'll call you tomorrow."Neutral; identifies the speaker before the words. Standard in journalism and formal writing.
Medial"I'll call you," Maria said, "as soon as I land."Breaks a long quotation; gives a beat of pause where the tag sits.
Final"I'll call you tomorrow," Maria said.The commonest position in fiction; the words land first, the attribution follows quietly.

In fiction, final and medial dominate — the reader wants the line before the label. In news writing the attribution comes early and often, since American newspaper style attributes almost every sentence of a quoted statement.

3.2 The inversion option: said Maria / Maria said#

When the subject is a noun, the reporting clause may invert. Both orders are standard, and the choice is pure rhythm.

  • "I'll call you tomorrow," Maria said.
  • "I'll call you tomorrow," said Maria.

Five constraints govern it.

ConstraintAllowedNot allowed
Subject is a noun phrase"Come in," said the man in the gray coat.—
Subject is a pronoun"Come in," he said."Come in," said he. (archaic outside of fairy tales and parody)
Verb is a simple one-word form"Come in," said Maria."Come in," had said Maria.
Reporting clause has a direct object"Come in," Maria told me."Come in," told me Maria.
Reporting clause is initialMaria said, "Come in."Said Maria, "Come in." — heavily marked; survives only in headlines and older journalism

Two soft preferences on top of that. Inversion sounds better the longer the subject is — said the woman who had been waiting by the door beats the woman who had been waiting by the door said. And American news style (AP) prefers the uninverted Smith said, reserving said Smith for features. Adverbs work with either order as long as they follow the verb-plus-subject unit: "Come in," said Maria quietly / "Come in," Maria said quietly, but not "Come in," quietly said Maria.

3.3 Which verbs can be reporting verbs#

Any verb of speaking: say, tell, ask, inquire, reply, answer, add, explain, admit, insist, whisper, shout, mutter, murmur, announce, note, observe, continue, repeat, warn, promise, apologize, complain, agree. The full inventory, with the complement each one takes, is in the reporting-verb dictionary.

American spellings of the reporting verbs. Several of the commonest ones are spelled differently on the two sides of the Atlantic, and the reporting verb is where a British-trained writer's spelling shows first.

AmericanBritishNote
apologizeapologiseThe -ize family is uniform in American English: apologize, criticize, emphasize, recognize, summarize, theorize. British publishers are split between -ize and -ise; American ones are not. The noun is apology in both.
inquire, inquiryenquire / inquire, enquiry / inquiryBritish English splits the pair by sense — enquire is to ask an ordinary question, inquire is to conduct a formal investigation. American English makes no such split: inquire covers both senses, and enquire in an American document reads as a typo or an affectation.

Neither dialect's spelling changes anything about the grammar. She inquired whether the room was still available and He apologized for forgetting backshift exactly as they would after said. The longer treatment is in When not to backshift, and inquire's complement pattern — it takes no person object, so She inquired me whether… is impossible — is in Reported questions and in section 5.4 below.

Verbs that are not speech verbs. Smile, laugh, grin, shrug, nod, frown, sigh describe things done with a face or a body, not sounds shaped into words. Strictly, "Hello," she smiled. is an error and most American editors will change it — but it is extremely common in published fiction, so calling it flatly wrong overstates the case, and laugh and sigh get a pass from many editors because they at least make noise. The safe form separates the two actions: "Hello," she said, smiling. or She smiled. "Hello."

Said-bookisms. Said is invisible; readers skim it the way they skim the. A page on which characters ejaculate, opine, expostulate, and chortle draws attention to the writer instead of the dialogue. Use said by default, asked for questions, and reach for something else only when the manner is not already obvious from the words.

3.4 Reporting clauses in reported speech#

None of this applies once you convert. In reported speech there is no separate reporting clause to position — the reporting verb becomes the main verb of the sentence, the message becomes its object, and there is nothing to invert and nothing to punctuate: "I'll call you tomorrow," said Maria. → Maria said (that) she would call me the next day.

4. The that-clause#

A reported statement is normally a noun clause introduced by that, functioning as the direct object of the reporting verb. That here is a complementizer — a pure grammatical marker that carries no meaning and fills no slot inside its own clause. It has nothing to do with demonstrative that (I said that) or relative that (the box that came). See the complementizer entry for the full treatment.

"The road is closed," he said. → He said that the road was closed.

4.1 Never put a comma before that#

  • He said, that the road was closed.
  • He said that the road was closed.

The comma belongs to direct speech only; the junction between a verb and its own complement clause never takes one. This is a top-three error for speakers of languages that do comma the subordinator, and it is instantly visible to an American reader. If a parenthetical falls right after that, it takes commas on both sides: He said that, if the storm holds, they'll cancel.

4.2 When that can be dropped#

After the common reporting verbs, that is optional and is usually left out in speech and informal writing. Both versions are fully correct: She said (that) she was tired. I told him (that) the meeting had moved. The drop is easy after say, tell, think, know, believe, hope, feel, guess, mean, suppose, hear, see (in the "understand" sense), and promise. It gets progressively harder as the verb gets rarer or more formal.

SituationThatExample
Everyday verb, speech or informal writingUsually droppedShe said she was tired.
Formal, academic, or legal writingUsually keptThe authors state that the sample was too small.
Low-frequency or formal reporting verb (assert, contend, maintain, stipulate, testify, allege, conclude)KeptHe maintained that the invoices were genuine.
Something intervenes between verb and clauseKeptShe told the committee, after a long silence, that she would resign.
The clause opens with a time or place adverbialKept, to prevent misreadingHe said that on Monday the office would be closed.
The clause subject is long or contains a clause of its ownKeptShe said that the man who had been waiting by the door was gone.
Second and later clauses in a coordinated pairKeptHe said he was tired and that he wanted to go home.
Passive report frameRequiredIt is said that the house is haunted.
ask, insist, demand, recommend + subjunctiveKeptShe asked that the meeting be postponed.
Clause is the subject of the sentenceRequiredThat he never called back says everything.

The adverbial case is the one that actually creates ambiguity: He said that on Monday the office would be closed (the closure is Monday) vs He said on Monday that the office would be closed (the saying was Monday). Without either that, He said on Monday the office would be closed is genuinely ambiguous — do not write it.

4.3 The subjunctive row is an American/British split#

After the demanding and recommending verbs — ask, insist, demand, recommend, require, request, suggest, propose, urge, move — American English puts the bare base form in the that-clause. British English typically inserts should. The American form is the mandative subjunctive, and presenting the should version as the default is the single most common way a British coursebook misdescribes American practice.

AmericanBritish
DemandShe insisted that he be present.She insisted that he should be present.
RecommendationThe doctor recommended that she stop smoking.The doctor recommended that she should stop smoking.
Institutional requestThe judge asked that the room be cleared.The judge asked that the room should be cleared.
NegativeHe asked that we not sign anything.He asked that we should not sign anything.

Four things follow, and all four matter when you report:

  • It is tenseless, so it never backshifts. He insists that she be there → He insisted that she be there — not was there, not should be there. Only the reporting verb moves.
  • The negative is a bare not, with no do-support: recommended that she not sign, never that she doesn't sign and never that she didn't sign.
  • It is not formal or archaic in American English. My landlord demanded that I move out and they're asking that everyone be seated by seven are ordinary speech, not legalese.
  • That is kept. The bare form is the clause's only signal, and without that it reads as an error: She asked the meeting be postponed.

The form is invisible except in the third-person singular and with be — they demanded that we leave is spelled identically to the indicative — which is why the difference only ever surfaces in that he leave, that she not sign, that it be. And note that the same verbs have a second, indicative life in which they report a claim rather than a demand, and that one backshifts normally: She insisted that he was present means she asserted he was there. Full treatment: Reported commands, requests, and advice and When not to backshift.

4.4 What follows the that#

A full finite clause with a subject and a tensed verb — never an infinitive, never an -ing form: She said that she would help (not She said to help, which means something else entirely); He admitted that he had lied (not He admitted to have lied).

Not every reporting verb takes a that-clause, and the ones that do differ on what else has to be present. Tell takes one only with the person object in place — He told me that the road was closed, never He told that the road was closed — while promise takes the person object or leaves it out freely: He promised me that he would come and He promised that he would come are both fully grammatical. Ask takes if/whether for questions and a to-infinitive for requests; speak and talk take none at all. That is section 5.

5. Say, tell, ask, speak, and talk#

Five verbs that translate to one or two verbs in many other languages, and that take five different complement patterns in English. Getting these wrong is the most reliable single marker of a non-native speaker. The general grammar of verb complementation is in the verbs reference.

5.2 say#

Say takes the message, never the listener. If you need to name the listener, you need the preposition to.

  • She said, "I'll be late." · She said (that) she would be late. · She said something rude to the cashier.
  • She said me she would be late. → She said to me she would be late. / She told me she would be late.

Say to someone is grammatical and sometimes necessary, but tell someone is far more idiomatic when a listener is named. Reserve say to for when the listener is contrasted or emphasized: He didn't say a word to me.

Fixed objects of say: say hello, say goodbye, say yes, say no, say thank you, say sorry, say grace, say a prayer, say a few words, say nothing, say so, needless to say, say the word, that is to say. None of them takes quotation marks — She said hello and He said thank you are complete and correct as they stand.

Say sorry is the one item on that list with a dialect tilt. It is ordinary adult English in Britain; in American English it survives mainly as something said to or about children (Say sorry to your sister). The adult American defaults are say I'm sorry and, in a report, the verb itself — spelled apologize, never apologise:

"I'm sorry I missed the meeting," he said. → He apologized for missing the meeting.

Say is also the verb for what a text states, and there it stays in the present tense indefinitely: The sign says no parking. The contract says the deposit is refundable.

5.3 tell#

Tell requires a person object. This is the rule that trips up the greatest number of learners, and it is not a preference — the sentence is ungrammatical without it.

  • He told me (that) the road was closed. · He told the class a story. · He told her to wait outside.
  • He told that the road was closed.
  • He told to me that the road was closed. — no preposition; tell takes the person bare.

The person object also blocks tell from carrying a direct quotation on its own: "I'm late," he told. → "I'm late," he told me. / He told me, "I'm late."

The exceptions. A short, closed list of fixed expressions where tell takes a non-person object and no listener:

ExpressionExample

In several of these, tell does not mean "say" at all — it means distinguish or know: I can't tell if it's working. You can tell she's nervous. That sense takes no person object and no message. Tell also has a passive that is extremely common in reporting: I was told the office had moved. We were told to wait.

5.4 ask#

Ask is the flexible one: the person object is optional, and it takes questions, requests, and demands.

PatternExample
ask + (person) + questionShe asked (me) a question.
ask + (person) + if/whether clauseHe asked (me) whether the store was open.
ask + (person) + wh- clauseHe asked (me) where I had parked.

Two errors to watch. There is no preposition before the person: She asked to me whether… And ask does not take a plain that-clause for a question: She asked me that I was late → She asked me whether I was late. The only that ask takes is the subjunctive one, which is a request, not a question. (The alternative ask a question of someone exists but is stiff.)

ask that + subjunctive. That that-clause takes the American bare base form: The judge asked that the room be cleared — not should be cleared, which is the British pattern, and not was cleared, which turns a demand into a claim about the past. It reports a request or a directive; everything above it in the table reports a question. See section 4.3 above, and Requests: the ask family.

The formal alternative: inquire. American English spells it inquire in every sense — asking an ordinary question and conducting a formal investigation alike — with inquiry for the noun. British English splits the spellings (enquire = ask, inquire = investigate); enquire in an American document reads as a typo. Inquire is also markedly more formal than ask: it belongs to police reports, journalism, and business correspondence, and in ordinary speech Americans just ask. Its complement pattern is not ask's, because inquire takes no person object.

PatternExample
inquire + whether / if clauseShe inquired whether the position had been filled.
inquire + wh- clauseThe detective inquired where I had been that night.
inquire about + topicHe inquired about the deposit.
inquire of + person (very formal)She inquired of the registrar whether the transcript had arrived.
✗ inquire + personShe inquired me whether the position had been filled. → She asked me whether… / She inquired whether…
✗ British spellingShe enquired whether the position had been filled.

The full entry, alongside wonder, query, and want to know, is in Reported questions.

5.5 speak and talk#

Neither verb takes the message as an object, and neither takes the listener without a preposition. They report the event of speaking, not its content: I spoke to her yesterday. We talked about the budget for an hour. Never I spoke her or We talked the budget.

5.6 Choosing among them in reported speech#

OriginalReporting verbReported
"It's raining."say / tellShe said it was raining. / She told me it was raining.
"Is it raining?"askShe asked whether it was raining.
"Close the window."tell (order) / ask (request)She told me to close the window. / She asked me to close the window.
Two people, one hour, no content givenspeak / talkThey spoke for an hour. / They talked for an hour.

Say cannot take a person object in an order (He said me to sit down → He told me to sit down), and tell cannot report a question with a that-clause (He told me that where I lived → He asked me where I lived).

One honest footnote to the first of those. Objectless say + to-infinitive — He said to wait. She said not to touch it. — is extremely common in spoken American English, and it is what most Americans would actually produce. Edited prose still prefers He told us to wait or He said we should wait, and say never takes the person object in any register, but calling He said to wait flatly ungrammatical misdescribes the language. It is worked through in Reported commands, requests, and advice.

6. The three things that change#

Converting direct speech to reported speech means recalculating everything anchored to the original speaker and the original moment. Three systems need it, and the sections that follow this one take them in turn: the tense gets two sections of its own (Backshift and When not to backshift), and the pronouns and the pointing words share a third (Pronouns, time, and place shifts).

What changesWhyDirectReported
TenseThe reporting verb is usually past, so the message shifts back one step — backshift"I am tired."She said she was tired.
Pronouns and possessivesI and you were the original speaker and listener; from where you stand, they are somebody else"I lost my keys."Maria said she had lost her keys.
Time and place wordsnow, today, here, this pointed at the original moment and location"I'll see you here tomorrow."He said he'd see me there the next day.

All three at once:

"I'm bringing my laptop here tomorrow," Maria said. → Maria said she was taking her laptop there the next day.

am → was (tense), I → she and my → her (pronouns), here → there and tomorrow → the next day (place and time) — and bring → take travels with them, because bring means "carry it to where I am" and you are no longer standing where Maria stood. Not touched: laptop, and the fact being reported.

Change where you are standing and half of that unwinds. Reporting the same sentence the same day, from that same room: Maria said she was bringing her laptop here tomorrow. The tense and the pronouns still shift, because Maria is still not you; here, tomorrow, and bring do not, because they still point where they pointed.

6.1 Tense — a preview#

The reporting verb's tense drives everything. When it is past (said, told, asked), the reported verb normally moves one step back.

"I work downtown." → She said she worked downtown. "I saw him." → She said she had seen him. "I will call." → She said she would call.

Backshift is not automatic. It does not happen when the reporting verb is present (She says she works downtown), and it is routinely skipped when the fact is still true at the moment of reporting. Full rules, the modal table, and the cases where American speakers ignore the textbook: Backshift.

Two of those cases are worth stating here rather than deferring, because ordinary American speech breaks the textbook rule in both, and a learner who has only ever seen the rule will hear correct English and think it is wrong.

A still-true statement often keeps its present tense. Backshift is available, not compulsory. When what was said is still true at the moment you report it, both versions are correct, and the unshifted one is the more common in speech:

"I live in Denver," she said. → She said she lived in Denver. / She said she lives in Denver. "The meeting is at four," he said. → He said the meeting was at four. / He said the meeting is at four.

The shifted version is never wrong. The unshifted version says one thing extra — that the fact still holds — and shifting can actually mislead: She said she was a vegetarian invites the reading that she has since stopped being one.

A past simple usually stays a past simple. The textbook shift is past simple → past perfect, and it is correct, but Americans routinely leave the past simple where it is when the time order is already clear from the context or from a time adverbial:

"I saw the email," she said. → She said she had seen the email. / She said she saw the email. "We moved here in 2019," he said. → He said they had moved there in 2019. / He said they moved there in 2019.

Both members of each pair are standard American English, and the second is the more likely in speech. Reach for the past perfect when the sequence genuinely needs marking — when the reported event has to be pinned before some other past event — and let the past simple stand otherwise. The long versions: Backshift is available, not compulsory, Optional backshift: the statement is still true, and Past simple often stays past simple.

6.2 Pronouns — a preview#

I/we becomes whatever the original speaker is to you; you becomes whatever the original listener is to you; third person usually stays put. The possessives (my, your, our) and reflexives (myself, yourself) move with them.

Maria, to me: "I need your signature." → Maria said she needed my signature. Maria, to Daniel: "I need your signature." → Maria told Daniel she needed his signature.

The same original sentence, two different reports, because the listener changed. Pronouns are recalculated from your position, not the speaker's — which is why shift is a misleading word for what happens to them. There is no dial to turn; you work out who each pronoun refers to and then name that person from where you stand. The full recalculation, including the reflexives, the inclusive-versus-exclusive we, and the generic you that must not become I, is in Pronouns, time, and place shifts; the pronoun system itself is in the pronouns reference.

6.3 Time and place — a preview#

Words that point at the speaker's moment and location get re-anchored to yours.

"I'll finish it tonight." → He said he'd finish it that night. "Leave it here on this desk." → She told me to leave it there on that desk.

The verbs come/go and bring/take shift too, because they encode direction relative to a location: "Come over," she said → She asked me to go over. Whether a time word changes at all depends on when you are reporting: if Maria says "I'll call you tomorrow" and you report it the same day, tomorrow stays tomorrow — the day she meant has not moved. The full table of time and place equivalences, and the same-day, same-place test that decides whether any of them apply, is in Pronouns, time, and place shifts; adverbs in general are covered in the adverbs reference.

6.4 What does not change#

Stays the sameExample
Proper nouns"I saw Daniel." → She said she had seen Daniel.
The content of the messageNothing is added, removed, or reinterpreted.
Modals with no further-back form — would, could, might, should, ought to, used to, had better"You should call." → She said I should call.
The past perfect (already as far back as it goes)"I had already left." → He said he had already left.
The mandative subjunctive (section 4.3) — it has no tense to move"I insist that he be present." → She insisted that he be present.
Word order of a statementIt was statement order before, and it stays statement order.
Verbs in an unreal conditional or wish"If I had more time, I would finish." → She said that if she had more time she would finish.

Could, would, might, and should are of course past forms already; the point is that English gives them nowhere further back to go, so a past reporting verb leaves them alone. The modal-by-modal inventory is in Modal verbs in reported speech. Two more things need not change, though here you have a choice rather than a constraint: a still-true present and a plain past simple may both stay exactly as they were (section 6.1).

And one thing is removed: everything that only works in a live voice. Interjections, vocatives, hesitations, tag questions, please, and exclamations do not survive into reported speech; their meaning has to be rebuilt into the reporting verb or dropped.

"Wow, that's expensive!" she said. → She said she was astonished at how expensive it was. "Hey, Daniel, could you help me, please?" → She politely asked Daniel to help her.

Not She asked, "where I lived." and not She asked where I lived? — just She asked where I lived. One live question mark does survive: if your sentence is a question, it gets one. Did she really ask where you lived?

7. Quick reference#

Direct speech, one checklist:

  1. Comma (or colon) before the opening quotation mark.
  2. Capital letter on the first word of a quoted sentence.
  3. Double quotation marks outside, single inside.
  4. Period and comma always inside the closing mark.
  5. Question mark and exclamation point inside if they belong to the quote, outside if they belong to your sentence — and never two terminal marks in a row.
  6. Reporting clause after the quote is lowercase.
  7. New speaker, new paragraph.

Conversion, one checklist:

  1. Delete the quotation marks and the comma.
  2. Add that (or leave it out — check the table in section 4.2).
  3. Backshift the tense — unless the reporting verb is present, in which case nothing moves at all. If the fact is still true, or the verb is a past simple whose sequence is already clear, the shift is optional: both versions are standard American English.
  4. Recalculate every pronoun and possessive from your position.
  5. Recalculate here, now, today, this, come, bring.
  6. Change the reporting verb if the sentence was not a statement — ask for a question, tell/order/ask for a command.
  7. Delete anything that only works out loud (um, wow, please, hey, right?), and leave no question mark, exclamation point, or quotation mark in the result.

8. Worked example: a dialogue, paragraph by paragraph#

Here is a short exchange in direct speech, followed by a paragraph-by-paragraph conversion.

"I'm not going to finish this report tonight," Daniel said. "I've been staring at the same paragraph for an hour."

"Do you want me to take a look at it tomorrow?" Priya asked.

"Would you?" he said. "I sent the draft to your email this morning."

"I'll read it on the train," she said. "But you have to promise me you'll go home now."

"I promise," he said.

Assume you are reporting this a week later, and that you were not one of the speakers.

Paragraph 1#

"I'm not going to finish this report tonight," Daniel said. "I've been staring at the same paragraph for an hour." → Daniel said that he wasn't going to finish that report that night, because he had been staring at the same paragraph for an hour.

  • I → he — Daniel is a third person from where you stand.
  • am not going to → wasn't going to — the be of be going to backshifts, and the rest of the phrase rides along; I've been → he had been (present perfect continuous to past perfect continuous).
  • this report → that report — the demonstrative is repointed away from the speaker.
  • tonight → that night — the night he meant is a week gone.
  • Two quoted sentences become one reported sentence. The second explained the first, so because makes that relationship explicit — a conversion that keeps every sentence boundary sounds like a transcript, not a report.

Paragraph 2#

"Do you want me to take a look at it tomorrow?" Priya asked. → Priya asked whether he wanted her to take a look at it the next day.

  • The verb becomes ask, not say — this was a question, and a yes/no question takes whether (or if). Nothing else can introduce it.
  • Do you want → he wanted — the do-support disappears, the word order becomes statement order, and want backshifts.
  • you → he, me → her, tomorrow → the next day.
  • The question mark is gone. A reported question is not a question.

Paragraph 3#

"Would you?" he said. "I sent the draft to your email this morning." → Daniel asked if she would, and told her that he had sent the draft to her email that morning.

  • he said → asked — Would you? is a question, so the reporting verb changes even though the original tag said said. Reporting verbs are chosen for the function of the utterance, not copied from the tag.
  • Would you? is elliptical, and the report keeps the ellipsis (if she would), which is what an English speaker would actually do, rather than reconstructing the whole clause. And would does not backshift — it has no further-back form.
  • I sent → he had sent (past simple to past perfect); your → her; this morning → that morning. …told her that he sent the draft that morning is equally standard American English, and more likely in speech — the past perfect is the textbook shift, not an obligation, and that morning has already fixed the sequence (section 6.1).
  • Two utterances, two functions, so two reporting verbs joined by and: asked … and told her …. Note told her — tell cannot appear without its person object.

Paragraph 4#

"I'll read it on the train," she said. "But you have to promise me you'll go home now." → Priya said she would read it on the train, but that he had to promise her he would go home right then.

  • I'll → she would — will backshifts to would; you → he, you'll → he would.
  • have to → had to — a semi-modal, and unlike must it has a past form, so it uses it.
  • now → right then — the moment being pointed at is a week old.
  • The second that is kept: but that he had to. When two reported clauses are coordinated, dropping the second that makes the reader lose track of where the report ends.

Paragraph 5#

"I promise," he said. → Daniel promised.

  • A performative — a sentence that performs the act it names — collapses into the reporting verb. He said that he promised is grammatical but redundant.
  • If the content matters, restore it from context: Daniel promised (that) he would. The full entry for this verb, with its complement patterns, is at promise.

The whole thing#

Daniel said that he wasn't going to finish that report that night, because he had been staring at the same paragraph for an hour. Priya asked whether he wanted her to take a look at it the next day. Daniel asked if she would, and told her that he had sent the draft to her email that morning. Priya said she would read it on the train, but that he had to promise her he would go home right then. Daniel promised.

Three observations about the finished paragraph, and they hold for every conversion of real dialogue.

Names beat pronouns. With two third-person participants, an unbroken run of he and she becomes unreadable. Repeating Daniel and Priya at each change of speaker is not clumsy; it is necessary.

Sentence boundaries move. The dialogue had eight sentences; the report has five. Reported speech compresses, and it uses connectives — because, and, but — to carry relationships that the original conveyed through juxtaposition and tone of voice.

The reporting verbs do work the tags did not. Said four times and asked once became said, asked, asked, told, said, promised. Choosing a verb that names what the speaker was doing — asking, promising, admitting, refusing — is the single biggest quality difference between a mechanical conversion and a good one. That is what the reporting-verb dictionary is for.
