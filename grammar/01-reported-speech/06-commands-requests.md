# Reported commands, requests, and advice

> How an imperative — a bare order, a polite request, a piece of advice — turns into a to-infinitive, which reporting verbs demand an object before it, and why *He suggested me to leave* is wrong.

[← Reported speech](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

Statements are reported with a *that*-clause and questions with an embedded wh- or *whether*-clause. Directives — commands, requests, instructions, warnings, invitations, advice — take a third route entirely: the imperative collapses into a **to-infinitive**, and the person being directed moves into the object slot in front of it.

> "Sit down."  →  He told me **to sit down**.

That one line contains almost everything. The imperative *sit down* becomes *to sit down*. The addressee, invisible in the original because an imperative has no stated subject, surfaces as *me*. And the reporting verb is no longer *said* — *say* cannot carry this pattern — but *told*, chosen because it encodes the force of the original: an order rather than a request or a suggestion.

The two things that go wrong here are the negative (*not to*, not *to not*) and the object. Whether a reporting verb requires an object, forbids one, or tolerates either is a lexical fact about that verb, learned one verb at a time. Getting it wrong produces the most common structural error in reported speech: ***He suggested me to leave*.**

## The core pattern

### Verb + object + to-infinitive

The frame is fixed. Nothing goes between the object and *to*.

| Slot | Filled by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting verb | *tell, ask, order, advise, warn*… | She **told** |
| Object | the person addressed | **me** |
| To-infinitive | the original imperative | **to wait** |

> "Wait here."  →  She told me **to wait** there.
> "Call me when you land."  →  He asked me **to call** him when I landed.
> "Fill out the form in blue ink."  →  The clerk instructed us **to fill out** the form in blue ink.

The infinitive carries the whole predicate of the original, including its objects, complements, and adverbials. Only the verb itself changes shape.

> "Put the boxes in the garage before it rains."  →  He told me **to put** the boxes in the garage before it rained.

### The infinitive itself never backshifts

An imperative has no tense to shift. *Sit down* is not present, past, or future — it is the bare base form, and a to-infinitive is equally tenseless. So the whole apparatus described in [Backshift](02-backshift.md) has nothing to grip on the infinitive.

> "Be here at eight."  →  He told me **to be** there at eight. (not *to have been*)

This makes reported commands structurally the easiest kind of reported speech: the verb form is decided for you. It also means the report is silent about whether the command was obeyed, or even whether the deadline has passed — *He told me to be there at eight* works whether it is now seven, nine, or next year. What backshift *can* still reach is any finite clause the command drags along with it, and that is [a few paragraphs down](#finite-clauses-riding-inside-the-command).

### Everything else still shifts

Backshift is off in the infinitive, but every other adjustment stays on: pronouns, possessives, demonstratives, and adverbials of time and place all move to fit the new speaker and moment.

> "Bring **your** laptop to **this** meeting **tomorrow**," she said.  →  She told me to bring **my** laptop to **that** meeting **the next day**.
> "Meet me **here** in an hour."  →  She asked me to meet her **there** in an hour.
> "Don't touch **these** wires."  →  The electrician warned us not to touch **those** wires.

Note what happens to the speaker's *me*: it becomes *him* or *her* in the report, while the addressee becomes the object of the reporting verb. Both pronouns rotate, in opposite directions.

> "Send **me** the file, and I'll forward it to **you**."  →  He asked **me** to send **him** the file, and said he would forward it to **me**.

### Finite clauses riding inside the command

The infinitive itself is tenseless, but an imperative can carry a full subordinate clause along with it, and that clause *does* have a tense. It backshifts by the ordinary rules — the reporting verb resets the clock for everything finite underneath it, infinitive or no infinitive.

> "Call me when you **land**."  →  He asked me to call him when I **landed**.
> "Lock up before you **leave**."  →  She told me to lock up before I **left**.
> "Don't sign anything until you'**ve read** it."  →  She advised me not to sign anything until I'**d read** it.
> "Water the plants while I'**m** gone."  →  She asked me to water the plants while she **was** gone.

**And here is where the textbook rule loosens.** Everything [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md) says about optional backshift applies inside a reported directive too. When the embedded clause is still true, or still ahead on the calendar, ordinary American speech very often leaves it alone — and nobody hears an error:

> "Text me when you get in."  →  She told me to text her when I **get** in. *(I have not gotten in yet)*
> "Text me when you get in."  →  She told me to text her when I **got** in. *(also correct, and the safer choice in writing)*

The same escape hatch covers a past simple inside the command. [The past perfect is a floor, not an obligation](02-backshift.md#the-floor-is-also-why-past-simple-often-stays-put), so a past simple riding inside a directive usually just stays put:

> "Don't mention what I **told** you last night."  →  He asked me not to mention what he **told** me the night before. ✓ ordinary
> →  He asked me not to mention what he **had told** me the night before. ✓ more formal, equally correct

Backshifting is never wrong here; skipping it is merely often right. If you are editing rather than talking, backshift and move on. What you cannot do is shift half a sentence — [pronouns and time words](04-pronouns-time-place.md) rotate whether or not the tense does.

### Vocatives, *please*, and other politeness debris

A vocative in the original tells you who the object should be, and then disappears.

> "**Marcus**, close the door."  →  She told **Marcus** to close the door.

*Please*, *if you don't mind*, and hesitation markers are not reported as words. Their content is absorbed into the choice of reporting verb, or into an adverb of manner.

| Original | Standard report | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "Please sit down." | He **asked** me to sit down. | *ask* already encodes politeness |
| "Please sit down." | He **politely told** me to sit down. | adverb, if the politeness matters |
| "Sit down!" | He **ordered** me to sit down. | force lives in the verb |
| "Sit down, please, if you don't mind." | He asked me to sit down. | hedges dissolve; do not stack them |

*He asked me to please sit down* is possible but reads as a near-quotation, and it splits the infinitive. Prefer the adverb or a stronger verb.

### The passive report

Directives are reported in the passive constantly, especially in workplace and institutional English, because the person who gave the order is often unknown or irrelevant. The object of the active becomes the subject; the infinitive does not change.

> They told us to wait outside.  →  We **were told** to wait outside.
> "Do not leave bags unattended."  →  Travelers **are warned** not to leave bags unattended.

Verbs that take no object in the active (*suggest*, *insist*, *propose*) cannot form this passive either: **He was suggested to leave* is wrong for the same reason ***They suggested him to leave* is.

## The negative: *not to*

### Where *not* goes

*Not* goes **immediately before *to***, not after it and not before the object.

> "Don't worry."  →  He told me **not to** worry.
> "Don't sign anything until you've read it."  →  She advised me **not to** sign anything until I'd read it.

There is no auxiliary *do* anywhere in the report. ✗ *He told me not to worry*, never ✗ *He told me to don't worry* or ✗ *He told me don't worry*.

### *to not* is marked

***He told me to not worry* is marked.** The split-infinitive negative is not ungrammatical, and it is not rare in casual speech, but in reported commands it is a distinctly minority form, and in edited American prose *not to* is close to universal. Produce *not to* every time.

The one place *to not* genuinely earns its keep is under contrastive stress, when the choice not to act is itself the point — and even there it belongs to decision verbs (*choose, decide, prefer*) rather than to reported commands.

| Form | Status | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *not to* | standard everywhere | She told me **not to** call him. |
| *to not* (reported command) | marked; avoid | ?She told me **to not** call him. |
| *to not* (deliberate choice) | fine in speech, still avoided in formal writing | I decided **to not** respond. |
| *to don't* / *don't to* | not English | ✗ *She told me to don't call him.* |

### *never* and *don't ever*

An emphatic negative imperative is usually reported with *never* in the same slot as *not*, before *to*. *Stop* and *quit* imperatives keep their own gerund complement.

> "Don't ever call me at work."  →  He told me **never to** call him at work.
> "Stop interrupting me."  →  She told me **to stop interrupting** her.

## Which verbs take an object

Three groups.

### Group 1 — the object is required

These verbs cannot appear with the infinitive unless somebody is named in between. They are the ordinary directive verbs, and they cover most of what you will ever need.

**tell, order, command, remind, warn, advise, persuade, convince, urge, forbid, instruct, invite, encourage, permit, allow, require, force, get**

> "Sit down."  →  He **told me** to sit down. (not ✗ *He told to sit down*)
> "Don't forget your passport."  →  She **reminded me** to bring my passport.
> "You really should see a doctor."  →  She **advised me** to see a doctor.
> "Come on, stay one more night."  →  They **persuaded me** to stay one more night.

### Group 2 — the object is impossible

These verbs never take a personal object before a to-infinitive. This is the group that generates errors, because in many other languages the equivalent verbs behave like Group 1.

**suggest, recommend, propose, insist, demand, say, mention, explain, announce, complain**

> "Let's leave early."  →  He **suggested that we leave** early. (not ✗ *He suggested us to leave*)
> "You should try the branzino."  →  She **recommended trying** the branzino. (not ✗ *She recommended me to try*)
> "You have to see it for yourself."  →  She **insisted that I see** it for myself.

### Group 3 — either way, with a meaning difference

A handful of verbs take an object or not, and the presence of the object changes who does the action.

| Verb | No object | With object |
|---|---|---|
| **ask** | She asked **to leave** early. (*she* leaves) | She asked **me to leave** early. (*I* leave) |
| **beg** | He begged **to differ**. (*he* differs) | He begged **me to reconsider**. (*I* reconsider) |
| **want** | She wanted **to drive**. (*she* drives) | She wanted **me to drive**. (*I* drive) |
| **expect** | We expect **to finish** by five. (*we* finish) | We expect **you to finish** by five. (*you* finish) |
| **promise** | He promised **to call**. (*he* calls) | rare — see below |

### *He suggested me to leave* — and the three repairs

This deserves its own heading because it is the most common single error in reported speech, and because every learner reproduces it independently.

***He suggested me to leave* is not English.** *Suggest* takes a clause or a gerund. It never takes a person plus an infinitive — not with an object (✗ *suggested me to leave*), not with a preposition (✗ *suggested to me to leave*), not with a bare infinitive (✗ *suggested me leave*).

Three repairs, all standard, all American:

| Repair | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. *that*-clause | *suggest that* + subject + **base form** | He suggested **that I leave**. |
| 2. Bare clause | drop *that* — the subjunctive stays | He suggested **I leave**. |
| 3. Gerund | *suggest* + **-ing**, no person named | He suggested **leaving**. |

Repair 1 is the mandative subjunctive, covered in full [below](#the-mandative-subjunctive) — note *leave*, not *leaves* and not *left*. Repair 3 loses the information about who was supposed to leave, so use it only when context supplies that.

If you need to name the person you spoke to, *suggest* takes *to* + person, but only alongside a clause or gerund:

> He suggested **to me** that I apply. ✓ &middot; He suggested **to me** to apply. ✗

The same three repairs work for the rest of Group 2:

| Verb | ✗ Wrong | ✓ *that*-clause | ✓ Gerund or other |
|---|---|---|---|
| suggest | *He suggested me to go.* | He suggested that I **go**. | He suggested **going**. |
| recommend | *She recommended me to apply.* | She recommended that I **apply**. | She recommended **applying**. |
| propose | *They proposed us to meet.* | They proposed that we **meet**. | They proposed **meeting**. |
| insist | *He insisted me to stay.* | He insisted that I **stay**. | He insisted **on my staying**. |
| demand | *She demanded me to leave.* | She demanded that I **leave**. | She demanded **to see** the manager. |
| mention | *He mentioned me to call.* | He mentioned that I **should call**. | — |
| explain | *She explained me to wait.* | She explained that we **had to wait**. | — |
| say | *He said me to wait.* | He said that I **should wait**. | He said **to wait**. (see below) |

The bolded verbs in the middle column are not all the same animal. In the first five rows the clause verb is a genuine **mandative subjunctive** — a bare base form, no *-s*, no tense, covered in full [below](#the-mandative-subjunctive). The last three verbs are not directive verbs at all: *mention*, *explain*, and *say* do not license the subjunctive, so their *that*-clauses take an ordinary modal (*should*, *had to*) and backshift normally. ✗ *He said that I wait* is not English.

### *He said to wait* — the rule that real speech breaks

Textbooks say *say* cannot report a command. In practice, **say + to-infinitive with no object is entirely normal in American speech and informal writing**, and *say not to* even more so.

> "Wait in the lobby."  →  He **said to wait** in the lobby.
> "Don't bother locking up."  →  She **said not to bother** locking up.

What is genuinely impossible is *say* with an object: ✗ *He said me to wait*, ✗ *He said us not to bother*. To name the addressee, switch to *tell*: *He told us not to bother.* Treat *say to* as good spoken English, and reach for *tell someone to* in anything edited.

### Verbs that take *from* + *-ing*

A separate group of directive-adjacent verbs — all about stopping someone — never reports a directive with an infinitive. They take a person plus *from* plus a gerund.

**prevent, prohibit, ban, bar, stop, discourage, dissuade, deter, restrain**

> "You can't enter the site without a helmet."  →  The rule **prohibits** workers **from entering** the site without a helmet.
> "Don't go into the water."  →  Lifeguards **discouraged** swimmers **from going** into the water.

Several of them also take a bare gerund when no person needs naming, and that is the natural way to report a blanket rule: *The building prohibits smoking*, *The city discourages idling*, *The sign bans parking*. What none of them permits is the infinitive — ✓ *prohibits smoking* is fine, ✗ *prohibits workers to smoke* is not.

***Forbid* is the one that goes both ways, and it is contested.** The traditional pattern is *forbid someone to do something*; *forbid someone from doing something* is now at least as common in American English and is accepted by most usage guides, though a minority of editors still flag it. Use whichever sounds better to you and expect no one to notice. *Prohibit*, by contrast, allows only *from*: ✗ *The rule prohibits workers to enter* is an error.

> "You may not enter the lab."  →  She **forbade** us **to enter** the lab. / She **forbade** us **from entering** the lab.

### Complete pattern table

Read the columns strictly. **+ object + *to*** means *verb + person + to-infinitive*, the person doing the action. **+ *to* (no object)** means the subject of the reporting verb is also the subject of the infinitive. **+ *that* + subjunctive** means a mandative *that*-clause with a bare base form — a ✗ here does not mean the verb refuses a *that*-clause, only that it refuses the subjunctive. **+ *-ing*** means a bare gerund with no person named.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Two cells are worth pausing on. *Warn* and *advise* both take **against**, not *from* — ✗ *warned us from going*, ✓ *warned us against going* — which is why neither gets a checkmark in the last column even though both are prohibition-flavored. And *want* and *expect* take an object plus an infinitive but never a *that*-clause of any kind: ✗ *I want that you come* is the mirror image of ✗ *He suggested me to leave*, and learners who avoid one often produce the other.

## Requests: the *ask* family

*Ask* is the general-purpose request verb, and it takes four structures depending on who is doing what.

### *ask someone to do something* — you want them to act

> "Could you close the window?"  →  She asked **me to close** the window.
> "Would you mind moving your car?"  →  He asked **me to move** my car.
> "Please hold my place in line."  →  She asked **me to hold** her place in line.

### *ask to do something* — you want to act yourself

No object. The subject of *ask* is also the subject of the infinitive.

> "Can I leave early today?"  →  She asked **to leave** early **that day**.
> "May I see the manager?"  →  He asked **to see** the manager.

The contrast is total, and it is the one thing to get right about *ask*:

| Sentence | Who leaves? |
|---|---|
| She asked **to leave**. | She does. |
| She asked **me to leave**. | I do. |

### *ask for* + noun — you want a thing

*Ask for* takes a noun phrase, never an infinitive. ✗ *She asked for to see the manager* is an error. With both a person and a thing, the person comes first and the thing follows *for*.

> "Can I have the check?"  →  He asked **for the check**.
> "Dad, can I borrow twenty dollars?"  →  She asked **her father for twenty dollars**.

The three side by side:

| Structure | Example | Means |
|---|---|---|
| ask + object + *to* | She asked me **to call** the office. | I make the call. |
| ask + *to* | She asked **to call** the office. | She makes the call. |
| ask + (object) + *for* + noun | She asked me **for** the office number. | She wants a thing from me. |

### *ask that* + subjunctive — formal and institutional

In notices, policies, and formal requests, *ask* takes a *that*-clause with the base form. This is noticeably more formal than *asked us to*, and it is the standard register for signage and written policy.

> "Please turn off your phones."  →  We ask **that all phones be** turned off.
> "Don't record this session."  →  The speaker asked **that the session not be** recorded.

### Politeness is not preserved

The elaborate politeness of a spoken request evaporates in the report, because the report is about content, not manner. All four of these come out the same way:

> "Close the window." / "Could you close the window?" / "Would you mind closing the window?" / "I hate to ask, but would it be at all possible to close the window?"  →  He asked me **to close** the window.

If the manner mattered — if he was rude, or absurdly deferential — say so with an adverb or a different verb: *He barked at me to close the window* / *He practically begged me to close the window*.

## Offers, promises, refusals, and threats

Not every reported directive points at the listener. Offers and promises are commitments the *speaker* makes, so the infinitive's subject is the speaker, and no object appears.

| Verb | Original | Report |
|---|---|---|
| offer | "I'll give you a ride." | He offered **to give** me a ride. |
| promise | "I'll have it done by Friday." | She promised **to have** it done by Friday. |
| agree | "Fine, I'll cover your shift." | He agreed **to cover** my shift. |
| refuse | "No, I'm not signing that." | She refused **to sign** it. |
| threaten | "If you do that again, I'll call the police." | He threatened **to call** the police. |
| volunteer | "I'll take the early flight." | She volunteered **to take** the early flight. |
| swear | "I'll never do it again." | He swore **never to do** it again. |

> "I'll pick you up at seven," he said.  →  He offered **to pick** me up at seven.
> "I promise I'll pay you back next week."  →  She promised **to pay** me back the following week.

More on these verbs in the reporting-verb dictionary: [promise](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise).

### *promise me to* — technically possible, practically dead

Unlike *tell* and *ask*, *promise* does not comfortably take a person plus an infinitive. *He promised me to be there* is attested and old-fashioned; almost nobody says it. American English overwhelmingly uses a *that*-clause when the addressee is named.

> "I'll be there, I swear."  →  He promised me **that he would be** there. ✓ natural
> "I'll be there, I swear."  →  He promised **to be** there. ✓ natural
> "I'll be there, I swear."  →  ?He promised **me to be** there. ✗ avoid

The same holds for *offer*: *He offered to drive* ✓, but ✗ *He offered me to drive* ✗. To name a beneficiary, use a noun: *He offered me a ride.*

## Force: *tell* vs. *ask* vs. *advise*

The infinitive frame is identical across these verbs; the reporting verb alone carries the force. Choosing it accurately is the substance of reporting a directive — *He told me to resign* and *He advised me to resign* describe very different conversations.

| Force | Verbs | Original sounds like | Report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute authority | order, command, direct | "Evacuate the building." | The chief **ordered** us to evacuate. |
| Plain command | tell, instruct | "Sit down." | He **told** me to sit down. |
| Neutral request | ask | "Could you sit down?" | He **asked** me to sit down. |
| Pleading | beg, plead with, implore | "Please, please sit down." | He **begged** me to sit down. |
| Strong push | urge, press | "You really need to sit down." | He **urged** me to sit down. |
| Advice | advise, recommend, suggest | "You should sit down." | He **advised** me to sit down. |
| Support | encourage | "Go on, apply. You'd be great." | She **encouraged** me to apply. |
| Success at convincing | persuade, convince, talk into | "Come on, one more night." | She **persuaded** me to stay. |
| Danger ahead | warn, caution | "Careful — that step is loose." | He **warned** me to watch the step. |
| Memory | remind | "Don't forget the passport." | She **reminded** me to bring my passport. |
| Prohibition | forbid, prohibit, ban | "You may not enter." | She **forbade** us to enter. |
| Invitation | invite | "Come to dinner Saturday." | They **invited** us to come to dinner that Saturday. |
| Non-negotiable | insist, demand | "No, you're taking a cab." | She **insisted that I take** a cab. |

Two notes on the boundaries.

**Advice verbs split by structure, not by force.** *Advise*, *recommend*, and *suggest* mean nearly the same thing, but only *advise* takes an object plus an infinitive. This is arbitrary and must simply be memorized.

> "You should apply."  →  She **advised me to apply**. ✓
> "You should apply."  →  She **recommended that I apply**. / She **suggested that I apply**. ✓
> "You should apply."  →  ✗ *She recommended me to apply.* / ✗ *She suggested me to apply.* ✗

**Persuade and convince entail success.** *He persuaded me to stay* means I stayed. If the attempt failed, say *tried to persuade*: *He tried to persuade me to stay, but I left anyway.*

**And *convince someone to do something* is standard American English**, whatever an older handbook may tell you. The traditional rule reserved the infinitive for *persuade* and gave *convince* only *that* or *of* — *convinced me that I should stay*, *convinced me of the need to stay*. That distinction lost. *He convinced me to stay* is used by educated American writers everywhere and flagged by almost no one; use it without worrying.

### *tell* is two verbs

*Tell* reports a command with an infinitive and a statement with a *that*-clause, and the two are not interchangeable.

| Structure | Force | Example |
|---|---|---|
| tell + object + **to**-infinitive | command | He told me **to go** home. (an order) |
| tell + object + **that**-clause | information | He told me **that he had gone** home. (a fact) |

> "Go home."  →  He told me **to go** home.
> "I went home."  →  He told me **that he had gone** home.

*Tell* also refuses to drop its object in either use: ✗ *He told to go home* and ✗ *He told that he had gone home* are both wrong. That object requirement is the sharpest line between *tell* and *say* — see [Backshift](02-backshift.md) for the *say* / *tell* split in reported statements.

## Reporting *let's*

*Let's* is a first-person-plural imperative: the speaker is proposing something that includes themselves. Reporting it with *told* would be wrong, because nobody was ordered to do anything. The natural reports use *suggest* or *propose* — which means, unavoidably, the Group 2 structures.

> "Let's take a break."  →  He **suggested that we take** a break. / He **suggested taking** a break.
> "Let's not argue about this now."  →  She **suggested that we not argue** about it then.
> "Let's meet at noon."  →  They **agreed to meet** at noon. (if the proposal was accepted)

Never: ✗ *He suggested us to take a break.* ✗ *He told us to take a break* is not wrong English, but it reports a different original — an order, not *let's*.

| Original | Report |
|---|---|
| "Let's go." | He suggested **that we go** / **going**. |
| "Why don't we go?" / "Shall we go?" | He suggested **that we go**. |
| "How about going?" | He suggested **going**. |
| "Let me help you with that." | He offered **to help** me with it. |
| "Let me know when you get in." | She asked me **to let** her know when I got in. |

*Let me…* splits two ways: when it means "allow me," it is an offer (*offered to help*); when it is a fixed request formula (*let me know*), the *let* stays in the infinitive.

In casual speech Americans routinely report *let's* by simply quoting it: *He said let's take a break.* That is fine conversationally and out of place in writing.

## The mandative subjunctive

When Group 2 verbs report a directive with a *that*-clause, the verb inside that clause goes into the **subjunctive**: the bare base form, for every person, regardless of the tense of the reporting verb.

> "You have to be present."  →  She insisted that he **be** present. (not *is*, not *was*, not *should be*)

### The form

| Subject | Indicative | Mandative subjunctive |
|---|---|---|
| I | I go | that I **go** |
| you | you go | that you **go** |
| he / she / it | he goe**s** | that he **go** |
| we | we go | that we **go** |
| they | they go | that they **go** |
| *be*, any subject | I am / he is / they are | that I **be** / that he **be** / that they **be** |

Only two cells differ from the ordinary present: **third-person singular loses its *-s***, and ***be* stays *be***. Everywhere else the subjunctive is invisible, which is why learners often use it correctly by accident and then get caught on the one form that shows.

> Her lawyer insisted that she **say** nothing. (not *says*)
> Policy requires that every visitor **sign** in. (not *signs*)
> The judge ordered that the records **be** sealed.

### Triggers

The subjunctive is licensed by a word of demanding, urging, or requiring — a verb, an adjective, or a noun.

| Class | Members |
|---|---|
| **Verbs** | insist, demand, recommend, suggest, propose, require, request, ask, urge, advise, order, command, direct, instruct, stipulate, move, prefer, arrange, decree |
| **Adjectives** (in *it is …that*) | essential, important, vital, crucial, necessary, imperative, urgent, advisable, desirable, best, fitting, appropriate |
| **Nouns** (in *the … that*) | recommendation, suggestion, demand, insistence, requirement, request, proposal, order, condition, stipulation, motion |

> It is essential that every applicant **submit** three references.
> The board's recommendation is that the office **remain** open.

### The negative and the passive

The negative subjunctive takes **plain *not*** directly before the verb. No *do*, no *does*, no *doesn't*.

> "Don't tell the press."  →  She insisted that we **not tell** the press. ✓
> ✗ *She insisted that we don't tell the press.* ✗ &middot; ✗ *She insisted that we didn't tell the press.* ✗

The passive uses **be** plus the past participle, and the negative passive is *not be*.

> "The invoice has to be paid today."  →  He demanded that the invoice **be paid** that day.
> "Don't let the recording be shared."  →  He asked that the recording **not be shared**.

### No backshift, ever

The subjunctive is tenseless, so a past-tense reporting verb leaves it untouched. This is one of the very few places in reported speech where a past reporting verb changes nothing at all in the clause that follows.

> She **insists** that he **be** present.  →  She **insisted** that he **be** present. (not *were*, not *was*)

### American bare subjunctive vs. British *should*

Both varieties can express the mandative with *should* plus the infinitive. **American English strongly prefers the bare subjunctive; British English has historically preferred *should*,** although the bare form has been gaining ground in Britain for decades.

| | American | British (traditional) |
|---|---|---|
| Preferred | She insisted that he **be** present. | She insisted that he **should be** present. |
| Also possible | She insisted that he **should be** present. (reads as British, or as advice) | She insisted that he **be** present. (increasingly common) |
| Rare / non-American | — | She insisted that he **was** present. (indicative) |

In American writing, *should* in these clauses is not an error, but it is a marked choice, and it introduces a genuine ambiguity: *should* also means "ought to," so *He suggested that she should apply* can read as ordinary advice rather than as a formal proposal. The bare subjunctive has no such second reading. The indicative option — *insisted that he pays*, *recommended that she applies* — is normal in British English and sounds wrong to most American ears in a directive context.

### The two meanings of *insist*, *suggest*, and *demand*

Several of these verbs have a second, non-directive sense: instead of demanding an action, they assert or indicate a fact. **The mood of the *that*-clause is what distinguishes the two senses**, and getting it wrong changes the meaning of the sentence.

| Sentence | Mood | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| She insisted that he **be** present. | subjunctive | She demanded his attendance. |
| She insisted that he **was** present. | indicative, backshifted | She claimed, over objections, that he had been there. |
| I suggest that he **leave**. | subjunctive | I am recommending it. |
| The evidence suggests that he **left**. | indicative, backshifted | The evidence indicates it. |

The directive sense takes the tenseless subjunctive and no backshift; the assertive sense takes an ordinary *that*-clause and backshifts normally, per [Backshift](02-backshift.md).

## American and British forms

Everything in this section describes American English. Directives are a place where general grammar references quietly hand you the British system, so the differences are worth collecting.

| Point | American English | British English |
|---|---|---|
| Mandative clause | She insisted that he **be** present | She insisted that he **should be** present |
| Mandative, indicative | not used in a directive sense | She insisted that he **was** present |
| Proposing to a group | "**Should we** leave?" / "**Let's** leave." | "**Shall we** leave?" is ordinary |
| Negative *let's* | **Let's not** argue | **Don't let's** argue (dated, still heard) |
| *forbid* + preposition | *forbade us **to** enter* / *forbade us **from** entering*, both current | *from* long resisted, now common |
| Obligation reported as a directive | *told me I **had to** sign* | *told me I **must** sign* survives more readily |

The first row is the big one, and it is set out in full [above](#american-bare-subjunctive-vs-british-should). The third matters here because *Shall we…?* is a proposal, and an American reporting one still reports it the *let's* way:

> "**Shall we** take a break?"  →  He **suggested that we take** a break. (not ✗ *He asked whether we shall take a break*)

### Spelling

Three verbs that turn up constantly in reported directives have split spellings, and the British forms circulate widely in ESL material.

| American | British | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *apologize*, *apologized* | *apologise*, *apologised* | *-ize* is the only American spelling; *-ise* is British convention, though Oxford house style uses *-ize* too |
| *inquire*, *inquiry* | *enquire* / *enquiry* | American English uses *inquire* for every sense; British English traditionally reserves *inquire* for formal investigation and uses *enquire* for simply asking |
| *practice* (noun **and** verb) | *practice* (noun), *practise* (verb) | American English spells the verb *practice*; ✗ *practise* in American writing |

> "Tell him you're sorry."  →  She told me **to apologize** to him. (never ✗ *to apologise* in American writing)
> "Find out whether the position is still open."  →  He told me **to inquire** whether the position was still open. (✗ *to enquire*)
> "Practice the scales every day."  →  My teacher told me **to practice** the scales every day. (✗ *to practise*)

Note that *apologize* and *inquire* are not themselves directive verbs — they cannot take an object plus an infinitive. ✗ *He apologized me to wait*, ✗ *She inquired me to sit down*. They report an apology and a question respectively; see [Special cases](08-special-cases.md) and [Reported questions](05-questions.md).

## Directives that were not imperatives

Plenty of commands, requests, and advice never take imperative form in the first place. They are reported the same way, with the reporting verb chosen to match the force.

| Original | Report as directive | Report as statement |
|---|---|---|
| "You must sign here." | He told me **to sign** there. | He said I **had to** sign there. |
| "You have to be there by nine." | She told me **to be** there by nine. | She said I **had to** be there by nine. |
| "You should see a doctor." | She advised me **to see** a doctor. | She said I **should** see a doctor. |
| "You'd better not park there." | He warned me **not to park** there. | He said I'**d better not** park there. |
| "You are not to leave the building." | She forbade us **to leave** the building. | She said we **weren't to** leave the building. |
| "Why don't you call her?" | He suggested **that I call** her. | He asked why I **didn't** call her. |

Both columns are correct; the infinitive version is more compact and foregrounds the force, while the *that*-clause version preserves the exact modal. Pick one and stay with it.

An interrogative used as a request is normally reported as a request, not as a question. Reporting it as a question is technically accurate and usually misleading.

> "Could you email me the slides?"  →  She asked me **to email** her the slides. ✓ natural
> "Could you email me the slides?"  →  She asked me **whether I could email** her the slides. ✓ accurate, sounds evasive

Report it as a genuine question only when the ability, not the action, was really at issue: *She asked whether I could drive a stick shift.*

## Common errors

- ✗ *He suggested me to leave.* → ✓ *He suggested **that I leave**.* / *He suggested **leaving**.* — *Suggest* never takes a person plus an infinitive. The three repairs are the *that*-clause, the bare clause, and the gerund.

- ✗ *She recommended me to see that movie.* → ✓ *She recommended **that I see** that movie.* / *She recommended **seeing** that movie.* — Same structure as *suggest*. Of this meaning group, only *advise* takes the object plus infinitive.

- ✗ *He insisted me to stay.* → ✓ *He insisted **that I stay**.* / *He insisted **on my staying**.* — *Insist* takes a clause or *on* plus a gerund, never an object plus infinitive.

- ✗ *He wanted that I come early.* / *She expects that we finish by five.* (as a directive) → ✓ *He wanted **me to come** early.* / *She expects **us to finish** by five.* — The mirror image of the *suggest* error. *Want* and *expect* take an object plus an infinitive and never a directive *that*-clause.

- ✗ *He told me to don't worry.* → ✓ *He told me **not to** worry.* — There is no auxiliary *do* in a to-infinitive. *Not* goes in front of *to*.

- ✗ *He told me to not worry.* → ✓ *He told me **not to** worry.* — Not ungrammatical, but marked. In reported commands the standard order is *not to*.

- ✗ *He told to sit down.* / *He said me to wait.* → ✓ *He told **me** to sit down.* / *He **told me** to wait.* — *Tell* always requires an object; *say* never takes one. Without an object, *say to* is perfectly good spoken American English: *He said to wait.*

- ✗ *She asked me for to close the window.* / *She asked to me to close the window.* → ✓ *She asked **me to close** the window.* — *For* introduces a noun (*asked for the check*), never an infinitive; and *ask* takes a bare object, with no preposition.

- ✗ *He insisted that she goes to the doctor.* → ✓ *He insisted that she **go** to the doctor.* — Mandative subjunctive: third-person singular loses its *-s*. The indicative here is British at best.

- ✗ *She insisted that he was present at the meeting.* (intending a demand) → ✓ *She insisted that he **be** present at the meeting.* — The indicative flips the meaning to "she claimed he was there."

- ✗ *He demanded that the report is finished by noon.* / *She insisted that we don't tell anyone.* → ✓ *He demanded that the report **be** finished by noon.* / *She insisted that we **not tell** anyone.* — The passive subjunctive is *be* plus the participle, and the negative is bare *not*, with no *do*-support.

- ✗ *The committee recommended that the deadline was extended.* → ✓ *The committee recommended that the deadline **be** extended.* — No backshift into the subjunctive; a past reporting verb leaves it alone.

- ✗ *He suggested us to take a break.* → ✓ *He suggested **that we take** a break.* — This is how *Let's take a break* is reported. *He told us to take a break* is grammatical but reports an order, not a proposal.

- ✗ *The policy prohibits employees to accept gifts.* → ✓ *The policy prohibits employees **from accepting** gifts.* — *Prohibit*, *prevent*, *ban*, and *discourage* take *from* plus a gerund and never an infinitive.

- ✗ *He warned us from going in.* / *She advised me from signing.* → ✓ *He warned us **against going** in.* / *She advised me **against signing**.* — *Warn* and *advise* pattern with *against*, not *from*, even though they sit next to the *from* verbs in meaning. Both also take the plain infinitive: *He warned us not to go in.*

- ✗ *She asked me to water her plants while I'm gone.* → ✓ *She asked me to water her plants **while she was** gone.* — A half-shifted report is worse than an unshifted one. The infinitive has no tense to move, but the pronouns and the finite clause riding inside the command still have to rotate together.

- ✗ *He promised me to be there.* / *He offered me to drive.* → ✓ *He promised me **that he would be** there.* / *He offered **to drive**.* — *Promise* and *offer* do not take an object plus an infinitive; use a *that*-clause or a plain noun object (*He offered me a ride*).

- ✗ *She was suggested to reapply.* → ✓ *She **was advised** to reapply.* / *It **was suggested that she** reapply.* — A verb that takes no object in the active cannot form this passive.

## Quick reference

**The pattern:** reporting verb + object + *to*-infinitive. Negative: *not* + *to*.

> "Sit down."  →  He told me **to sit down**. &middot; "Don't worry."  →  He told me **not to worry**.

**Backshift:** none in the infinitive, ever. Ordinary backshift in any finite clause riding inside it — and optional there, as usual, when that clause is still true or still upcoming. Pronouns, demonstratives, and time and place words rotate either way.

| Original type | Report |
|---|---|
| Order | He **told me to** wait. / He **ordered me to** wait. |
| Request | She **asked me to** wait. |
| Request for a thing | She **asked for** the check. |
| Request to act yourself | She **asked to** leave early. |
| Advice (object allowed) | He **advised me to** wait. |
| Advice (no object allowed) | He **suggested that I wait** / **suggested waiting**. |
| Warning, reminder | He **warned me not to** wait. / She **reminded me to** wait. |
| Prohibition | She **forbade us to** enter. / She **prohibited us from** entering. |
| Offer, promise, refusal | He **offered / promised / refused to** wait. |
| *Let's* | He **suggested that we** wait. |
| Non-negotiable demand | She **insisted that he be** present. |

**Object required:** tell, order, command, remind, warn, advise, persuade, convince, urge, forbid, instruct, invite, encourage, permit, allow, require, force, get

**Object impossible:** suggest, recommend, propose, insist, demand, say, mention, explain, announce, complain

**Object optional, and it changes who acts:** ask, beg, want, expect

**No object — the speaker is the one who acts:** offer, promise, refuse, agree, threaten, volunteer, swear

**Takes *from* + *-ing*:** prevent, prohibit, ban, bar, stop, discourage, dissuade, deter, restrain — but *warn* and *advise* take **against**

**Mandative subjunctive:** base form, all persons, no *-s*, no backshift, negative with bare *not*.

> insisted that he **be** &middot; recommended that she **apply** &middot; asked that the phones **be turned off** &middot; insisted that we **not tell** anyone

## Practice

**A. Report each directive, using the verb in parentheses.**

1. "Don't touch the wet paint." (warn)
2. "Could you water my plants while I'm gone?" (ask)
3. "Let's split the check." (suggest)
4. "The report has to be on my desk by nine." (insist)
5. "Call me when you get to the hotel." (tell)

**B. Correct the error.**

6. She suggested me to take the earlier train.
7. He told me to not open the envelope until Monday.
8. The lease prohibits tenants to sublet the apartment.
9. My advisor recommended that I applies for the fellowship.
10. He said me to leave the key under the mat.
11. My manager wanted that I finish the audit by Friday.
12. The inspector warned us from entering the building.

**C. Explain the difference.**

13. *She asked to leave early.* vs. *She asked me to leave early.*
14. *He insisted that she be at the hearing.* vs. *He insisted that she was at the hearing.*

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **He warned me not to touch the wet paint.** — Negative imperative, so *not* goes directly before *to*. *Warn* requires an object; ✗ *He warned not to touch* is incomplete. *He warned me about the wet paint* is also fine but reports a caution rather than a directive.

2. **She asked me to water her plants while she was gone.** — *Could you…?* is a request, not a real question, so it is reported with *ask* plus an object plus an infinitive. Note the two shifts that survive: *my* → *her*, and *I'm* → *she was*.

3. **He suggested that we split the check.** (or **He suggested splitting the check.**) — *Let's* is a proposal that includes the speaker, so *tell* is wrong. *Suggest* takes no object: ✗ *He suggested us to split the check* is the classic error. The base form *split* is the subjunctive, invisible here because the subject is *we*.

4. **He insisted that the report be on his desk by nine.** — *Insist* takes no object, so ✗ *He insisted me to have the report* is out. The subjunctive *be* is required, and it does not backshift to *was* even though *insisted* is past. *He insisted that the report was on his desk* would mean something entirely different — that he claimed it was already there.

5. **He told me to call him when I got to the hotel.** — The infinitive *to call* has no tense to shift, but the *when*-clause is finite and backshifts by the ordinary rules, and *me* → *him*. If the arrival is still ahead of you, **He told me to call him when I get to the hotel** is equally good in speech — an embedded clause that is still true or still upcoming is one of the standard optional-backshift cases.

6. **She suggested that I take the earlier train.** (or **She suggested taking the earlier train**, or **She suggested I take the earlier train**.) — *Suggest* never takes a person plus an infinitive. Note that the base form is *take*, not *takes* and not *took*.

7. **He told me not to open the envelope until Monday.** — *To not open* is not ungrammatical, but it is marked; the standard order in a reported command is *not to*.

8. **The lease prohibits tenants from subletting the apartment.** — *Prohibit* takes *from* plus a gerund and never an infinitive. *Forbid* is the verb that allows both *to sublet* and *from subletting*.

9. **My advisor recommended that I apply for the fellowship.** — Two problems: *recommend* takes no object plus infinitive, and the clause verb is the bare subjunctive. *Applies* is an indicative intruder. *My advisor recommended applying for the fellowship* also works.

10. **He told me to leave the key under the mat.** (or **He said to leave the key under the mat.**) — *Say* cannot take a personal object. With no object, *say to* + infinitive is normal American speech; with an object, the verb has to be *tell*.

11. **My manager wanted me to finish the audit by Friday.** — *Want* takes an object plus an infinitive and never a *that*-clause, in either mood: ✗ *wanted that I finish*, ✗ *wanted that I finished*. This is the error that mirrors *He suggested me to leave* — same learner, opposite direction.

12. **The inspector warned us against entering the building.** (or **The inspector warned us not to enter the building.**) — *Warn* takes *against* plus a gerund, or an object plus an infinitive. *From* belongs to *prohibit*, *prevent*, *ban*, and *discourage*.

13. In *She asked to leave early*, **she** is the one leaving — *ask* with no object means the speaker is requesting something for herself, so the original was "Can I leave early?" In *She asked me to leave early*, **I** am the one leaving, and the original was "Could you leave early?" The presence or absence of the object flips the subject of the infinitive.

14. *He insisted that she **be** at the hearing* is the mandative subjunctive: he demanded her attendance, and whether she came is left open. *He insisted that she **was** at the hearing* is the indicative, backshifted from *She is at the hearing*: he was asserting a fact, probably against someone's denial. *Insist*, *suggest*, and *demand* all carry this double life, and mood is the only thing that distinguishes the two readings.
