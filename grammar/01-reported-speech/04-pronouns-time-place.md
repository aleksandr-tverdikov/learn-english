# Pronouns, time, and place shifts

> How the pointing words — *I*, *you*, *my*, *now*, *today*, *tomorrow*, *here*, *this*, *Mom*, *come*, *bring* — are recalculated when a quotation is re-anchored to the reporter's own person, time, and place.

[← Reported speech](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

[Backshift](02-backshift.md) handles the verb. This section handles everything else — and "everything else" is a single coherent group of words called **deictics** (pronounced *DIKE-tics*, from Greek *deiknynai*, "to point"). A deictic word does not carry a fixed meaning. It carries a set of instructions for locating something relative to whoever is talking, whenever they are talking, and wherever they are standing. The word *I* means "the person now speaking." The word *tomorrow* means "the day after the day on which this is said." The word *here* means "the place where the speaker is."

That is why they have to be recalculated. When you report someone's words, the sentence moves: a new person is speaking, at a new time, in a new place. The old pointing instructions now point somewhere else.

> "**I** was **here yesterday**," Marcus said.  →  Marcus said **he** had been **there the day before**.

Every word in that transformation is doing the same job — preserving what the original sentence pointed at, by changing the word that points.

---

## The principle: recalculate, do not substitute

Most textbooks teach these changes as a substitution table: *today* becomes *that day*, *here* becomes *there*, *I* becomes *he*. The tables in this section are all here and all complete, because they are a useful shortcut. But they are a shortcut, and the shortcut fails often enough that you need the reasoning underneath it.

The reasoning is one question, asked separately of every pointing word:

> **If I use this word now, from where I am standing, to the person I am talking to, does it still point at the same thing it pointed at originally?**
>
> If **yes**, leave the word alone.  
> If **no**, replace it with the word that does point there.

Run that question on *here* in the example above. Marcus said *here* while standing in the office; I am reporting it at home; *here* now means my kitchen. Different thing — so it has to change to *there*. Now run it on *today* in a sentence I am reporting four minutes after it was spoken, in the same room. *Today* still means the same calendar day. Same thing — so it stays.

This is why *"the shifts are optional"* is a bad summary and *"the shifts are automatic"* is a worse one. Nothing is optional and nothing is automatic; the word simply has to keep pointing where it pointed.

### The three dials turn independently

A reported sentence has three separate things going on, and learners routinely fuse them into one rule.

| Dial | Controlled by | Example of it turning |
|---|---|---|
| **Backshift** (verb tense) | The tense of the reporting verb, plus whether the fact is still true | *am* → *was* |
| **Person** (pronouns) | Who is speaking now, and who they are speaking to | *I* → *he* |
| **Time and place** (adverbs, demonstratives) | When and where the report is made | *today* → *that day*, *here* → *there* |

They do not move together. Person shift can apply with no backshift at all:

> "**I**'m on **my** way," Danielle says.  →  Danielle says **she**'s on **her** way.

The reporting verb is present, so nothing backshifts — but *I* and *my* still had to be recalculated, because Danielle is not the one talking now. And backshift can apply with no time shift at all:

> "**I'm** stuck in traffic, but **I'll** be there **tonight**," he said an hour ago.  →  He said he **was** stuck in traffic but **would** be there **tonight**.

Both verbs moved back a step and *I* became *he*, but *tonight* is still the same night, so it stays exactly where it was. Shift it to *that night* and you have told your listener that the arrival is no longer this evening — a factual error produced by a grammatical reflex.

Person shift is the least negotiable of the three. Time and place shifts depend entirely on when and where you are standing, and backshift is looser still — see [Backshift is available, not compulsory](02-backshift.md#backshift-is-available-not-compulsory). See [Backshift](02-backshift.md) for the first dial; the rest of this file is the second and third.

## Pronoun shifts

### Pronouns are recalculated, not shifted

The single most useful correction to the standard teaching: pronouns do not have a fixed destination. *I* does not "become *he*." *I* becomes **whatever word now refers to the person who did the original speaking**. Sometimes that word is *he*. Sometimes it is *she*, or *they*, or *you* — and sometimes it is still *I*.

> Marcus, to Priya: "**I** finished **my** report."
>
> Marcus reporting his own words:  →  I said **I** had finished **my** report.  
> Priya reporting them:  →  Marcus said **he** had finished **his** report.

Same original sentence, two different results, and neither one is a mistake. The pronoun tracks the person, not the form.

### The first-person forms

The person who spoke originally. If that person is the one now doing the reporting, nothing changes; otherwise everything changes to third person. (If the original speaker is the one being *spoken to* now, first person becomes second: *You said you'd finished your report.*)

| Original form | Class | Original speaker reports it | Someone else reports it | Reported *to* the original speaker |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | subject | I | he / she / they | you |
| me | object | me | him / her / them | you |
| my | possessive determiner | my | his / her / their | your |
| mine | possessive pronoun | mine | his / hers / theirs | yours |
| myself | reflexive | myself | himself / herself / themselves | yourself |
| we | subject | we | they | you |
| us | object | us | them | you |
| our | possessive determiner | our | their | your |
| ours | possessive pronoun | ours | theirs | yours |
| ourselves | reflexive | ourselves | themselves | yourselves |

> "**We**'ve made **our** decision," the committee chair said.  →  The chair said **they** had made **their** decision.

> "I did it **myself**," Ellen insisted.  →  Ellen insisted **she** had done it **herself**.

The *we* row assumes the reporter was outside the group. If the reporter was inside it, *we* stays *we* — that is the inclusive/exclusive problem, and it gets its own subsection below.

### The second-person forms

The person who was spoken *to*. This is where learners lose the thread, because *you* has three possible destinations depending on who is now in the room.

| Original form | Class | The original addressee reports it | Reported *to* the original addressee | Reported by and to third parties |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| you | subject | I | you | he / she / they |
| you | object | me | you | him / her / them |
| your | possessive determiner | my | your | his / her / their |
| yours | possessive pronoun | mine | yours | his / hers / theirs |
| yourself | reflexive | myself | yourself | himself / herself / themselves |
| you (plural) | subject or object | we / us | you | they / them |
| your (plural) | possessive determiner | our | your | their |
| yourselves | reflexive | ourselves | yourselves | themselves |

> Coach, to Wes: "**You**'ve earned **your** spot."
>
> Wes reporting it:  →  Coach said **I**'d earned **my** spot.  
> Someone reporting it to Wes:  →  Coach said **you**'d earned **your** spot.  
> Two teammates discussing it:  →  Coach said **Wes** had earned **his** spot.

The spoken American plural-you forms — *you guys*, *y'all*, *you all*, *you two* — recalculate exactly like plural *you*, and the plural marker itself simply disappears in the report:

> "Are **you guys** coming?" he asked.  →  He asked whether **we** were coming.

### The third-person forms

Third-person pronouns usually survive untouched, because a third party stays a third party. They change only when the person they refer to has walked into the conversation — as the reporter, or as the person being reported to.

| Original | Class | Reported by the person referred to | Reported *to* the person referred to | Everyone else |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| he / she | subject | I | you | unchanged |
| him / her | object | me | you | unchanged |
| his / her | possessive determiner | my | your | unchanged |
| his / hers | possessive pronoun | mine | yours | unchanged |
| himself / herself | reflexive | myself | yourself | unchanged |
| they | subject | we | you | unchanged |
| them | object | us | you | unchanged |
| their | possessive determiner | our | your | unchanged |
| theirs | possessive pronoun | ours | yours | unchanged |
| themselves | reflexive | ourselves | yourselves | unchanged |
| it / its / itself | — | see below | — | unchanged |

The *they* rows assume plural *they*, with the reporter inside the group. **Singular *they*** behaves like *he* and *she* instead: the person referred to reports it as *I / me / my / mine / myself*.

*It* has no natural first- or second-person counterpart, with one systematic exception: when *it* refers to an organization, someone inside that organization reports the same words with *we*.

> The company said **it** would appeal.  →  *(its spokesperson)* We said **we** would appeal.

Now the ordinary case, with all three destinations in one place:

> Nina, to Owen: "**She** never answered **my** email." (*she* = Bethany)
>
> Owen reporting to a colleague:  →  Nina said **she** had never answered **her** email.  *(ambiguous — see below)*  
> Owen reporting to Bethany:  →  Nina said **you** had never answered **her** email.  
> Bethany reporting it herself:  →  Nina said **I** had never answered **her** email.

### The full worked example: *"I saw you"*

Three people: **Ben** speaks, **Dana** listens, **Chris** is a bystander. Ben says to Dana:

> "**I** saw **you** at the concert."

That one sentence can be reported six ways, and the pronouns land differently every time, because each row pairs a different reporter with a different audience.

| Who reports it | Who they report it to | Reported version |
|---|---|---|
| Ben | Chris | I told Dana **I**'d seen **her** at the concert. |
| Ben | Dana | I said **I**'d seen **you** at the concert. |
| Dana | Chris | Ben said **he**'d seen **me** at the concert. |
| Dana | Ben | You said **you**'d seen **me** at the concert. |
| Chris | Dana | Ben said **he**'d seen **you** at the concert. |
| Chris | Elena (a fourth party) | Ben said **he**'d seen **Dana** at the concert. |

Two things fall out of this table that no substitution rule can give you.

**One: sometimes nothing changes at all.** In row 2, Ben is reporting his own words back to the same person he originally said them to. *I* is still Ben and *you* is still Dana. The only change in the whole sentence is the backshift on the verb.

**Two: sometimes the pronoun has to become a name.** In the last row, Chris cannot say *he'd seen her* to Elena, who has no idea who *her* is. When the report crosses into an audience that does not share the original context, a pronoun frequently has to be upgraded to a proper noun.

### Possessive determiners and possessive pronouns

Both possessive series follow the person exactly as the subject and object pronouns do — the determiner sits in front of a noun (*my keys*), the pronoun stands alone (*mine*). See [Pronouns](../../parts-of-speech/02-pronouns/README.md) for the two paradigms in full, and [Possessive determiners](../../parts-of-speech/09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) for the determiner series on its own.

> "**My** flight got canceled," Rosa said.  →  Rosa said **her** flight had gotten canceled.

> "Take **my** car — **yours** is still in the shop," he told me.  →  He told me to take **his** car because **mine** was still in the shop.

> "The corner office is **ours**, not **theirs**," the partners said.  →  The partners said the corner office was **theirs**, not **the other firm's**.

Note that last one. *Ours* → *theirs* is straightforward, but the original *theirs* cannot also stay *theirs* — the two would fuse and the sentence would collapse into nonsense. When a shift would create a collision, one of the two has to be spelled out with a noun. This is the same repair as the name-upgrade above.

### Reflexive pronouns

Reflexives shift with whatever they refer back to — normally the subject of their own clause, though an object can bind one too (*She told Ben about **himself***). Once you have gotten the antecedent right, the reflexive follows automatically.

| Original | Becomes (third-party report) |
|---|---|
| myself | himself / herself / themselves |
| yourself | myself / yourself / himself / herself / themselves |
| yourselves | ourselves / yourselves / themselves |
| ourselves | themselves |
| himself / herself / itself / themselves | usually unchanged |

> "I taught **myself** to code," Aaron said.  →  Aaron said he had taught **himself** to code.

> "You should be proud of **yourself**," she told me.  →  She told me I should be proud of **myself**.

> "Help **yourselves** to the coffee," he said to us.  →  He told us to help **ourselves** to the coffee.

Intensive pronouns — the identical forms used for emphasis (*the senator herself*) — shift the same way: *"I called them myself"* → *He said he had called them **himself**.*

### Singular *they*

When the original speaker's gender is unknown or irrelevant, first person shifts to singular *they*, with *their*, *theirs*, and *themself* or *themselves*.

> A caller said, "**I**'ve already sent in **my** paperwork."  →  A caller said **they** had already sent in **their** paperwork.

Both *themself* and *themselves* are in use for the singular. *Themselves* is the safer choice in edited American prose; *themself* is increasingly common and is no longer worth flagging as an error in ordinary writing.

Note that singular *they* takes plural agreement even when the reference is to one person, so the backshifted verb is *were*, not *was*:

> A student said, "**I'm** not registered yet."  →  A student said **they were** not registered yet.

### *We*: does it include you or not?

*We* is the one pronoun where the recalculation genuinely requires you to know the facts. English does not mark the difference between **inclusive** *we* (speaker + addressee) and **exclusive** *we* (speaker + others, not you), so you have to work out from context whether the reporter was inside the group.

> Manager, to the whole team including me: "**We**'re moving the deadline."  
>  →  She said **we** were moving the deadline.  *(I was in the group)*

> Manager, to another department: "**We**'re moving the deadline."  
>  →  She said **they** were moving the deadline.  *(I was not in the group)*

Getting this wrong changes the facts, not just the grammar. If you cannot tell which *we* was meant, name the group: *She said the engineering team was moving the deadline.*

### Generic *you* does not become *I*

*You* meaning "people in general" is not a real second person and does not participate in person shift at all. It either stays as *you* or is replaced with *one*, which in American English is distinctly formal and rare outside edited prose.

> "**You** can't park **here** after six," the attendant said.  →  The attendant said **you** couldn't park **there** after six.

Not ✗ *The attendant said I couldn't park there after six* — unless the attendant really was talking about me specifically. Note that *here* still shifts to *there* in that sentence: the generic *you* is frozen, but the place deictic beside it is not.

The same immunity covers idiomatic *you* in fixed expressions: *"You never know," he said* → *He said you never know.* The verb does not backshift either, because the statement was never anchored to the moment of speaking in the first place.

### Names, kinship terms, and *home*

Not every speaker-relative expression is a pronoun. Kinship terms used as names — *Mom*, *Dad*, *Grandma*, *Uncle Ray* — point outward from the speaker, and so does bare *home*. They obey the same test as everything else in this file: does the word still point at the same person or place?

> "**Mom** called **me** twice," my sister said.  →  My sister said **Mom** had called **her** twice.

*Mom* stays, because my sister's mother is my mother too. The reference has not moved. Change the speaker and it does:

> "**Mom** called **me** twice," Priya said.  →  Priya said **her mother** had called **her** twice.

Bare *home* usually needs no repair, because *home* means "the home of the person the sentence is about," and that person has already been shifted:

> "**I** left **my** charger at **home**," Dev said.  →  Dev said he had left **his** charger at **home**.  *(= Dev's home)*

But *home* meaning "here at our place" is a genuine place deictic and does move:

> "Come see us at **home** sometime," they said.  →  They invited me to visit them **at their place** sometime.

Definite descriptions that rely on shared context — *the boss*, *the office*, *the usual spot* — take the same repair as the name-upgrade above. If the new audience does not share the context, spell the referent out.

### The ambiguity problem

Person shift systematically destroys information. The original *I* and *my* are unambiguous — there is only one speaker. The reported *he* and *his* are not.

> "**I** left **my** phone in **his** car," Devon said.  →  Devon said **he** had left **his** phone in **his** car.

Three instances of *his* and *he*, referring to two different men. The original was perfectly clear; the report is unreadable. English has no grammatical fix for this, so you repair it lexically:

> Devon said he had left **his own** phone in **Elliot's** car.  
> Devon said he had left his phone in **the other guy's** car.

Watch for this any time the reported clause contains a third-person referent alongside a shifted first person. It is the single most common way a technically correct report turns out to be useless.

## Time shifts

If the report is made at a different time from the original speech, every time word anchored to the moment of speaking has to be re-anchored to the moment being reported.

### The full table

| Original (anchored to *now*) | Reported (anchored to *then*) |
|---|---|
| now | then / at that time / right then |
| right now | right then / at that very moment |
| at the moment | at that moment / at the time |
| just now | just then / a moment earlier |
| currently / at present | at that time / then |
| so far / up to now | up to then / until that point |
| today | that day |
| tonight | that night |
| this morning / this afternoon / this evening | that morning / that afternoon / that evening |
| this week / this month / this year | that week / that month / that year |
| this weekend | that weekend |
| these days | those days / at that time |
| nowadays | at that time / in those days |
| yesterday | the day before / the previous day |
| yesterday morning | the previous morning / the morning before |
| last night | the night before / the previous night |
| the day before yesterday | two days before / two days earlier |
| the other day | a few days before / a few days earlier |
| last week / last month / last year | the week / month / year before, the previous week / month / year |
| last weekend | the previous weekend / the weekend before |
| last Friday | the previous Friday / the Friday before |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day / the day after |
| tomorrow morning | the next morning / the following morning |
| tomorrow afternoon | the next afternoon / the following afternoon |
| tomorrow night | the next night / the following night |
| the day after tomorrow | two days later / two days after that |
| this time tomorrow | at that time the next day |
| next week / next month / next year | the following week / month / year, the next week / month / year |
| next weekend | the following weekend |
| next Friday | the following Friday / the Friday after |
| this coming Monday | that coming Monday / the following Monday |
| in an hour / in three days | an hour later / three days later |
| an hour ago | an hour before / an hour earlier |
| two days ago | two days before / two days earlier |
| a week ago today | a week before that day |
| long ago | long before |
| soon | soon afterward / shortly afterward |
| recently | shortly before / not long before |

### *Now* → *then*

> "**I'm** busy **now**," she said.  →  She said she **was** busy **then**.

*Then* is thin and can be genuinely unclear, so American speakers often prefer *at that time*, *right then*, *at that moment*, or simply drop the word:

> She said she **was busy at that moment**.  
> She said she **was busy**.

Dropping it is usually the best option. The backshifted verb already locates the state in the past; *then* frequently adds nothing.

One *now* does not shift: the conjunction *now that*, which means "given that," not "at this moment."

> "**Now that** I have a car, I can visit more," Kim said.  →  Kim said that **now that** she had a car, she could visit more.

### *Today*, *tonight*, *this morning*

> "The results come out **today**," he said.  →  He said the results **came** out **that day**.

> "I'm working late **tonight**," Marcus said.  →  Marcus said he **was** working late **that night**.

> "I called you **this morning**," she said.  →  She said she **had** called me **that morning**.

### *Yesterday* and *tomorrow*

> "I mailed it **yesterday**," he said.  →  He said he **had** mailed it **the day before**.

> "**I'll** call you **tomorrow**," she said.  →  She said she **would** call me **the next day**.

*The day before* and *the previous day* are interchangeable, as are *the next day*, *the following day*, and *the day after*. *The previous day* and *the following day* read slightly more formal and are the usual choice in written reports; *the day before* and *the next day* dominate in speech.

There is one trap with *the next day*. In casual American speech it sometimes means "tomorrow" rather than "the day after the day being reported," and that can leave a sentence genuinely ambiguous. *The following day* never has that reading, which is a good reason to prefer it whenever the timeline matters.

### *Last* and *next*

> "We shipped it **last week**," they said.  →  They said they **had** shipped it **the week before**.

> "The lease is up **next month**," he told us.  →  He told us the lease **was** up **the following month**.

Named days need care, because *last Friday* and *next Friday* are ambiguous even in direct speech. If the exact day matters, replace the deictic phrase with the date outright — see [Prefer absolute references in writing](#prefer-absolute-references-in-writing) below.

### *Ago* → *before* / *earlier*

*Ago* counts backward from the moment of speaking. *Before* and *earlier* count backward from a reference point in the past — which is exactly what a reported clause needs.

> "I moved here **three years ago**," she said.  →  She said she **had** moved there **three years before**.

> "He left **ten minutes ago**," the receptionist said.  →  The receptionist said he **had** left **ten minutes earlier**.

*Earlier* is the more natural of the two in American speech; *before* can sound stilted with short intervals (*ten minutes before* invites the question "before what?").

**Where the textbook rule breaks.** When the report follows the original closely and the interval is still being measured from right now, Americans keep *ago*, and it is not an error:

> Nadia just called — she said she **landed twenty minutes ago**.

The interval really is twenty minutes from the present moment, so *ago* is the accurate word and *before* would be wrong. Note that the verb has not backshifted either — *landed*, not *had landed* — which is the ordinary spoken pattern and the subject of the subsection two below. This is the same logic that governs every other item in this section: the word stays when it still points at the right thing.

Also note that *ago* attached to the **reporting** verb never shifts, because it is outside the quotation entirely: *He told me **an hour ago** that he was leaving.*

### *These days*

> "**These days** I hardly drive," he said.  →  He said that **in those days** he hardly **drove**.

*Those days* almost always needs *in* in front of it to sound natural, and even then it can read as oddly literary. In practice most American speakers rewrite it:

> He said he hardly drove **anymore**.  
> He said that **at that time** he hardly drove.

### The adverb and the verb must agree

A backshifted verb and an unshifted time word contradict each other, and vice versa. This is the most common mechanical error in the whole topic.

| Wrong | Right | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ He said he **will** call me **the next day**. | He said he **would** call me the next day. | Unshifted verb with a shifted adverb |
| ✗ He said he **would** call me **tomorrow**. *(reported a week later)* | He said he would call me **the next day**. | Shifted verb with an adverb still anchored to now |
| ✗ She said she **is** busy **that day**. | She said she **was** busy that day. | Same mismatch, present tense |
| ✗ They said they **had** finished it **yesterday**. *(quoted a month ago)* | They said they had finished it **the day before**. | The original *yesterday* is not this *yesterday* |

The second and fourth rows are only errors given the stated context. Reported on the same day, both original sentences are perfectly correct — which is the subject of the next major section.

### The past simple often stays put

The textbook says the past simple backshifts to the past perfect. In ordinary American speech it usually does not, and nobody hears the plain past as an error — the full treatment is in [Past simple often stays past simple](03-no-backshift.md#6-past-simple-often-stays-past-simple), with the structural reason in [Backshift](02-backshift.md#the-floor-is-also-why-past-simple-often-stays-put). It belongs here because it changes what "the verb and the adverb must agree" means in practice.

> "I **mailed** it **yesterday**," he said.  
>  →  He said he **had** mailed it **the day before**.  *(edited writing)*  
>  →  He said he **mailed** it **the day before**.  *(ordinary speech, equally correct)*

The tense is a stylistic choice. **The adverb is not.** Both versions shift *yesterday* to *the day before*, because the day really has changed, and the deictic is reporting a fact about the calendar rather than a preference about register.

> ✗ He said he mailed it **yesterday**.  *(reported a week later — wrong in both registers)*

The same asymmetry runs through the still-true cases. *Rachel said she's meeting the client today* leaves the verb alone because the meeting has not happened yet; that is the optional half. If you are reporting from a different city, *here* still has to become *there*, and that half was never optional. See [Optional backshift](03-no-backshift.md#3-optional-backshift-the-statement-is-still-true) for where the tense choice is genuinely free.

## Place and demonstratives

### *Here* → *there*

> "I've been waiting **here** for an hour," he said.  →  He said he **had** been waiting **there** for an hour.

*There* is even vaguer than *then*, and naming the place is usually a real improvement:

> He said he'd been waiting **at the airport** for an hour.

### The place table

| Original | Reported (reporter somewhere else) |
|---|---|
| here | there |
| over here | over there |
| right here | right there / at that exact spot |
| in here / out here / up here / down here | in there / out there / up there / down there |
| back here | back there |
| this way | that way |
| this side | that side |
| this place | that place |
| this room / this building | that room / that building |
| this city / this state / this country | that city / that state / that country |
| around here | around there / in that area |
| here in Denver | there in Denver / in Denver |
| home *(= the subject's home)* | home — already anchored to the subject |
| home *(= "our place")* | their place / my place, whichever now applies |
| nearby / next door / upstairs / across the street | usually unchanged |
| abroad / overseas / out of town | unchanged |

The last two rows are the ones worth understanding rather than memorizing. *Nearby*, *next door*, *upstairs*, and *across the street* are **relative** rather than deictic: they anchor to whatever place the sentence is already talking about, not to the speaker, so a change of speaker leaves them pointing where they were. *He said there was a diner nearby* means "near the place under discussion," which is what the original meant too.

### *This* → *that*, *these* → *those*

Demonstratives point in space, in time, and in the flow of the conversation, and all three uses shift. See [Demonstrative determiners](../../parts-of-speech/09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md) for the underlying two-way near/far system.

| Original | Reported |
|---|---|
| this (determiner) | that |
| these (determiner) | those |
| this (pronoun) | that |
| these (pronoun) | those |
| this one | that one |

**Pointing at an object:**

> "**This** seat is taken," she said.  →  She said **that** seat **was** taken.

**Pointing at a time:**

> "I'll finish it **this week**," he promised.  →  He promised **he'd** finish it **that week**.

**Pointing at the conversation itself:**

> "**This** is ridiculous," Karen said.  →  Karen said **that was** ridiculous.

That last pattern collides with the complementizer *that*, which produces the ugly but grammatical *Karen said that that was ridiculous*. In practice, drop one of them — either the complementizer (*Karen said that was ridiculous*) or the demonstrative (*Karen said the whole thing was ridiculous*).

There is one *this* that does not shift: the storytelling *this* that introduces a brand-new character in casual speech, where it works like *a* rather than like a pointer.

> "So **this guy** walks up to me," he said.  →  He said **this guy** had walked up to him.

### *Come* → *go*, *bring* → *take*

These two verb pairs are deictic in exactly the way *here* and *there* are. *Come* and *bring* describe movement **toward** the speaker's location; *go* and *take* describe movement **away from** it. Move the speaker and you move the verb. See [Verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md) for the wider behavior of these two pairs.

| Original | Reported (reporter not at the destination) |
|---|---|
| come | go |
| come back | go back / return |
| bring | take |
| come here | go there |
| bring it here | take it there |

> "**Come** to my office at three," he said.  →  He told me to **go** to his office at three.

> "**Bring** the file **here** tomorrow," she said.  →  She told me to **take** the file **there the next day**.

> "I'll **come** get you at eight," Dad said.  →  Dad said he **would go** get me at eight.

That last one is worth pausing on. It is correct only if the reporter is no longer at the pickup location. If I am reporting from my own apartment, where Dad was going to collect me, *come* stays: *Dad said he would **come** get me at eight.*

**Where *come* stays.** *Come* and *bring* survive whenever the reporter is at, or identifies with, the destination:

> "I'll **come** to the party," Luis said.  →  Luis said he **would come** to the party.  *(said by the host)*  
>  →  Luis said he **would go** to the party.  *(said by someone not attending)*

Some fixed expressions also freeze *come*: *come true*, *come on*, *come to think of it*, *how come*, *come in handy*. These are not deictic and never become *go*.

## When the shifts do *not* happen

This is the part the tables cannot capture, and the part that separates reports that sound like English from reports that sound like exercises. **The shifts happen only when the report has actually moved.** If you are reporting on the same day, *today* stays. If you are standing in the same room, *here* stays.

### The same-day, same-place test

The original in each pair below is identical. Only the reporter's position in time and space differs.

> **Original:** "I'm meeting the client **here today**," Rachel said.

| When and where you report it | Reported version |
|---|---|
| An hour later, same office | Rachel said she**'s** meeting the client **here today**. |
| Same day, from somewhere else | Rachel said she **was** meeting the client **there** today. |
| A week later, same office | Rachel said she **was** meeting the client **here that day**. |
| A week later, somewhere else | Rachel said she **was** meeting the client **there that day**. |

Every one of those four is correct in its own context, and every one of them is wrong in the other three.

Two details in that table are easy to get wrong. In the first row the verb has not backshifted at all, because the meeting is still ahead of the reporter — that is the still-true option, not sloppiness. And in the last two rows the verb is *was meeting*, not ✗ *had been meeting*: the present progressive backshifts to the **past progressive**. The past perfect progressive is the backshifted form of *I've been meeting* or *I was meeting*, and using it here would claim the original said something it did not.

### Pairs both ways

**Time — reported the same day vs. later:**

> "I have a doctor's appointment **today**," Jonah said.  
>  →  *(same day)* Jonah said he **has** a doctor's appointment **today**.  
>  →  *(the following week)* Jonah said he **had** a doctor's appointment **that day**.

> "**I'll** email you **tomorrow**," she said.  
>  →  *(reported the same day)* She said she **would** email me **tomorrow**.  
>  →  *(reported three days later)* She said she **would** email me **the next day**.

**Place — reported in the same room vs. elsewhere:**

> "It's freezing in **here**," Tom complained.  
>  →  *(still in the room)* Tom said it **was** freezing in **here**.  
>  →  *(back at home)* Tom said it **was** freezing in **there**.

**Demonstratives — the object still in front of you vs. not:**

> "**This** is the one I want," she said.  
>  →  *(the item still on the counter between you)* She said **this** is the one she **wants**.  
>  →  *(back at the office)* She said **that was** the one she **wanted**.

**Motion verbs — reporting at the destination vs. away from it:**

> "I'm **coming** over at six," Alicia said.  
>  →  *(said by someone at the house she's coming to)* Alicia said she **was coming** over at six.  
>  →  *(said by someone elsewhere)* Alicia said she **was going** over at six.

### Immediate reporting

The most common real-world context for reporting speech is relaying something that was said seconds ago, in the same place, to someone standing right there — across a room, over a phone, through a doorway. Almost nothing shifts.

> The manager, thirty seconds ago: "I need **these** files by **tonight**, and I want **you two** to leave **them here**."  
> Relayed to your coworker at the next desk:  →  She said **she** needs **these** files by **tonight**, and **she** wants **us** to leave **them here**.

Only the person deictics moved, and even the verb stayed in the present. Applying the full textbook transformation to a sentence like that — *She said she needed those files by that night and wanted us to leave them there* — produces something no American would say in that situation, and it actively misinforms the listener about which files, which night, and which desk. The shifts exist to preserve reference. When reference has not moved, applying them destroys it.

### Prefer absolute references in writing

For anything written down and read later — reporting, minutes, legal summaries, documentation — the professional habit is to skip the relative expression entirely and name the actual date, time, or place. It is the only version that is still correct a year later.

> "We'll ship **next Friday**," the vendor said on **Tuesday, March 3**.  
>  →  *(relative)* The vendor said they would ship **the following Friday**.  
>  →  *(absolute)* The vendor said they would ship **on March 13**.

Writing the date also forces you to resolve *next Friday*, which is the real service. Spoken on a Tuesday, it could mean March 6 or March 13, and the relative version hands that ambiguity straight to the reader.

> "I was **here** all night," he said.  
>  →  *(absolute)* He said he had been **at the warehouse** all night.

Journalists do this by default, and it is worth adopting for the same reason: a reader who arrives late to the document has no access to your original *here* and *next Friday*.

### The reverse case: shifts with no backshift

Because the three dials are independent, a sentence can need place and person shifts while the verb stays exactly as it was. This happens with a present-tense reporting verb, or with a fact that is still true.

> Rosa, calling from Denver: "**I'm** stuck **here** until Thursday."  
>  →  *(reported from Boston, same day)* Rosa says **she's** stuck **there** until Thursday.

*Says* triggers no backshift, and *Thursday* is still the same Thursday, so the only changes are the two deictics that genuinely moved. [Time, immediacy, and things still on the calendar](03-no-backshift.md#10-time-immediacy-and-things-still-on-the-calendar) works through the tense side of this in detail.

## American and British forms

Everything in this file is American English. General grammar references and ESL material circulate the British forms freely, so the four differences that touch deixis are worth collecting in one place.

| Point | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| *-ward* adverbs | *afterward*, *toward*, *backward* | *afterwards*, *towards*, *backwards* |
| Counting forward from a reference point | *two days later*, *two days after that* | *in two days' time* is ordinary |
| Two weeks | *two weeks*, *a week from Friday* | *a fortnight*, *Friday week* |
| Weekend | *on the weekend*, *over the weekend* | *at the weekend* |
| Past participle of *get* | *had gotten* (obtained, became) | *had got* |
| Mandative clause | *insisted that he **be** present* | *insisted that he **should be** present* |
| Spelling of common reporting verbs | *apologize*, *inquire*, *practice* (verb) | *apologise*, *enquire*, *practise* |

None of the British forms are errors in British writing. They are simply not the American default, and *in two days' time* in particular gets taught as though it were the neutral rendering of *the day after tomorrow*, which in American prose it is not.

### The mandative subjunctive: the pronouns move, the verb cannot

This is the cleanest illustration in the language of the three dials turning independently. The American mandative subjunctive is a **bare base form with no tense at all**, so backshift has nothing to grab. Person shift still applies in full.

> "**I** insist that **you resign**," she said.  →  She insisted that **I resign**.

*I* became *she* on the reporting verb, *you* became *I* inside the subordinate clause, and *resign* did not move, because a tenseless form has nowhere to go. Two things it is not:

- ✗ *She insisted that I **resigned**.* — that is the indicative, and it means something else entirely: she claimed, over objections, that I had resigned.
- *She insisted that I **should** resign.* — grammatical, but this is the British pattern. In American writing it reads as advice rather than as a demand, because *should* also means "ought to."

> "**You** have to **be** at the hearing," the judge told **him**.  →  The judge ordered that **he be** at the hearing.

> "Please don't tell **my** family," he said.  →  He asked that **his** family **not be told**.

The negative is plain *not* in front of the verb — never *don't* or *didn't* — and the passive is *be* plus the past participle. The full treatment, with the trigger list of verbs, adjectives, and nouns, is in [Reported commands, requests, and advice](06-commands-requests.md#the-mandative-subjunctive); the variety split is set out at [American bare subjunctive vs. British *should*](06-commands-requests.md#american-bare-subjunctive-vs-british-should).

### *Apologize* and *inquire*

*Apologize* and *inquire* are the American spellings; *apologise* and *enquire* are British. American English uses *inquire* for every sense — asking a casual question and conducting a formal investigation alike — where British English traditionally reserves *inquire* for the investigation and uses *enquire* for the question. Both verbs turn up constantly as reporting verbs, and the clause after them recalculates like any other:

> "**I'm** sorry **I** missed **your** call **yesterday**," he said.  →  He **apologized** for missing **my** call **the day before**.

> "When did **you** get **here**?" she **inquired**.  →  She **inquired** when **I** had gotten **there**.

Note *gotten* in that second report. It is the American past participle of *get* in the sense of arriving or obtaining; the British *had got* is not the American default. See [American and British forms](06-commands-requests.md#american-and-british-forms) for the rest of the split, and [American and British modal usage](07-modals.md#american-and-british-modal-usage) for the modal side of it.

## Deixis inside questions and directives

Every recalculation in this file applies unchanged inside a reported question and inside a reported directive. The clause type changes; the pointing words do not care what kind of clause they are sitting in.

> "Can **you** get **these** to **me** by **tomorrow**?" she asked.  →  She asked whether **I** could get **those** to **her** by **the next day**.

> "**Bring** the samples **here tomorrow**," he told her.  →  He told her to **take** the samples **there the next day**.

The one addition is that the reporting verb's own object is itself a person deictic and has to be recalculated with everything else — *he told **me*** versus *he told **her*** is the same decision as *I* versus *he*, made one clause higher up. The clause mechanics are in [Reported questions](05-questions.md) and [Reported commands, requests, and advice](06-commands-requests.md).

## Putting it together

A single exchange, reported from four positions. Original, spoken by **Elena** to **Paul**, in Elena's kitchen, on a Tuesday, with a stack of boxes on the floor between them:

> "**I** need **you** to **bring** your truck over **here tomorrow** and help me move **these** boxes, because **I'm** flying out **the day after**."

**Paul, same kitchen, ten minutes later, to Elena's roommate:**

> Elena needs **me** to **bring** my truck over **here tomorrow** and help **her** move **these** boxes, because **she's** flying out **the day after**.

Only the person deictics moved. Same room, same day, present-tense reporting verb — so *bring*, *here*, *these*, *tomorrow*, and *the day after* are all still pointing exactly where Elena aimed them.

**Paul, from his own house, that evening, to his wife:**

> Elena **wants me** to **take** my truck over **there tomorrow** and help her move **those** boxes, because **she's** flying out **the day after**.

Place, the demonstrative, and the motion verb have now moved. The days have not, because it is still Tuesday.

**Paul, the following month, to a friend:**

> Elena **asked me** to **take** my truck over **there the next day** and help her move **those** boxes, because **she was** flying out **the day after that**.

Everything has moved.

**Elena herself, the following month, to Paul:**

> **I** asked **you** to **bring** your truck over **the next day** and help me move **those** boxes, because **I was** flying out **the day after that**.

Person shift is nearly a no-op — Elena is still *I*, Paul is still *you* — while the time words and the demonstrative have fully shifted. And note that Elena keeps *bring* where Paul had to switch to *take*. The destination is her own house, and speakers hold on to *come* and *bring* for their own home base even when they are standing somewhere else. Paul has no such claim on the place, so for him the movement is away, and the verb is *take*.

## The checklist

Four questions, asked in order, for every reported sentence.

1. **Who is speaking now, and to whom?** Recalculate every *I*, *you*, *we*, and their possessives and reflexives against the current pair of participants — and check the kinship terms and names while you are there. Then check the result for ambiguity, and upgrade a pronoun to a name wherever two referents would collide.
2. **Has the day changed?** If not, *today*, *tonight*, *tomorrow*, and *yesterday* all stay. If it has, re-anchor them to the day of the original speech.
3. **Has the place changed?** If not, *here*, *this*, *these*, *come*, and *bring* all stay. If it has, move them out.
4. **Does the verb agree with the adverb?** A backshifted verb needs a shifted time word, and an unshifted verb needs an unshifted one. Fix any mismatch — remembering that the tense itself is often a free choice while the adverb never is.

## Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| ✗ She said she would call me **tomorrow** *(reported a week later)* | She said she would call me **the next day**. |
| ✗ He said he **will** be there **the following day**. | He said he **would** be there the following day. |
| ✗ They said they had arrived **two hours ago** *(reported a month later)* | They said they had arrived **two hours earlier**. |
| ✗ The attendant said **I** couldn't park there *(the original was generic, not about me)* | The attendant said **you** couldn't park there. |
| Karen said **that that** was ridiculous. *(grammatical, but nobody writes it)* | Karen said **that** was ridiculous. |
| ✗ She told me to **come** to her office *(you are not at her office)* | She told me to **go** to her office. |
| ✗ Devon said he had left **his** phone in **his** car. | Devon said he had left **his own** phone in **Elliot's** car. |
| ✗ Marcus said he was busy **that day** *(you are reporting it the same day)* | Marcus said he was busy **today**. |
| ✗ He said **you** should be proud of **yourself** *(he said it to me, and I am the one reporting)* | He said **I** should be proud of **myself**. |
| ✗ Tom said it was freezing in **there** *(you are both still in the room)* | Tom said it was freezing in **here**. |
| ✗ She said she **had been** meeting the client that day *(the original was "I'm meeting the client today")* | She said she **was** meeting the client that day. |
| ✗ Priya said **Mom** had called her *(Priya is not your sister)* | Priya said **her mother** had called her. |
| ✗ The director insisted that I **should** take the day off. *(American report)* | The director insisted that I **take** the day off. |
| ✗ The director insisted that I **took** the day off. *(indicative — changes the meaning to a claim)* | The director insisted that I **take** the day off. |

## Practice

Report each of these **the following week, from somewhere else**, unless the item says otherwise.

1. "I'll see you here tomorrow," Marta said.
2. "Bring your laptop to my office this afternoon," my manager said to me.
3. "We finished the audit two days ago," the accountant said. *(you were not on the audit team)*
4. "It's too dark in here today," Ivan said. — report this one **the same afternoon, in the same room**.
5. "I hurt myself moving these crates yesterday," Ken told Rita. — reported by **Rita**.
6. "You left your badge in my car," Sonia said to me. — reported by **me**, to a coworker.
7. "Come pick me up at the airport tonight," Dad said on the phone. — reported by **me**, from home.
8. "I insist that you take tomorrow off," the director said to Wen. — reported by **Wen**.
9. "These days I barely see my kids," he said.
10. "Mom is flying in next Friday," my brother said. — reported by **me**; we have the same mother.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Marta said she **would see me there the next day**. (*here* → *there*, *tomorrow* → *the next day*, *you* → *me*)
2. My manager told me **to take my laptop to her office that afternoon**. (*bring* → *take*, because I am reporting from somewhere other than her office; *my office* → *her office*; *this afternoon* → *that afternoon*)
3. The accountant said **they had finished the audit two days earlier**. (exclusive *we* → *they*; *two days before* is equally correct, though *earlier* is the more natural American choice for a short interval)
4. Ivan said it **was too dark in here today**. — or, just as good, *Ivan said it's too dark in here today*. Same room and same day, so *here* and *today* both stay; the backshift is optional because the complaint is still true.
5. Ken said he **had hurt himself moving those crates the day before**. — or *Ken said he hurt himself…*, which is what most Americans would actually say. (*myself* → *himself*, *these* → *those*, *yesterday* → *the day before*)
6. Sonia said **I had left my badge in her car**. (*you* → *I*, *your* → *my*, *my* → *her* — three different pronouns, three different directions)
7. Dad asked me **to go pick him up at the airport that night**. (*come* → *go*, because I am at home and the destination is the airport; *me* → *him*; *tonight* → *that night*)
8. The director insisted that **I take the next day off**. (bare subjunctive — not ✗ *that I took*, which would mean she claimed I had; and not *that I should take*, which is the British pattern. *You* → *I* and *tomorrow* → *the next day* apply normally.)
9. He said he **barely saw his kids anymore**. — or *He said that at that time he barely saw his kids*. (✗ *in those days* is grammatical but reads as literary)
10. My brother said **Mom was flying in the following Friday**. — better still, *on the 14th*. (*Mom* stays, because she is my mother too; *next Friday* has to move, and naming the date resolves the ambiguity it carries.)

</details>
