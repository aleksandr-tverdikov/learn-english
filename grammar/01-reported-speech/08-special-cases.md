# Special cases, register, and common mistakes

> The structures that resist the ordinary rules — conditionals, wishes, exclamations, and bare speech acts — plus the American forms British coursebooks get wrong, how reporting actually works in news, academic, legal, and business writing, and the sixteen errors learners make most.

[← Reported speech](README.md) &middot; [All grammar topics](../README.md)

Everything up to this point has assumed a reportable proposition: somebody said something, and the words can be pushed one step into the past. This section covers what happens when that assumption fails. A conditional has two clauses and only one of them may move. A wish is already in the past, so there is nowhere to push it. An exclamation has no proposition at all, and *Ouch!* cannot be pushed anywhere. And once a report leaves the classroom for a newspaper, a journal article, a deposition, or a set of board minutes, the choice of reporting verb stops being a matter of variety and becomes a matter of accuracy, fairness, or liability. Two ideas carry the whole section. **A past form that is already doing non-past work cannot backshift** — that single fact explains type 2 conditionals, type 3 conditionals, *wish*, *if only*, *would rather*, and *it's time* all at once. And **no reporting verb is neutral except *said*** — every other choice tells the reader something about what the writer thinks of the speaker, which is a stylistic detail in fiction and an ethical one in journalism. Everything here describes **American** English, and one section is devoted to the three places where a British coursebook will steer you wrong: the *-ize* spellings, *inquire* against *enquire*, and the mandative subjunctive, which is a difference in grammar rather than in spelling.

## Reporting conditionals

A conditional has two verbs to worry about, and its type decides whether either one moves. The short version: **only type 1 shifts.** For the conditional forms themselves see [subordinators of condition](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md); for the mechanism, [Backshift](02-backshift.md).

### Type 1: the only one that shifts

A real, open condition uses present + *will*. Both halves move back one step: the present becomes past, and *will* becomes *would*. Modals in the result clause shift the same way — *can* → *could*, *may* → *might*.

> "If it rains, we'll cancel the picnic," she said.  →  She said that if it **rained**, they **would** cancel the picnic.  
> "If you finish early, I'll buy you lunch," he told me.  →  He told me that if I **finished** early, he **would** buy me lunch.  
> "If we leave now, we can still make the 7:40," Dana said.  →  Dana said that if they **left** then, they **could** still make the 7:40.

**Never put *would* in the reported *if*-clause.** The condition takes a past form, not a conditional one — the same restriction that keeps *will* out of the direct version.

> ✗ She said that if it **would rain**, they would cancel.  
> ✓ She said that if it **rained**, they would cancel.

The one *would* that legitimately appears in an *if*-clause is the volitional *would* of polite requests — *if you would sign here*, *if you would just listen* — which marks willingness rather than futurity and survives a report unchanged: *He asked whether I **would** sign at the bottom.*

**Where the rule is routinely broken:** when the condition is still open at the moment of reporting, American speakers very often leave type 1 alone.

> "If it rains, we'll cancel."  →  She said that if it **rains**, they'**ll** cancel. (said an hour ago; it still might rain)

This is not sloppiness — it is the standing-fact exception applied to a standing condition, and it is what you hear in conversation and read in news copy. Shift it when the matter is closed (the picnic already happened); keep it when the matter is live.

### Type 2: already past-shaped, so nothing moves

A type 2 conditional uses the past form to mark unreality, not past time. There is no further past that would preserve the meaning, so the tenses stay exactly where they are and only the pronouns change. *Were* survives intact.

> "If I had a car, I'd drive you to the airport," he said.  →  He said that if he **had** a car, he'**d** drive **me** to the airport.  
> "If I were you, I'd take the offer," she said.  →  She said that if she **were me**, she'**d** take the offer.

Shifting the *if*-clause into the past perfect is not a stylistic upgrade; it changes the meaning from "unlikely now" to "impossible, and it did not happen."

> ✗ He said that if he **had had** a car, he would drive me. (now reports a condition that failed in the past — that he did not have a car back then — which is not what he said)  
> ✓ He said that if he **had** a car, he would drive me.

### Type 3 and mixed conditionals: no shift is possible

Type 3 is built from the past perfect and *would have* + past participle, both already at the far end of the tense system, so backshift has nothing to do. Mixed conditionals — a type 3 condition with a type 2 result, or the reverse — behave the same way.

> "If I had known you were in town, I would have called," he said.  →  He said that if he **had known** I **was** in town, he **would have called**.  
> "If I'd taken that job, I'd be living in Austin now," she said.  →  She said that if she **had taken** that job, she **would be living** in Austin **now**.  
> "If I weren't so tired, I would have driven you home," he said.  →  He said that if he **weren't** so tired, he **would have driven** me home.

*Now* survives in the second example only because the report is contemporaneous with the speech. Reported on a later day it becomes *then* — *She said that if she had taken that job, she would be living in Austin **then*** — on the ordinary time-word rule, which is a separate mechanism from backshift and is set out in [Pronouns, time, and place](04-pronouns-time-place.md).

### Zero conditionals and standing rules

A zero conditional states a rule that holds regardless of when it is uttered. Both versions are grammatical, and the choice signals whether the writer still endorses the rule.

> "If you heat water to 212 degrees, it boils," the teacher said.  →  The teacher said that if you **heat** water to 212 degrees, it **boils**. (still true; preferred)  
> →  The teacher said that if you **heated** water to 212 degrees, it **boiled**. (grammatical, but faintly suggests the claim is only hers)  
> "If a package is over ten pounds, it ships separately," she explained.  →  She explained that if a package **is** over ten pounds, it **ships** separately. (policy still in force)

### Summary table

| Type | Direct | Reported | What moves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | "If you press this, the light **comes** on." | He said that if you **press** that, the light **comes** on. | Nothing, while the fact holds |
| Type 1 | "If it **rains**, we'**ll** cancel." | He said that if it **rained**, they **would** cancel. | Both clauses |
| Type 1, live condition | "If it **rains**, we'**ll** cancel." | He said that if it **rains**, they'**ll** cancel. | Nothing — the condition is still open |
| Type 2 | "If I **had** more time, I'**d** learn Spanish." | He said that if he **had** more time, he'**d** learn Spanish. | Pronouns only |
| Type 3 | "If I **had left** earlier, I **would have caught** it." | He said that if he **had left** earlier, he **would have caught** it. | Pronouns only |
| Mixed 3→2 | "If I'**d studied** medicine, I'**d be** a doctor now." | He said that if he **had studied** medicine, he'**d be** a doctor now. | Pronouns only |
| Unreal *were* | "If I **were** you…" | He said that if he **were** me… | Pronouns only |

### Where the *if*-clause attaches

Moving the reporting clause changes what the condition applies to, and the ambiguity is real.

> He said that if he was elected, he would cut taxes. (the condition is part of what he said)  
> If he was elected, he said he would cut taxes. (reads as: assuming he won, he made a promise — a claim about the reporting, not the content)

In news and legal writing, keep the *if*-clause inside the *that*-clause so the condition is unambiguously the speaker's.

### Packaging a conditional into a speech act

Conditionals are often threats, offers, warnings, and promises, and a reporting verb can absorb the whole structure — see the [reporting-verb dictionary](catalog/README.md).

| Direct | Reported as a clause | Reported as a speech act |
|---|---|---|
| "If you tell anyone, I'll deny it." | He said that if I told anyone, he would deny it. | He **threatened** to deny it if I told anyone. |
| "If you need a ride, I'm free at six." | She said that if I needed a ride, she was free at six. | She **offered** me a ride at six. |
| "If you touch that wire, you'll get a shock." | He said that if I touched that wire, I'd get a shock. | He **warned** me not to touch the wire. |
| "If you finish by Friday, I'll add a bonus." | She said that if we finished by Friday, she'd add a bonus. | She **[promised](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise)** a bonus for finishing by Friday. |

## Reporting *wish* and *if only*

### The rule: *wish* shifts, its clause does not

*Wish* is an ordinary present-tense verb and backshifts normally. The clause after it is already unreal-past and stays put. With a present-tense reporting verb nothing moves at all.

> "I wish I **knew** the answer," she said.  →  She said she **wished** she **knew** the answer.  
> "I wish I **hadn't sold** the truck," Dana said.  →  Dana said she **wished** she **hadn't sold** the truck.  
> "I wish he **would** call," my mother said.  →  My mother said she **wished** he **would** call.  
> "I wish I were taller."  →  She says she **wishes** she **were** taller.

| Direct | What it means | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| "I wish I **knew** German." | regret about now | She said she **wished** she **knew** German. |
| "I wish I **had known**." | regret about the past | She said she **wished** she **had known**. |
| "I wish it **would** stop." | irritation, wanting a change | She said she **wished** it **would** stop. |
| "I wish I **could** come." | inability now | She said she **wished** she **could** come. |
| "I wish you **wouldn't** do that." | complaint about a habit | She said she **wished** I **wouldn't** do that. |
| "I wish I **were** somewhere else." | unreal present with *were* | She said she **wished** she **were** somewhere else. |

### *if only*

*If only* is the emphatic twin of *wish* (see [if only](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/06-condition.md#if-only)). It can survive into the report unchanged, but because it is an exclamation as much as a statement, the more idiomatic move is to convert it to *wish* or to a verb that names the feeling.

> "If only I had listened!" she said.  →  She said that **if only** she **had listened**.  →  She **wished** she **had listened**. (more natural)  →  She **lamented** not having listened. (formal)  
> "If only my brother would answer his phone!" he said.  →  He said that **if only** his brother **would** answer his phone.  →  He **complained** that his brother never answered his phone.

Two cautions: the exclamation point never survives — no reported clause ends in one — and *if only* stranded with no result clause reads as a sentence fragment in formal prose, which is the main reason editors convert it to *wish*.

### The rest of the unreal-past family

The same "already past, so it cannot move" logic covers five more structures. In every one of them the past-tense verb marks unreality or preference, not past time, and reporting touches only the pronouns and the place and time words.

| Structure | Direct | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| *would rather* + past | "I'd rather you **didn't** smoke in here." | She said she'd rather I **didn't** smoke in there. |
| *it's time* + past | "It's time we **left**." | He said it **was** time they **left**. |
| *as if / as though* + past | "He acts as if he **owned** the place." | She said he acted as if he **owned** the place. |
| *would sooner* + past | "I'd sooner you **told** her yourself." | He said he'd sooner I **told** her myself. |
| *what if / suppose* + past | "What if she **said** no?" | He asked what they would do if she **said** no. |

## Reporting exclamations

An exclamation has emotional content but often no reportable proposition. There are three ways out, and the right one depends on how much of the original there is to work with.

**1. Keep the exclamative clause.** *What a…* and *how…* exclamatives survive as subordinate clauses. The exclamative word order stays; no inversion is added, and no exclamation point or question mark appears. The verbs that take the clause directly are *say*, *exclaim*, and *cry*; *remark on*, *comment on*, and *marvel at* take the same clause as the object of their preposition.

> "What a mess!" he said.  →  He said **what a mess it was**.  →  He **remarked on what a mess it was**.  
> "How beautiful the garden is!" she said.  →  She exclaimed **how beautiful the garden was**.

Note the difference in complement: *remark* and *comment* need their preposition here — ✓ *He remarked **on** what a mess it was*, ✗ *He remarked what a mess it was* — while *say* and *exclaim* take the clause bare.

**2. Flatten it to a *that*-clause.** The exclamative force is traded for an intensifier — *such*, *so*, *really*, *quite*. This is the safest option in formal prose.

> "What a mess!"  →  He exclaimed **that it was a terrible mess**.  
> "What an idiot I've been!"  →  He said **that he had been such an idiot**.

**3. Name the act instead.** Frequently the cleanest report drops the words entirely.

> "What a mess!"  →  He **complained about the mess**.  
> "What nonsense!"  →  He **dismissed it as nonsense**.

Interjections — *ouch*, *wow*, *ugh*, *huh*, *yikes* — have no clause inside them, so there is nothing to subordinate (see [interjections](../../parts-of-speech/08-interjections/README.md)). Either quote them or describe the reaction.

> ✗ He said that ouch.  
> ✓ He **cried out in pain**.  /  ✓ "Ouch!" he said.

| Direct | Exclamative clause kept | *that*-clause | Act named |
|---|---|---|---|
| "What a mess!" | He said what a mess it was. | He said that it was a real mess. | He complained about the mess. |
| "How lovely!" | She exclaimed how lovely it was. | She said that it was lovely. | She admired it. |
| "What a waste of money!" | He remarked **on** what a waste of money it was. | He said that it was a waste of money. | He objected to the cost. |
| "Wow!" | — | — | He was astonished. / He gasped. |
| "Ouch!" | — | — | She cried out in pain. |
| "Oh no!" | — | — | He groaned in dismay. |
| "Yuck!" | — | She said that it was disgusting. | She made a face. |

## Speech acts: when a verb replaces the words

Greetings, thanks, apologies, congratulations, and toasts are **performances**, not statements. *Sorry* is not a proposition, so *she said that sorry* is impossible. English handles these with a verb that names the act, and the original words disappear entirely.

| Direct words | Reported |
|---|---|
| "Hi." / "Good morning." | She **greeted** me. / She **said hello**. |
| "Goodbye." / "See you Monday." | He **said goodbye**. / He **took his leave**. (formal) |
| "Thanks so much." | She **thanked** me for the ride. |
| "Sorry I'm late." | He **apologized for being late**. |
| "I do apologize for the confusion." | She **apologized for the confusion**. |
| "Congratulations!" | She **congratulated** me on the promotion. |
| "Happy birthday!" | He **wished** me a happy birthday. |
| "Good luck!" | She **wished** me luck. |
| "Excuse me." (leaving) | He **excused himself**. |
| "Excuse me." (getting attention) | He **stopped me**. / He **interrupted**. |
| "Cheers!" / "Welcome." | They **toasted** the new office. / She **welcomed** us. |
| "Yes." / "No." (to a request) | He **agreed**. / He **refused**. |
| "Yes." / "No." (to a question) | He **said yes**. / He **said no**. / He **denied it**. |
| "Never mind." | She **told me to forget it**. / She **dropped it**. |
| "You're welcome." | He **said I was welcome**. / He **brushed off the thanks**. |
| "Please, please reconsider." | She **begged** me to reconsider. |
| "Would you like a ride?" | He **offered** me a ride. |
| "Let's split it." | She **suggested** splitting it. |
| "I promise I'll be there." | She **[promised](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#promise)** to be there. |

Three points worth stating plainly. **Performatives collapse:** "I apologize," "Sorry," "My apologies," and "That's on me" all report as *he apologized* — accurate about the act and silent about the register, so if the register matters, quote. **Some formulas have no verb at all:** *Bless you*, *No worries*, *There you go*, *Knock it off* — there is no English verb meaning "to say bless you," so quote them or describe them (*She said "bless you" when I sneezed*). **Frozen quotations without quotation marks are normal:** *She said hello*, *He said thank you*, and *He said goodbye* are standard American English and need no quotation marks, because the words name the act rather than quote it.

## American forms: spelling, *inquire*, and the subjunctive

Most reported-speech material in circulation was written for a British classroom, and three of its habits will make American copy read wrong — one of them grammatically, not just cosmetically. The full inventory is in [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md#12-american-practice-and-where-the-british-books-differ); what follows is the part that bears on the reporting verbs and registers in this file.

### The reporting verbs are spelled *-ize*

American English writes **-ize** on every Greek-suffix verb, and reporting verbs are full of them: *apologize*, *criticize*, *emphasize*, *summarize*, *recognize*, *characterize*, *minimize*, *theorize*, *sympathize*. *Apologise*, *criticise*, and *emphasise* are the British spellings and have no place in American writing.

> "Sorry about the delay," she said.  →  She **apologized** for the delay. (never *apologised*)  
> "The estimate is far too low," he said.  →  He **criticized** the estimate as far too low. (never *criticised*)  
> "This is the central problem," she said.  →  She **emphasized** that it **was** the central problem. (never *emphasised*)

Two traps sit inside the rule. **Not every *-ise* is a disguised *-ize*.** In *promise*, *advise*, *surmise*, *apprise*, *compromise*, *supervise*, and *devise*, the *-ise* belongs to the stem rather than to the Greek suffix, and it stays in both standards. Several of them are reporting verbs, and "correcting" them produces nonsense: ✓ *He **promised** to pay me back*, ✗ *promized*; ✓ *She **advised** me to get a lawyer*, ✗ *advized*. And **the *-yse* verbs go to *-yze*:** American *analyze*, *paralyze*, *catalyze*, against British *analyse*, *paralyse*, *catalyse*. The *-l-* doubling runs the other way from what a British book will show — American *counseled*, *canceled*, *labeled*, *signaled*, *traveled*, *marveled*, against British *counselled*, *cancelled*, and the rest.

### *inquire*, not *enquire*

British English splits the pair by sense: *enquire* is to ask an ordinary question, *inquire* is to conduct a formal investigation. **American English does not make that split.** Write *inquire* and *inquiry* for both senses; *enquire* and *enquiry* read to an American editor as either British or a typo.

> "Has the position been filled?" she asked.  →  She **inquired whether** the position **had been** filled.  
> "Where were you that night?" the detective asked.  →  The detective **inquired** where I **had been** that night.  
> The committee opened an **inquiry** into the contract. (never *enquiry*)

Two things to keep straight while you are here. *Inquire* takes **no personal object**: ✓ *She inquired whether…*, ✓ *She inquired of the registrar whether…*, ✗ *She inquired the registrar whether…*, ✗ *She inquired me…*. And *inquire* is markedly more formal than *ask* in American English — in ordinary speech Americans ask, and *inquire* belongs to police reports, journalism, and business correspondence. The complement patterns are in [Reported questions](05-questions.md).

### The mandative subjunctive: American *be*, British *should be*

This is the most visible grammatical difference between the two standards and the one most often miscopied out of a British coursebook. After verbs and adjectives of demanding, requiring, and recommending — *insist*, *demand*, *require*, *request*, *ask*, *recommend*, *propose*, *suggest*, *urge*, *move*, and *it is essential / vital / imperative / crucial that* — American English puts a **bare base form** in the *that*-clause: no *-s*, no *to*, no agreement, no *should*. Having no tense, it has nothing to backshift, so reporting leaves it exactly where it is.

> "I insist that he **be** present," she said.  →  She **insisted** that he **be** present.  
> "We demand that the fee **be** waived," they wrote.  →  They **demanded** that the fee **be** waived.  
> "I recommend that she **not sign** it," the lawyer said.  →  The lawyer **recommended** that she **not sign** it.  
> "It's essential that every member **vote**," he said.  →  He said it **was** essential that every member **vote**.

| | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | "I insist that he **be** present." | "I insist that he **should be** present." |
| Reported | She insisted that he **be** present. | She insisted that he **should be** present. |
| Negative | …recommended that she **not sign** it. | …recommended that she **should not sign** it. |
| Third-person singular | …demanded that he **pay** the fee. | …demanded that he **should pay** the fee. |
| Passive | …required that the notice **be** filed. | …required that the notice **should be** filed. |

Three consequences. The reporting verb and any surrounding real-tense verb shift normally — *insist* → *insisted*, *is* → *was* — while the subjunctive verb sits still. **Negation is a bare *not* in front of the verb, with no *do* and no agreement**: ✓ *recommended that she **not sign***, ✗ *that she doesn't sign*, ✗ *that she not signs*. And the form is invisible everywhere except the third-person singular and *be*, so *they demanded that we leave* gives no clue at all; it surfaces only in *that he leave*, *that she not sign*, *that it be filed*.

The bare subjunctive is not formal or archaic in American English — *the doctor recommended he stop smoking* is ordinary conversation. The *should* version is not ungrammatical for an American, but it reads as British, and inside a reported clause it can be misread as advice (*should* = "ought to") rather than as the demand that was actually made. Prefer the bare form.

One genuine American use of the plain indicative remains. *Insist*, *suggest*, and *urge* straddle the line between performing a demand and making a claim, and the claim sense takes an ordinary tensed verb, which backshifts normally.

> "He was present, I'm telling you," she insisted.  →  She **insisted** that he **had been** present. (a claim about a fact — ordinary backshift)  
> "He must be present," she insisted.  →  She **insisted** that he **be** present. (a demand — subjunctive, no shift)

Decide which act the speaker performed before choosing the form. See [insist](catalog/03-agree-refuse.md#insist) in the reporting-verb dictionary and [American practice vs. the British books](03-no-backshift.md#12-american-practice-and-where-the-british-books-differ).

### Punctuation, in one line

American style makes double quotation marks the default, uses single marks only inside them, and puts commas and periods **inside** the closing quote whether or not they belong to the quoted material — *"We're done here," she said.* British style reverses both conventions. This applies in every register below, and it is the difference an American editor catches first.

## Mixing types in one sentence

### One subject, several reporting verbs

A single turn of speech often contains a statement, a question, and a command. They cannot share one reporting verb, because each requires a different complement. Name the subject once and coordinate the verbs.

> "I'm leaving at six. Can you lock up? Don't forget the alarm," he said.  →  He said that he **was** leaving at six, **asked** whether I **could** lock up, and **told** me **not to forget** the alarm.  
> "The shipment's late. Should we notify the client? Call them first thing," she said.  →  She said the shipment **was** late, **asked** whether we **should** notify the client, and **told** us **to call** them first thing.

Do not try to run two speech-act types under one verb.

> ✗ He said that he was leaving at six and if I could lock up.  
> ✓ He said that he was leaving at six and **asked** whether I could lock up.

### Repeating *that*

When two *that*-clauses are coordinated, repeat the *that* — otherwise the second clause can be read as the writer's own assertion rather than the speaker's. See [complementizers](../../parts-of-speech/07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md).

> Ambiguous: She said the road was closed and the detour added an hour.  
> Clear: She said **that** the road was closed and **that** the detour added an hour.

The same applies across a change of reporting verb: *He admitted **that** the numbers were wrong but insisted **that** the conclusion held.*

### Mixing shifted and unshifted tenses

One sentence may legitimately contain both, when part of the content is over and part still holds.

> "I was born in Ohio, and I still have family there," she said.  →  She said she **had been born** in Ohio and still **has** family there. (acceptable; the second fact is current)  →  She said she **had been born** in Ohio and still **had** family there. (safer in formal writing)

The mixed version is common in speech and journalism; in academic and legal prose, keep the tense consistent within a sentence unless the switch is doing real work.

### The past simple usually stays put

The textbook rule sends a simple past one step further back, into the past perfect. **In ordinary American speech it usually does not go**, and the shifted version can sound stiff or faintly overwrought in conversation. This is not a slip that careful speakers avoid; it is the majority pattern, and it is worth saying plainly because the rule as taught and the rule as spoken part company here more than anywhere else.

> "I saw her yesterday," he said.  →  He said he **saw** her yesterday. (what Americans actually say)  →  He said he **had seen** her yesterday. (textbook, and the right choice in edited prose)  
> "We finished the audit last week," she said.  →  She said they **finished** the audit the week before.  /  She said they **had finished** the audit the week before.

Keep the two mechanisms apart. *Yesterday* stays in the first pair because that report comes on the same day; *last week* becomes *the week before* in **both** halves of the second pair because that report comes the following week. The tense is a free choice; the time word is not, and it moves or stays for its own reasons.

The past perfect earns its keep when it disambiguates the order of two past events — *He said the shipment **had left** before the storm hit* — and in formal registers, where the strict form is expected. Where a time adverbial or the obvious sequence already fixes the order, the simple past costs nothing.

The same honesty applies to the still-true present. Under a past-tense reporting verb, a statement that still holds takes either form, and Americans pick the unshifted one constantly.

> "I have two kids," she said.  →  She said she **has** two kids. (still true; the more natural report)  /  She said she **had** two kids. (also correct, and neutral about whether it still holds)  
> "The office opens at eight," he said.  →  He said the office **opens** at eight. (standing fact)  /  He said the office **opened** at eight.

Neither case licenses abandoning backshift. Both are places where two forms are available and the choice carries meaning — the unshifted form vouches for the statement as current, the shifted form does not. The full treatment, with the constraints on when the unshifted form is actually available, is in [When not to backshift](03-no-backshift.md#6-past-simple-often-stays-past-simple).

## Long stretches: quotation, summary, and the mix

**Quote when the wording itself is the point:** the phrase is memorable or inflammatory (*"dead on arrival"*); the statement is contested and the reader needs to see it came from the speaker; it commits the speaker to something — a promise, a denial, a number, an admission; or the exact language has legal weight. **Summarize when the words are routine, repetitive, or long:** background the reader already knows, lists of figures that read better in your own tighter sentences, rambling or ungrammatical speech that would embarrass the source without informing the reader, and anything you would have to cut with more than one ellipsis.

### The standard shape

Professional reporting alternates: **summarize the substance, quote the punchline.**

> The mayor spent most of the hour defending the budget, saying that the shortfall was temporary and that no layoffs were planned. "Nobody is losing a job over this," she said.

The indirect sentence carries the information efficiently; the quotation delivers the commitment in the speaker's own words. Reversing the order — a long quote followed by a summary of the same thing — wastes the reader's time.

### Partial quotation

A distinctive phrase can be quoted inside an otherwise indirect sentence. The fragment keeps the speaker's original grammar and **is not backshifted**, because nothing inside quotation marks may be altered.

> He said that the plan was **"dead on arrival"** and that the council would start over.  
> ✗ He said that the plan **"had been dead on arrival."** (he said *is*, so the quotation may not read *had been*)

If a changed word is unavoidable, bracket it — *He said **[the bill]** would never pass* — and if material is cut, use an ellipsis that does not reverse the sense.

### Keeping the attribution alive

Across a long stretch of indirect speech the reader loses track of whose claims they are reading. Re-anchor every few sentences with a light reminder — *she added*, *he went on*, *according to Ruiz* — and put the substantive attribution at the end of the first sentence, not buried in the third.

> Ruiz said the audit had found three discrepancies. Two were clerical, she said, and the third had already been corrected. She added that the office would publish the findings in full.

## Free indirect speech

**Free indirect speech** (also *free indirect discourse*, *style indirect libre*) is third-person past narration that keeps the grammar of indirect speech — third person, backshifted tense — but the **voice** of direct speech: the character's vocabulary, syntax, questions, exclamations, fragments, and slang, with **no reporting clause and no quotation marks**. It is the default mode for rendering thought in modern fiction, and the reason a novel can stay in the third person while sounding as though the character is talking.

| Mode | Example |
|---|---|
| **Direct** | "Where on earth did I leave those keys? I'm never going to get out of here on time," she thought. |
| **Indirect** | She wondered where she had left her keys and thought that she would never get out of the house on time. |
| **Free indirect** | Where on earth had she left those keys? She was never going to get out of here on time. |

The free indirect version takes its **pronouns and tense** from the indirect row and its **question form, its *on earth*, and its *here*** from the direct row. That mixture is the whole technique.

### A literary example

Virginia Woolf's *Mrs. Dalloway* (1925) opens with a plain indirect report and lets go of the reporting clause before the first page is out:

> Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.
>
> […] And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning — fresh as if issued to children on a beach. What a lark! What a plunge! For so it had always seemed to her, when, with a little squeak of the hinges, which she could hear now, she had burst open the French windows and plunged at Bourton into the open air.

The first sentence is textbook indirect speech, reporting clause and all. The next one still carries an attribution — *thought Clarissa Dalloway* — but it has already been shoved into the middle of the sentence and surrounded by the character's own words. Then the attribution stops entirely: *What a lark! What a plunge!* are Clarissa's exclamations in Clarissa's rhythm, sitting inside third-person past narration with nothing at all introducing them. And *which she could hear now* sets *now* beside a past-tense verb, inside a sentence whose main clause is a past perfect (*she had burst open*) — impossible in strict indirect speech, standard in free indirect. Jane Austen is generally credited with developing the technique in English, in *Emma* (1815) and *Persuasion* (1817), a century before it had a name in English criticism.

### Deixis: the giveaway

The reliable diagnostic is a deictic word belonging to the character sitting beside a tense belonging to the narrator.

| Combination | Strict indirect | Free indirect |
|---|---|---|
| past + *now* | ✗ | He was late **now**, and there was nothing to be done. |
| *would* + *tomorrow* | ✗ | She **would** see him **tomorrow**. Everything depended on it. |
| past + *here* | ✗ | Why had she ever come **here**? |
| past + *this* | ✗ | **This** was the last time. He meant it. |
| past + question word order | ✗ | **Had** she locked the door? She couldn't remember. |
| past + exclamation | ✗ | What a fool he had been! |

**How to write it.** Write the sentence as ordinary indirect speech; delete the reporting clause and the *that*; restore the speaker's question order, exclamations, fragments, and word choices; keep third person and the backshifted tense; then put back the character's deictics — *here*, *now*, *this*, *tomorrow*.

> Indirect: He thought that he should have called her back and wondered whether it was too late to try.  →  Free indirect: He **should** have called her back. Was it too late to try **now**?

### Where not to use it

Free indirect speech works precisely because the reader cannot always tell where the character's view ends and the narrator's begins. That ambiguity is an asset in fiction and a defect everywhere else. **Never use it in news, academic, legal, or business writing**, where an unattributed claim reads as the writer's own assertion and can be quoted against them.

> ✗ (news) The merger was a disaster waiting to happen. The board had been warned for months.  
> ✓ (news) Ruiz said the merger was a disaster waiting to happen and that the board had been warned for months.

## Reporting in news writing

### *said* is the default, and it does not wear out

American newsrooms use *said* — past tense, after the name — as the neutral attribution, and repeat it without apology. Readers stop registering it the way they stop registering *the*. Reaching for *opined*, *voiced*, *enthused*, *quipped*, or *chuckled* to avoid repetition is the clearest marker of amateur copy, and every one of those verbs smuggles in an opinion. Standard: *Ruiz said.* With an audience: *Ruiz told reporters.* For a follow-up quote: *she added.* Inverted attribution (*said Ruiz*) is acceptable but generally avoided, with one exception — it is the natural order when a long title follows the name.

> Awkward: "The number is wrong," Maria Ruiz, the district's chief financial officer, said.  
> Better: "The number is wrong," **said Maria Ruiz**, the district's chief financial officer.

### Verbs that carry judgment

| Verb | What it tells the reader | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **said** | nothing | always, unless there is a specific reason not to |
| **told** | said, and names the audience | *told reporters*, *told the committee* |
| **added** | continues the same speaker | second and later quotes from one source |
| **claimed** | the writer doubts it | only when the doubt is documented in the story |
| **admitted**, **conceded** | the statement is against the speaker's interest and implies a prior denial | only when there actually was a denial or reluctance |
| **insisted** | the speaker is repeating in the face of contradiction | only when the contradiction is on the record |
| **explained** | the writer accepts the statement as true and clarifying | rarely — it takes the source's side |
| **noted**, **pointed out** | the writer endorses it as established fact | only for facts, never for contentions |
| **argued**, **contended** | one side of a live dispute | analysis and disputed claims |
| **revealed**, **disclosed** | the information was genuinely hidden until now | when it truly was |
| **refused to say** | the speaker had an obligation to answer | when the question was fair and pressed; *declined to say* is the neutral form |
| **according to** | attribution with no verb of speaking | documents, agencies, secondhand sourcing |

The difference is not subtle in context:

> The company **said** its servers had been offline for six hours. (neutral)  
> The company **admitted** its servers had been offline for six hours. (implies it had been hiding it)  
> The company **claimed** its servers had been offline for six hours. (implies the writer thinks it was longer)

If the outage was disclosed voluntarily and nobody has disputed it, only the first sentence is fair.

### Placement and punctuation

- Put the attribution at the **end of the first sentence** of a multi-sentence quotation, not after the whole block.
- American style places commas and periods **inside** the closing quotation mark, always: *"We're done here," she said.*
- A quoted question keeps its question mark inside the quotes and takes no comma: *"Who authorized this?" she asked.* One speaker per paragraph; a new speaker gets a new paragraph.

### Headlines: present tense, no backshift

Headlines use the historical present for past events and *says* for attribution. Because the reporting verb is present, **the reported clause does not backshift**.

> Body: Mayor Ruiz **said** the budget gap **was** temporary.  →  Headline: **Mayor says budget gap is temporary**

Related conventions: the infinitive for the future (*Council to vote Tuesday*), the colon for compressed attribution (*Ruiz: "Nobody is losing a job"*), and single quotation marks in place of double (*Mayor calls gap 'temporary'*). Documents, agencies, and data also take present-tense reporting verbs, because they still say what they say — *The report **finds**…*, *The filing **shows**…*, *The lawsuit **alleges**…* — and anonymous sourcing has its own formula, which should always state the reason: *a person familiar with the negotiations said, speaking on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss the talks publicly.*

## Reporting in academic writing

### Reporting verbs as stance markers

In academic prose the reporting verb is where the writer's evaluation of a source lives. Choosing it is an argumentative act, not a search for variety.

| Stance | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral | states, reports, describes, observes, writes, presents, examines | Kim (2019) **reports** a 12 percent decline. |
| Endorsing | shows, demonstrates, establishes, finds, confirms | Kim (2019) **demonstrates** that the effect persists. |
| Tentative | suggests, proposes, posits, hypothesizes, implies, indicates | Kim (2019) **suggests** that the effect may persist. |
| Argumentative | argues, contends, maintains, asserts, holds | Kim (2019) **argues** that the standard model is inadequate. |
| Distancing | claims, alleges, assumes, speculates, presumes | Kim (2019) **claims** the model is universal. |
| In dispute | disputes, challenges, questions, rejects, refutes, criticizes | Ortiz (2021) **disputes** Kim's interpretation. |

*Claims* means the same thing in a journal as in a newspaper — the writer is not signing on. *Proves* and *refutes* assert logical finality and should be reserved for cases where that is literally true. And **do not use *said* for written sources**: a paper *argues*, *writes*, or *states*; it does not *say*.

### Citation tense

| Tense | Reports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Present simple** | claims treated as current knowledge; what a text does; general truths | Nguyen (2020) **argues** that memory is reconstructive. &middot; The data **show** a clear trend. |
| **Past simple** | a specific completed study — design, method, sample, results | Nguyen (2020) **surveyed** 400 households and **found** a 12 percent gap. |
| **Present perfect** | a body of prior work, a trend, an ongoing line of research | Several studies **have shown**… &middot; Research on bilingualism **has focused** on… |

Style guides differ and you should follow the one your field requires. **APA** prescribes past or present perfect for reporting past research (*Nguyen (2020) found*; *researchers have found*) and present tense for discussing results and conclusions. **MLA** uses the literary present for what a text does (*Morrison writes*, *the narrator withholds*). **Chicago** author-date practice sits close to APA.

### No backshift for claims that still stand

This is the most useful exception in academic writing. A source's claim is presented as still holding, so the *that*-clause stays in the present even after a past-tense reporting verb.

> ✓ Nguyen (2020) **argued** that memory **is** reconstructive.  
> ✗ Nguyen (2020) argued that memory **was** reconstructive. (implies the claim has since been abandoned, or was true only then)

Backshift here is a deliberate signal that a claim has been superseded: *Early accounts held that the effect **was** universal; later work found otherwise.*

Two related choices. **Integral** citation foregrounds the researcher — *Nguyen (2020) argues that memory is reconstructive* — and is useful when the source is itself the topic; **non-integral** citation foregrounds the finding — *Memory is reconstructive (Nguyen, 2020)* — and is the default for established background. And hedges belong to the same system: *the results suggest that…*, *this may indicate…*, *X appears to…* report a finding while limiting its strength, so dropping the hedge misreports the source.

## Reporting in legal and business contexts

### Testimony: the words, not the gist

In court, what was said is often the evidence itself, so reporting is verbatim. Transcripts preserve hesitations, false starts, and errors, marking errors with *[sic]* and nonverbal events in parentheses.

> Q: What did he say to you?  
> A: He said, "I never touched the car."

A witness asked to characterize speech rather than reproduce it will be redirected: *He was angry* is a conclusion, while *He said, "Get out of my house"* is testimony. Attribution verbs in filings and opinions are formal and specific — *testified that*, *stated under oath that*, *avers*, *asserts*, *contends*, *submits*, *alleges*. **Allegedly** and **alleged** put a claim on the record without adopting it, and placement matters: *the alleged assault* (the event may not have occurred) versus *the assault by the alleged attacker* (the event is established, the identity is not).

### Documents speak in the present

A contract, statute, memo, or policy continues to say what it says for as long as it is in force, so it is reported in the present tense with no backshift.

> The Seller **represents and warrants** that the equipment **is** free of liens.  
> Section 4 **provides** that the buyer **must** give thirty days' notice.  
> The statute **requires** that the notice **be** filed within ten days. (mandative subjunctive — see [verbs](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/README.md))  
> The memo **says** the office **closes** at four.

The performative verbs of contract drafting — *represents*, *warrants*, *acknowledges*, *agrees*, *covenants*, *certifies* — are reporting verbs whose *that*-clause is a binding commitment. In legal drafting *shall* marks an obligation, though plain-language drafting now prefers *must*.

### Meeting minutes and business email

Minutes are past tense, third person, and indirect throughout. They name the speaker, use neutral verbs, and do not quote — except for a formal motion, which is recorded verbatim because the words are what was voted on.

> Rivera **reported** that third-quarter revenue was up 6 percent. Chen **asked** whether the hiring freeze applied to contractors. Okafor **objected** that the timeline was unrealistic. The committee **agreed** to revisit the budget in November.  
> **MOTION:** that the Board approve the revised FY26 budget as presented. Moved by Rivera, seconded by Chen. **Carried, 7–2.**

In email, reporting is usually compressed into a prepositional phrase — *Per our conversation*, *As discussed*, *To recap* — and two habits are worth naming. **Agentless passives hide the speaker** — *It was suggested that we delay the launch* is genuinely useful when the identity is irrelevant, and is also the standard way to avoid naming who said it; use it deliberately, knowing that experienced readers hear the evasion. And **loaded verbs read as accusations in email exactly as they do in print**: *Dana claimed the file was sent* will be read as calling Dana a liar. Write *Dana said the file was sent*.

### Register summary

| Context | Default verb | Tense practice | Quotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| News | *said* | backshift, but keep the present for standing facts; headlines present | verbatim; brackets for changes, ellipses for cuts |
| Fiction | *said* plus free indirect speech | past narration | dialogue verbatim; thought often unquoted |
| Academic | *argues*, *found*, *has shown* | present for claims, past for method | sparing; paraphrase preferred |
| Legal — testimony | *testified*, *stated* | verbatim, no shift inside quotes | exact, including *[sic]* |
| Legal — documents | *provides*, *states*, *requires* | present, no backshift | verbatim for operative language |
| Business minutes | *reported*, *asked*, *agreed* | past, backshifted | motions only |
| Business email | *said*, *confirmed*, *asked* | past or present as convenient | short quotes for commitments |

## Common mistakes

Sixteen errors in ✗ → ✓ pairs. The first several appear in nearly every learner's writing.

### Questions

**1. Inversion left in the reported question.**

> ✗ She asked me **where did I live**.  
> ✓ She asked me **where I lived**.

An indirect question is a noun clause, not a question. Subject before verb, always — even though the reporting verb is *ask*.

**2. Do-support left in.**

> ✗ He asked what **did she want**.  ✓ He asked what **she wanted**.  
> ✗ I wondered whether he **did know** about it.  ✓ I wondered whether he **knew** about it.

*Do*, *does*, and *did* exist to build direct questions. Once the inversion is gone they have no job, and the tense returns to the main verb.

**3. Question mark kept.**

> ✗ She asked me where I lived**?**  
> ✓ She asked me where I lived**.**

The sentence is a statement about a question, so it ends in a period. A question mark is correct only when the whole sentence is itself a question: *Did she ask you where I lived?*

**4. Yes/no question reported with no *if* or *whether*.**

> ✗ He asked me **was I coming**.  ✗ He asked me **that I was coming**.  
> ✓ He asked me **whether I was coming**.  ✓ He asked me **if I was coming**.

A yes/no question needs a complementizer, and *that* is for statements only. Use *whether* — not *if* — after a preposition, before *or not*, and before an infinitive: *We discussed **whether or not** to go*, never *if or not*.

### The reporting verb and its objects

**5. *said me*.**

> ✗ She **said me** she was tired.  
> ✓ She **told me** she was tired.  ✓ She **said** she was tired.  ✓ She **said to me** that she was tired.

*Say* takes no indirect object without *to*.

**6. *told to me*.**

> ✗ He **told to me** the truth.  
> ✓ He **told me** the truth.

*Tell* takes its person object bare. The mirror image of mistake 5, and learners routinely produce both.

**7. *tell* with no listener before *that*.**

> ✗ He **told that** he would be late.  
> ✓ He **told me that** he would be late.  ✓ He **said that** he would be late.

*Tell* requires someone told. The fixed exceptions take no listener because the object is part of the idiom: *tell the truth*, *tell a lie*, *tell a story*, *tell a joke*, *tell the time*, *tell the difference*, *tell fortunes*.

**8. *suggested me to*.**

> ✗ My advisor **suggested me to apply** for the fellowship.  
> ✓ My advisor **suggested that I apply** for the fellowship.  ✓ My advisor **suggested applying** for the fellowship.

*Suggest* never takes an object + infinitive. Same pattern for *recommend*, *propose*, *insist*, and *demand*. Note the bare subjunctive *apply*, not *applies* and not *should apply* — the American form is above under [the mandative subjunctive](#the-mandative-subjunctive-american-be-british-should-be).

**9. *explained me the rule*.**

> ✗ She **explained me** the rule.  
> ✓ She **explained the rule to me**.

*Explain*, *describe*, *announce*, *mention*, *repeat*, and *report* all take *to* before the listener.

**10. Missing *to* after *ask* or *tell* + object.**

> ✗ She asked me **close** the door.  ✓ She asked me **to close** the door.  
> ✗ He told me **don't be** late.  ✓ He told me **not to be** late.

Reported commands and requests take the full infinitive, and the negative is *not to*, never *don't*.

**11. Mismatched verb for the speech act.**

> ✗ He **said me sorry**.  ✓ He **apologized**.  
> ✗ He said **let's go** to the beach.  ✓ He **suggested** that we go to the beach.

### Tense

**12. Over-shift: pushing a past perfect further back.**

> ✗ She said she **had had been** waiting for an hour.  
> ✓ She said she **had been** waiting for an hour.

The past perfect is the end of the line. *Would*, *could*, *should*, *might*, *ought to*, and *used to* likewise have nowhere to go and stay as they are.

**13. Over-shift: moving an unreal past.**

> ✗ He said that if he **had had** a car, he would drive me. (from "If I had a car…")  ✓ …if he **had** a car…  
> ✗ She said she wished she **had known** the answer. (from "I wish I knew the answer")  ✓ …she wished she **knew** the answer.

**14. Turning deductive *must* into obligation.**

> ✗ She said he **had to be** at work. (from "He must be at work" — a deduction)  
> ✓ She said he **must be** at work.

*Must* of deduction stays. *Must* of obligation may become *had to*: "You must sign this" → *She said I **had to** sign it.*

### Pronouns, time, and place

**15. Pronoun not shifted.**

> ✗ Marco said that **I** would call **you** tomorrow. (meaning Marco would call me)  
> ✓ Marco said that **he** would call **me** the next day.

Every pronoun is anchored to whoever is speaking; when the speaker changes, the anchors move with them. See [pronouns](../../parts-of-speech/02-pronouns/README.md).

**16. Shifting a time word when the time has not changed.**

> ✗ (said at 9 a.m., reported at 3 p.m. the same day) He said he'd stop by **the next day**.  
> ✓ He said he'd stop by **tomorrow**.

*Tomorrow* becomes *the next day* only when the report happens on a different day. If you are still standing inside the same *today*, the original word is still correct and changing it makes the sentence wrong. The same applies to *here*, *this*, and *now* — and note that backshift disappears entirely after a present-tense reporting verb: *She **says** she **is** exhausted*, never *she says she was exhausted*.

## Practice

**A. Report these.**

1. "If it snows tomorrow, I'll work from home," Ellen said. (reported a week later)
2. "If I'd known you were in town yesterday, I would have called," Ben said. (reported the same evening)
3. "I wish I could take the whole week off," Marcus said.
4. "What a disaster this has been!" the coach said. (give two versions)
5. "Sorry I missed your call," Dana said.
6. "I'm heading out. Did you lock the back door? Text me when you get home," my father said.

**B. Fix the mistakes.**

7. She asked me where did I put the keys?
8. He told that his advisor suggested him to apply, and then he said me the deadline.

**C. Register and mode.**

9. Choose the better option and say why. (a) The company (said / admitted) its servers had been offline for six hours — the outage was announced voluntarily and nobody has disputed it. (b) Ortiz (2021) (argues / argued) that memory (is / was) reconstructive — the claim is still accepted.
10. Rewrite as free indirect speech: *She wondered whether she had remembered to lock the door, and thought that she would never be able to sleep now.*

**D. American forms.**

11. Fix the British forms and say what each one is: *The board insisted that the auditor should be present, and the chair enquired whether the report had been filed. She apologised for the delay.*
12. Report these: (a) "I recommend that she not sign anything today," the lawyer said. (b) "He was there the whole time, I'm telling you," she insisted.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Ellen said that if it snowed the next day, she would work from home.** — Type 1 shifts in both clauses (*snows* → *snowed*, *'ll* → *would*), and because the report comes a week later, *tomorrow* → *the next day*. Not *if it would snow*.

2. **Ben said that if he had known I was in town yesterday, he would have called.** — Type 3 cannot backshift: *had known* and *would have called* stay exactly as they are, and only the pronouns move. *Yesterday* stays because the report is the same day; changing it to *the day before* would be wrong (mistake 16).

3. **Marcus said he wished he could take the whole week off.** — *Wish* backshifts to *wished*; the clause after it does not, and *could* has no further past form.

4. **The coach said what a disaster it had been.** / **The coach complained that the season had been a disaster.** — The first keeps the exclamative clause with no inversion and no exclamation point; the second names the act and flattens to a *that*-clause. Either works; the second is better in formal prose.

5. **Dana apologized for missing my call.** — *Sorry* is a performance, not a proposition, so a verb replaces the words entirely. *She said that sorry* is impossible.

6. **My father said (that) he was heading out, asked whether I had locked the back door, and told me to text him when I got home.** — Three speech acts, three reporting verbs, one shared subject. The question loses its inversion and gains *whether*, the command becomes *to text*, and *when I get home* backshifts to *when I got home*.

7. **She asked me where I had put the keys.** — Three errors: the inversion *did I put*, the do-support, and the question mark. The sentence is a statement about a question, so it ends in a period. (*Where I put the keys* is also fine if the keys are still lost.)

8. **He told me that his advisor had suggested that he apply, and then he told me the deadline.** — Four errors: *told* with no listener; *suggested him to apply* (*suggest* takes a *that*-clause with the bare subjunctive *apply*, or a gerund); *said me* (*say* takes no bare indirect object); and *suggested* → *had suggested*, since the advice preceded the telling.

9. (a) **said** — the outage was disclosed voluntarily and is uncontested, so *admitted* would falsely imply a prior denial or a cover-up. (b) **argues** or **argued**, but **is** either way — an academic claim that still stands is not backshifted, and *was* would signal that the idea has since been abandoned. Present-tense *argues* is the safer default outside APA.

10. **Had she remembered to lock the door? She was never going to be able to sleep now.** — The reporting clauses (*she wondered*, *she thought*) and the *that* are deleted; the question recovers its inverted direct-speech word order; third person and past tense carry over from the indirect version; and *now* sits beside a past tense, which is the signature of free indirect speech.

11. **The board insisted that the auditor be present, and the chair inquired whether the report had been filed. She apologized for the delay.** — Three British forms. *Should be* is the British mandative subjunctive; American English uses the bare *be*, and here *should* also risks being read as advice rather than as the demand the board made. *Enquired* is the British spelling — American *inquire* covers both the "ask" and the "investigate" senses. *Apologised* is the British *-ise*; American reporting verbs take *-ize*. Nothing else moves: *had been filed* is correct backshift either way.

12. (a) **The lawyer recommended that she not sign anything that day.** — The subjunctive *not sign* has no tense and cannot move, so only the reporting verb shifts and *today* becomes *that day* if the report comes later. Not *that she didn't sign*, not *that she doesn't sign*, and not *that she should not sign*, which is British. (b) **She insisted that he had been there the whole time.** — Here *insist* reports a claim, not a demand, so the *that*-clause takes an ordinary tensed verb and backshifts normally. In speech, *she insisted that he was there the whole time* is just as good.
