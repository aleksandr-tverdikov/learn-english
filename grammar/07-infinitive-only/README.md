# Infinitive only

> Every word in English after which a verb has to appear as *to do* — and, kept strictly apart, every word after which it has to appear as plain *do*, because *persuade him to wait* and *make him wait* are built by two different rules and mixing them is the error.

[← All grammar topics](../README.md) &middot; [Library home](../../README.md)

English has three things it can put in the slot after a word when what goes there is another verb: the ***-ing* form** (*enjoy **going***), the ***to*-infinitive** (*decide **to go***), and the **bare infinitive** (*let him **go***). [The gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md) is the first of the three. **This page is the other two** — and its whole design is that it never lets them touch, because the sentence that gets a Russian speaker caught is almost never ✗ *enjoy to read*. It is ✗ *He made me to wait*, which is the two infinitives confused with each other.

So this page has two halves, and they are labeled:

- **[Part A](#part-a--only-a-to-infinitive)** — the words that require ***to* + verb**: *decide to go*, *persuade him to go*, *eager to go*, *a chance to go*, *too late to go*.
- **[Part B](#part-b--only-a-bare-infinitive-no-to)** — the words that require the **verb alone**: *must go*, *let him go*, *made him go*, *saw him go*, *had better go*, *Why go?*

The organizing idea is two sentences long, one per part.

**The *to*-infinitive points forward.** It is the same *to* as in *to Denver*, worn down to a grammatical particle, and it has kept the direction. Everything in Part A names something **not done yet** — an intention (*plan to go*), a push toward an act (*urge him to go*), a readiness for one (*willing to go*), an opportunity for one (*a chance to go*), a purpose (*came here to go*). That is why the forward-looking half of English verbs takes *to* and the backward-looking half takes *-ing*: *I hope to see it* against *I remember seeing it*.

**The bare infinitive appears when the word in front has already done the grammatical work.** A modal carries tense and mood by itself, so *must* needs no help: *must go*. A causative or perception verb has swallowed a whole small clause — *him go*, *her leave* — and a subject with its verb does not take *to*. An auxiliary *do* has stripped the tense off the main verb and is holding it: *Did you go?* The bare form is simply what is left over when another word is carrying the load.

**How to read the page.** Six questions, in the order worth asking them:

```
  Is the word in front a modal — will, can, must, should…?    →  bare verb.    §10
  Is it let, make, have (causative) or bid?                   →  bare verb.    §11   but GET takes to
  Is it see, hear, watch, notice, feel…?                      →  bare verb.    §13   or -ing, with a difference
  Is it had better, would rather, why (not), all I did was?   →  bare verb.    §15 §16
  Is it do / does / did doing auxiliary work?                 →  bare verb.    §17
  Is it anything else on this page?                           →  to + verb.    §1–§9
  Is it on none of these lists?                               →  ../06-gerund-only/README.md  or  §19
```

Section [§19](#19-what-is-not-on-either-list) is the safety rail, and it matters more here than on the gerund page. *Remember*, *stop*, *try*, *regret*, *go on*, *mean* and *need* take **both** an infinitive and a gerund with a real change of meaning, and *begin*, *start*, *continue*, *like*, *love*, *hate* and *prefer* take both with no change at all. None of them are on either list here. A reader who walks away thinking the infinitive is always safe has been made worse, not better.

## The form itself

Before the lists: the infinitive is not one shape. It is six, and three of them are the reason an advanced sentence goes wrong.

| Form | *to*-infinitive | Bare infinitive | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **simple** | *to go* | *go* | the default — same time as the main verb, or later |
| **continuous** | *to be going* | *be going* | in progress at that moment — *He seems **to be enjoying** it.* |
| **perfect** | *to have gone* | *have gone* | **earlier** than the main verb — *She's said **to have left** in June.* |
| **perfect continuous** | *to have been going* | *have been going* | in progress up to that moment — *He appears **to have been drinking**.* |
| **passive** | *to be taken* | *be taken* | the subject is acted on — *The form needs **to be signed**.* |
| **perfect passive** | *to have been taken* | *have been taken* | earlier, and acted on — *It's believed **to have been stolen**.* |

Four mechanical facts go with the table.

**1. The negative goes in front of *to*, not after it.** This is the single most reliable spelling error on the page.

> I decided **not to call**. — ✗ *I decided to not call* is heard, but *not to* is the standard order  
> He **asked me not to wait**. — ✗ *asked me to not wait* &middot; ***In order not to*** *wake the baby, we used the side door.*

**2. The perfect infinitive is how English says the second verb happened first.** It is not a fancy alternative to the simple one; it moves the time.

> He **seems to be** tired. — now &middot; He **seems to have been** tired. — then  
> I'm sorry **to have kept** you waiting. — the keeping is over &middot; She was **to have chaired** the meeting. — she was scheduled to, and did not

**3. The split infinitive is fine in American English.** *To boldly go*, *to really understand*, *to more than double*. The rule against it was invented in the nineteenth century by analogy with Latin, where the infinitive is one word and cannot be split. Put the adverb where the meaning wants it, and only move it if the split sounds clumsy — *to quickly and without any further discussion adjourn* is bad writing, not bad grammar.

**4. The *to* can stand alone when the verb is recoverable.** English deletes the verb and keeps the particle; this is called *to*-ellipsis, and it is everywhere in speech.

> "Are you coming?" "I'd **like to**." — not ✗ *I'd like.* &middot; I never went, though I meant **to**.  
> You don't have **to** if you don't want **to**. — and after a bare trigger there is nothing to strand: *"Will you go?" "I might."*

## Part A — only a *to*-infinitive

Nine lists. The first four are inventories of words — verbs, verbs with an object, adjectives, nouns — and the last five are constructions, which means they are rules and can be applied to words you have never seen.

### 1. Verbs that take a *to*-infinitive

No object in between, and no gerund ever. This is the part of Part A that is not a rule: *decide* takes *to* and *consider* takes *-ing*, *refuse* takes *to* and *deny* takes *-ing*, *offer* takes *to* and *suggest* takes *-ing*, and nothing in the meaning predicts it.

What makes the list learnable is the forward lean described above. Almost every verb here is about **an act that has not happened yet at the moment the main verb is true** — deciding on it, hoping for it, agreeing to it, failing at it, promising it, refusing it. Compare the gerund list, which is dominated by stopping, avoiding, enjoying and admitting things that already exist as events.

#### Four notes that decide sentences

**1. *Want*, *wish*, *would like* and *expect* never take a *that*-clause.** This is the Russian speaker's signature error and it gets its own row in [§20](#20-for-russian-speakers). Russian *чтобы* opens a clause; English opens an object plus an infinitive.

> ✗ *I **want that you come** tomorrow.* → *I **want you to come** tomorrow.*  
> ✗ *We **expect that he signs** it today.* → *We **expect him to sign** it today.* &middot; ✗ *She'**d like that we stay**.* → *She'**d like us to stay**.*

*Wish* does take a clause, but only in the unreal sense, with a past-tense verb — *I wish you **were** here* — which is a different construction entirely and belongs to [conditionals](../03-conditionals/README.md).

**2. *Seem*, *appear*, *happen*, *prove*, *tend* and *turn out* do not describe what the subject does.** They are raising verbs: the subject belongs to the infinitive, not to them. *She seems to like it* does not mean she is doing any seeming; it means *It seems that she likes it*. The practical consequence is that these verbs accept weather *it* and existential *there*, which real action verbs do not.

> **It seems to be** raining. &middot; ***There appear to be** two copies.*  
> *He **happened to be** standing right there.* = *It happened that he was standing there.* &middot; *The story **turned out to be** true.*

**3. *Ask* splits by who does the act.** *Ask to do* = ask permission for yourself. *Ask somebody to do* = make a request of them. Both are correct and they are not synonyms.

> He **asked to leave** early. — he wanted permission &middot; He **asked me to leave** early. — he wanted me gone  
> The same split runs through *beg*, *expect* and *want* — the ◆ verbs above, all covered in [§2](#2-verb-object-to-infinitive).

**4. *Mean* is on this list only in one of its two senses.** *Mean to do* = intend. *Mean doing* = involve, entail — and that one is on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md). See [§19](#19-what-is-not-on-either-list).

> I **meant to tell** you. — I intended to &middot; A rewrite **means starting** over. — it entails starting over

### 2. Verb, object, *to*-infinitive

The frame is **verb + somebody + *to* + verb**, and what it means, nearly always, is that the subject is doing something to get the object to act: advising them, allowing them, forcing them, reminding them, paying them. The person named is the one who performs the second verb.

> *The doctor **advised him to rest**.* — he rests  
> *They **forced us to sign**.* — we sign  
> *I **reminded her to bring** the receipt.* — she brings it

**Reading the table.** ◆ marks a verb that switches to ***-ing* when there is no object** — the one place where this page and [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md) describe the same word, and they are worked out under the table.

#### The ◆ verbs: the one place this page and the gerund page touch

Five of these verbs have **two patterns, and the object is the switch.** Put a person in the slot and you get an infinitive; leave the slot empty and you get *-ing*. This is not a choice and not a register difference — it is one verb with two grammars.

| Verb | No object → ***-ing*** | With an object → ***to*-infinitive** |
|---|---|---|
| *advise* | *I'd **advise waiting** a week.* | *I'd **advise you to wait** a week.* |
| *allow* | *We don't **allow smoking** in here.* | *They **allowed us to smoke** on the deck.* |
| *permit* | *We don't **permit parking** in the fire lane.* | *The guard **permitted us to park** out back.* |
| *require* | *This **requires rewriting**.* | *The law **requires employers to post** it.* |
| *forbid* | *The lease **forbids subletting**.* | *She **forbade him to sublet**.* &middot; *…**from subletting**.* |
| *recommend* | *I **recommend booking** early.* | *I **recommend that you book** early.* — **never** an infinitive |

The full *-ing* halves of those rows live on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md), which carries the same table from the other side. Between the two pages the verb is covered; on either page alone it is half covered, which is why both pages point here.

**And the flat prohibition.** *Suggest* and *recommend* do **not** take object + infinitive, however natural it sounds and however many of the verbs beside them do. There are exactly two correct constructions and neither uses *to*.

> ✗ *He **suggested me to leave** early.* &middot; ✓ *He **suggested leaving** early.* &middot; ✓ *He **suggested that I leave** early.*  
> ✗ *They **recommended us to try** the pier.* &middot; ✓ *They **recommended trying** the pier.* &middot; ✓ *They **recommended that we try** the pier.*

The *that*-clause takes the base form — *that I **leave***, not ✗ *that I leaves* — because it is a subjunctive. The mechanics are in [reported speech](../01-reported-speech/README.md), which is also where *tell somebody to do*, *ask somebody to do*, *order somebody to do* and *warn somebody not to do* are handled as reporting patterns rather than as infinitive patterns.

#### Three more things the object slot does

**1. The negative goes in front of *to*.** Always, and this is where learners put it in the wrong place most often.

> She **told me not to wait**. — ✗ *told me to not wait*, ✗ *didn't tell me to wait* (a different meaning)  
> They **warned us not to swim** out there.  
> *He **asked me not to mention** it.*

**2. *Get* takes *to* and *make*, *have* and *let* do not.** This is the fault line between Part A and Part B, and it runs through four verbs that mean nearly the same thing. It is laid out in full in [§11](#11-let-make-have-bid).

> I **got him to answer** the phone. — ***to*** &middot; I **made him answer** the phone. — **no *to***  
> I **had him answer** the phone. — **no *to*** &middot; I **let him answer** the phone. — **no *to***

**3. The passive puts the object in front and keeps the *to*.** Most of the verbs in this section passivize cleanly, and the result is one of the commonest shapes in written English.

> *We were **instructed to wait** in the hall.* &middot; *Passengers are **required to show** ID at the gate.* &middot; *He was **ordered to pay** restitution.*

*Make* is the one verb where the passive **adds** a *to* that the active did not have, and that is [§12](#12-the-trap-make-takes-to-in-the-passive), the most missed item on this page.

### 3. Adjectives that take a *to*-infinitive

An adjective with a preposition after it takes *-ing* — *good at cooking*, *afraid of flying* — and that is [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md). An adjective with **nothing** after it takes an infinitive. The presence or absence of a preposition decides it, and there is no third possibility.

> She's **good at fixing** things. — preposition → *-ing*  
> She's **ready to fix** it. — no preposition → *to*

#### *The first*, *the last*, *the only*, *the next*

Ordinals and superlatives in front of a noun pull an infinitive after it. The infinitive is doing the job of a relative clause, and the two are interchangeable.

| Frame | Example | The long way |
|---|---|---|
| *the first to do* | *She was **the first to arrive**.* | *…the first person who arrived* |
| *the last to do* | *He's always **the last to leave**.* | *…the last one who leaves* |
| *the only to do* | *It's **the only one to survive** the fire.* | *…the only one that survived* |
| *the next to do* | *You're **the next to go** in.* | *…the next person who goes in* |
| *the best to do* | *That's **the best place to eat** downtown.* | *…the best place where you can eat* |

> She was **the first woman to hold** the seat.  
> That was **the last thing to cross** my mind.  
> He's **the only one to have read** the whole report. — perfect infinitive, because he read it earlier

#### *Afraid*, *sorry* and the pairs that split

Four adjectives take **both** a preposition + *-ing* and a bare infinitive, and the two mean different things. This is the boundary between this page and the gerund page drawn inside a single word.

| Adjective | + preposition + *-ing* | + *to*-infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| *afraid* | *afraid of **falling*** — the thing that frightens you | *afraid **to look*** — the fear stops you acting |
| *sorry* | *sorry for **keeping** you waiting* — regret about the past | *sorry **to bother** you* — apologizing for what you are doing now |
| *ashamed* | *ashamed of **losing** my temper* — the past act | *ashamed **to admit** it* — reluctance right now |
| *interested* | *interested in **learning** Portuguese* — the subject | *interested **to hear** what she said* — reacting to news |

> I'm **afraid of flying**, so I drove. — the general fear &middot; I'm **afraid to look** at the bill. — this specific act, right now  
> I'm **sorry for missing** your call. — it already happened &middot; I'm **sorry to tell** you the position has been filled. — I am telling you now

#### *Easy*, *hard*, *difficult*, *impossible*: the pattern with the missing object

These four behave in a way no other adjectives do. *The book is easy to read* has a subject, *the book*, that is really the **object** of *read* — and English will not let you say it twice.

> ✓ *This book is **easy to read**.*  
> ✗ *This book is **easy to read it**.* — the object is already the subject  
> ✓ *It's **easy to read** this book.* — the other arrangement, with *it* holding the subject slot

The same pattern runs through *hard*, *difficult*, *impossible*, *tough*, *simple*, *fun*, *a pleasure*, *nice* and *dangerous*:

> The instructions are **impossible to follow**. — ✗ *impossible to follow them*  
> He's **hard to get** hold of. &middot; That chair is **dangerous to sit** in.  
> She's **a pleasure to work** with. — the preposition stays stranded at the end, and that is correct

#### *Keen*, and the prediction adjectives

**American English says *keen to do*; British English says *keen on doing*.** Both are grammatical everywhere and each marks the writer. Americans do use *keen on* — but for liking a thing, not for wanting to act: *I'm not **keen on** the new logo.*

And *sure*, *certain*, *bound*, *likely*, *unlikely* and *liable* take an infinitive when the prediction is **the speaker's**, and a clause when the confidence is **the subject's**. The two sentences look identical and are not.

> *American:* We're **keen to hear** what they propose. &middot; *British:* We're **keen on hearing** what they propose.  
> She's **sure to win**. — I am predicting it &middot; She's **sure that she'll win**. — she is the confident one

### 4. Nouns that take a *to*-infinitive

Most of these are nouns made from the verbs and adjectives in [§1](#1-verbs-that-take-a-to-infinitive) and [§3](#3-adjectives-that-take-a-to-infinitive), and they keep what the source word took. *Decide to* → *the decision to*; *able to* → *the ability to*; *refuse to* → *a refusal to*; *willing to* → *the willingness to*. When you meet a new abstract noun, check what its verb takes.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *ability* | *the ability to do* | *She has **the ability to explain** it simply.* |
| *ambition* | *an ambition to do* | *His **ambition to run** for office is no secret.* |
| *attempt* | *an attempt to do* | *An **attempt to reopen** the case failed.* |
| *chance* | *a chance to do* — the opportunity | *Give me **a chance to explain**.* |
| *decision* | *the decision to do* | ***The decision to close** came from headquarters.* |
| *desire* | *a desire to do* | *She has no **desire to go** back.* |
| *determination* | *the determination to do* | *His **determination to finish** carried the team.* |
| *duty* | *a duty to do* | *You have **a duty to report** it.* |
| *effort* | *an effort to do* | *It was **an effort to look** interested.* |
| *failure* | *a failure to do* | ***Failure to appear** is a separate charge.* |
| *need* | *a need to do* | *There's no **need to shout**.* |
| *offer* | *an offer to do* | *We turned down their **offer to mediate**.* |
| *opportunity* | *an opportunity to do* | *It's **an opportunity to start** over.* |
| *permission* | *permission to do* | *You need **permission to film** in here.* |
| *plan* | *a plan to do* | ***The plan to widen** the road is on hold.* |
| *promise* | *a promise to do* | *He broke **his promise to call**.* |
| *proposal* | *a proposal to do* | ***A proposal to merge** the two offices leaked.* |
| *reason* | *a reason to do* | *There's no **reason to panic**.* |
| *refusal* | *a refusal to do* | ***His refusal to answer** hurt him in the end.* |
| *reluctance* | *a reluctance to do* | *There's a real **reluctance to spend** right now.* |
| *request* | *a request to do* | *We got **a request to hold** the shipment.* |
| *right* | *the right to do* | *You have **the right to remain** silent.* |
| *tendency* | *a tendency to do* | *He has **a tendency to overexplain**.* |
| *time* | *time to do* | *There's no **time to argue**.* |
| *way* | *a way to do* | *There must be **a way to fix** this.* |
| *willingness* | *a willingness to do* | *Her **willingness to help** made the difference.* |
| *wish* | *a wish to do* | *She expressed **a wish to be** left alone.* |

#### The nouns that take both, and mean different things

Five of these also appear on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md) with a preposition and *-ing*, and the two versions are not synonyms: *the **chance of winning*** is the probability and *a **chance to win*** is the opportunity; *his **reason for leaving*** is the motive he had and *a **reason to leave*** is a justification that exists. The same split runs through *way of / way to*, *time doing / time to do* and *need for / need to*.

> There's little **chance of getting** a table. — the odds are bad &middot; Give me **a chance to explain**. — an opportunity  
> ✗ *There's no **point to argue**.* → *There's no **point in arguing**.* — *point* is the one that never takes an infinitive

### 5. The frames

Everything above is a list of words. This section is five constructions, which means you can apply them to a word you have never met.

| Frame | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **extraposed *it*** | *It's + adj + to do* | ***It's easy to forget** the second step.* |
| **with a subject** | *It's + adj + for sb + to do* | ***It's important for the tenant to sign** both copies.* |
| **judgment adjective** | *It's + adj + of sb + to do* | ***It was kind of you to drive** all that way.* |
| ***too*** | *too + adj + to do* | *He's **too tired to drive**.* |
| ***too*, with a subject** | *too + adj + for sb + to do* | *The print is **too small for her to read**.* |
| ***enough*, adjective** | *adj + enough + to do* | *She's **old enough to vote**.* |
| ***enough*, noun** | *enough + noun + to do* | *We have **enough money to cover** it.* |
| ***so … as to*** | *so + adj + as to do* — formal | *Would you be **so good as to sign** here?* |
| **indefinite + *to*** | *nothing / something / a lot + to do* | *There's **nothing to do** in that town.* |

#### *For* and *of*: the split that gets missed

Both frames put a person into *It's + adjective + to do*, and the preposition is chosen by **what the adjective is describing**.

- ***for*** — the adjective describes **the situation**: *important*, *easy*, *hard*, *necessary, possible, unusual, dangerous, cheaper, better*.
- ***of*** — the adjective describes **the person**: *kind, nice, generous, rude, stupid, careless, brave, clever, silly, thoughtful, wrong*.

> **It's important for you to be** on time. — being on time is important &middot; **It was kind of you to be** on time. — you were kind  
> ✗ *It was kind **for you** to drive us.* → *It was kind **of you** to drive us.* &middot; ✗ *It's difficult **of me** to say.* → *It's difficult **for me** to say.*

The *of* frame has a second form that says the same thing with the person as subject: *You were kind to drive us.* The *for* frame does not: ✗ *You are important to be on time* means something else entirely.

#### *Too*: the negative that is built in

*Too* does not mean *very*. It means **more than is workable**, so the sentence is already negative and never needs *not*.

> He's **too tired to drive**. = he is not going to drive &middot; It's **too late to call** them.  
> ✗ *He's **too tired to not drive**.* — nothing to fix; the negative is in *too*  
> ✗ *He's **very tired to drive**.* — *very* is not *too*, and this is not a sentence

And, exactly as with *easy to read* in [§3](#3-adjectives-that-take-a-to-infinitive), **the object is not repeated**:

> The box is **too heavy to lift**. — ✗ *too heavy to lift it*  
> The coffee's **too hot to drink**. — ✗ *too hot to drink it*  
> But when a subject is named, the object still stays out: *The box is **too heavy for me to lift***. — ✗ *for me to lift it*

#### *Enough*: the word that moves

*Enough* goes **after** an adjective or adverb and **before** a noun. Getting it backwards is the commonest word-order error in this section.

> She's **old enough to vote**. — ✗ *enough old* &middot; He didn't run **fast enough to qualify**. — ✗ *enough fast*  
> We have **enough money to cover** it. — ✗ *money enough*, except as an archaism &middot; There aren't **enough chairs for everyone to sit** down.

#### *There's nothing to do*

*Nothing*, *something*, *anything*, *a lot*, *plenty*, *much* and *little* all take an infinitive directly, and the infinitive is again a reduced relative clause: *nothing to do* = *nothing that you can do*.

> There's **nothing to eat** in the house. &middot; I've got **something to tell** you.  
> Is there **anything to drink**? &middot; There's **a lot to think about**. &middot; There's **plenty to do**.  
> ✗ *There's nothing **for doing**.* → *There's nothing **to do**.*

### 6. Purpose

English answers *why?* with an infinitive. This is the single most frequent use of *to* + verb in the language, and it is also where Russian *чтобы* does the most damage.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *to* | *to do* | *I came here **to study** engineering.* |
| *in order to* | *in order to do* — explicit, slightly formal | *We left early **in order to beat** the traffic.* |
| *so as to* | *so as to do* — formal, mostly written | *She spoke quietly **so as to avoid** waking him.* |
| *in order not to* | *in order not to do* | *He took the stairs **in order not to run** into her.* |
| *so as not to* | *so as not to do* | *I closed it slowly **so as not to make** a sound.* |
| *so that* | *so that + clause* — when the subject changes | *I called **so that she would know**.* |

**Plain *to* is the default.** *In order to* and *so as to* are available when you want the purpose marked explicitly, and they are the only option in the **negative**: ✗ *I took the stairs not to run into her* is not English, and ✗ *I took the stairs to not run into her* is a spoken hedge at best.

> I stopped **to ask** directions. — purpose &middot; I stopped **asking** directions. — a different sentence: I quit doing it  
> We moved the meeting **in order to give** everyone time to read it. &middot; She turned the radio down **so as not to wake** the baby.

#### The two wrong ways, and they are both common

**✗ *for to*** is the literal translation of *для того чтобы* and does not exist in modern English. **✗ *for doing*** exists, but it names **the function of a thing**, not the purpose of an action.

> ✗ *I came here **for to study** English.* → *I came here **to study** English.*  
> ✗ *I came here **for studying** English.* → *I came here **to study** English.*  
> ✗ *He stopped **for asking** directions.* → *He stopped **to ask** directions.* &middot; ✗ *She called **for to confirm**.* → *She called **to confirm**.*

What *for* + *-ing* really marks is **the function of a thing**, where the infinitive marks **the purpose of an action** — and the subject usually gives it away, a thing for the first and a person for the second.

> A corkscrew is **for opening** bottles. &middot; This app is **for tracking** expenses. — what the thing does  
> I went out **to buy** a corkscrew. &middot; I downloaded it **to track** my expenses. — why the person acted

***For* + a noun is perfectly fine.** The error is *for* + a verb, not *for* itself.

> I came here **for the conference**. ✓  
> She went out **for milk**. ✓  
> He's here **for an interview**. ✓

#### *For somebody to do*: purpose with a different actor

When the second verb has a **different subject**, English keeps *for* and adds the person before the infinitive. This is the pattern that looks like the error and is not.

> I bought a book **for you to read** on the plane. &middot; There's no need **for anyone to stay** late.  
> He held the door open **for us to get** the couch through. &middot; It would be better **for her to hear** it from you.

And when the sentence needs a modal — *can*, *could*, *would* — the infinitive cannot carry it, so *so that* takes over:

> I wrote it down **so that I wouldn't forget**. — ✗ *so that not to forget*  
> Leave the light on **so that they can find** the door.

### 7. Question word plus *to*-infinitive

A question word takes an infinitive after verbs of knowing, deciding, asking and explaining. The whole phrase is a compressed indirect question: *I don't know what to say* = *I don't know what I should say*.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *how to* | *how to do* | *Nobody showed me **how to reset** it.* |
| *what to* | *what to do* | *I have no idea **what to tell** him.* |
| *where to* | *where to do* | *We haven't decided **where to eat**.* |
| *when to* | *when to do* | *She knows **when to stop** talking.* |
| *whether to* | *whether to do* | *They're debating **whether to appeal**.* |
| *who to* &middot; *whom to* | *who to do* | *I didn't know **who to call**.* |
| *which + noun to* | *which one to do* | *He couldn't decide **which model to buy**.* |
| *how much / many to* | *how much to do* | *Tell me **how much to pour**.* |

The verbs in front of this pattern are a small, closed set: *know, learn, decide, wonder, ask, tell, show, teach, explain, forget, remember, discuss, consider, figure out, work out, have no idea, be unsure*.

> Can you show me **how to fold** it? &middot; We still have to figure out **where to park**. &middot; She's teaching him **how to swim**.

#### ✗ *Why to* does not exist

Every other question word takes an infinitive. *Why* does not — and the reason is [§16](#16-why-and-why-not): *why* already has a construction with the **bare** infinitive, and the two cannot both be live.

> ✗ *I don't know **why to go**.* → *I don't know **why I should go**.*  
> ✗ *He couldn't explain **why to wait**.* → *…**why we had to wait**.*  
> But: ***Why wait**?* ✓ — a direct question, bare infinitive, [§16](#16-why-and-why-not)

#### *Whether*, not *if*

This pattern takes *whether*. *If* is fine in an indirect question with a full clause and impossible in front of an infinitive.

> ✓ *I don't know **whether to call** him.* &middot; ✗ *I don't know **if to call** him.*  
> ✓ *I don't know **if I should call** him.* — a clause, so *if* is back in play  
> *Whether or not to appeal* is the emphatic form, and *or not* can also go at the end.

### 8. *be to* and the semi-modals

Two families, joined by the fact that both start with a word that looks like an ordinary verb and end with a *to* that cannot be dropped. Between them they cover most of what English says about obligation, schedule and likelihood — which is exactly what [the modals](#10-modal-auxiliaries) in Part B say without a *to*. That is the whole reason this section and [§10](#10-modal-auxiliaries) have to be read against each other.

#### The *be* family

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *be to* | *be to do* — arrangement, instruction, formal | *The president **is to visit** Ottawa in June.* |
| *be about to* | *be about to do* — immediate future | *I **was about to call** you.* |
| *be due to* | *be due to do* — scheduled | *The flight **is due to land** at 9:15.* |
| *be supposed to* | *be supposed to do* — expected of you | *You **were supposed to call** first.* |
| *be likely to* | *be likely to do* | *Rates **are likely to hold** steady.* |
| *be bound to* | *be bound to do* — it will certainly happen | *Somebody **was bound to complain**.* |
| *be apt to* | *be apt to do* — has a habit of | *That door **is apt to stick** in humid weather.* |
| *be set to* | *be set to do* — journalistic | *The company **is set to announce** layoffs.* |

Four notes:

***Be to*** is the formal arrangement form, and in American English it is mostly news writing and official instructions. In the past it also carries a specific meaning — the plan and its failure.

> All visitors **are to sign** in at the desk. — an instruction &middot; The two sides **are to meet** again Thursday. — a scheduled arrangement  
> She **was to have chaired** the panel. — she was scheduled to, and did not &middot; If we **are to finish** by Friday, we need two more people. — the conditional use

***Be supposed to*** has two meanings and they are not related. One is obligation; the other is reputation.

> You**'re supposed to be** here at eight. — that is the rule  
> The new place on Fifth **is supposed to be** good. — that is what people say  
> It **wasn't supposed to rain** today. — the forecast said otherwise

***Be due to*** is **scheduled to**, and it has an identical twin that means **because of**. The twin takes a noun and is not an infinitive pattern at all.

> The train **is due to arrive** at 6:40. — scheduled  
> The delay was **due to** ice on the wires. — because of; ✗ *due to arrive* means nothing here

***Be about to*** is the tightest future English has — *We**'re about to sit** down to dinner* — and its negative is idiomatic: *I'**m not about to pay** for their mistake* does not mean "not immediately," it means "absolutely not."

#### The semi-modals that keep *to*

These behave like modals in meaning and like ordinary verbs in grammar. They inflect, they need *do* for questions and negatives, and they keep the *to* — which is precisely what the real modals in [§10](#10-modal-auxiliaries) do not do.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *have to* | *have to do* | *I **have to be** at the airport by six.* |
| *have got to* | *have got to do* — informal | *You'**ve got to see** this.* |
| *need to* | *need to do* | *We **need to talk** about the invoice.* |
| *ought to* | *ought to do* | *You **ought to get** that looked at.* |
| *used to* | *used to do* — past habit | *I **used to work** nights.* |
| *dare to* | *dare to do* | *Nobody **dared to say** anything.* |
| *be able to* | *be able to do* | *She wasn't **able to make** it.* |
| *had better* | see [§15](#15-had-better-would-rather-and-friends) — **no *to*** | *You'**d better go**.* |

> ✗ *I **must to go**.* → *I **must go**.* / *I **have to go**.* — *must* is a modal, *have to* is not &middot; ✗ *You **ought go**.* → *You **ought to go**.*  
> ✗ *He **needs go** now.* → *He **needs to go** now.* — the lexical *need*; the modal *need* is in [§10](#10-modal-auxiliaries)  
> ✗ *She **used work** here.* → *She **used to work** here.*

**The two *used to*s** are the one trap this family shares with the gerund page. *Used to do* is a finished past habit; *be used to doing* is being accustomed, and its *to* is a preposition, which is why *-ing* follows it. The full treatment is on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md).

> I **used to work** nights. — and I don't now  
> I'**m used to working** nights. — it stopped bothering me  
> ✗ *I'm used to **work** nights.* — a real sentence that means neither one

### 9. Passive reporting frames

English has two ways to attribute a claim without naming who made it, and the second one is an infinitive pattern. It is the dominant shape in news writing, academic prose and obituaries.

> **It is said that** he left the country in March. — the clause version  
> He **is said to have left** the country in March. — the infinitive version

The subject of the reported clause is hauled up to the front of the sentence and the rest becomes an infinitive. The meaning is identical; the second is shorter and more formal.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *be said to* | *sb is said to do* | *The house **is said to be** haunted.* |
| *be believed to* | *sb is believed to do* | *The fire **is believed to have started** in the kitchen.* |
| *be thought to* | *sb is thought to do* | *She **is thought to be** the oldest resident.* |
| *be known to* | *sb is known to do* | *The drug **is known to cause** drowsiness.* |
| *be expected to* | *sb is expected to do* | *Rates **are expected to rise** again.* |
| *be reported to* | *sb is reported to do* | *Two hikers **are reported to be** missing.* |
| *be considered to* | *sb is considered to do* | *He **is considered to be** the front-runner.* |
| *be alleged to* | *sb is alleged to do* | *The firm **is alleged to have falsified** the tests.* |
| *be supposed to* | *sth is supposed to be* — reputation | *The chowder **is supposed to be** excellent.* |

**The perfect infinitive is what shifts the time back.** This is the only moving part in the construction, and it is the one learners drop.

> *It is said that he **is** a millionaire.* → *He **is said to be** a millionaire.* — same time  
> *It is said that he **was** a millionaire.* → *He **is said to have been** a millionaire.* — earlier  
> *It is believed that the fire **started** in the kitchen.* → *The fire **is believed to have started** in the kitchen.*  
> ✗ *The fire is believed **to start** in the kitchen.* — that says it starts there regularly

**And *there is said to be*.** When the reported clause begins with *there*, *there* is what gets raised, and the infinitive keeps the *be*.

> *It is said that **there are** three copies.* → ***There are said to be** three copies.*  
> ***There is believed to be** no connection between the two cases.*  
> ***There are thought to have been** as many as forty people inside.*

More on the whole attribution system, including which verbs allow which patterns, is in [reported speech](../01-reported-speech/README.md).

## Part B — only a bare infinitive, no *to*

Nine lists again, and they are shorter, because the bare infinitive is a small, closed system. Nothing here is a matter of vocabulary: if you know the nine triggers, you know the whole pattern, and every one of them is a place where a Russian speaker's hand reaches for a *to* that must not be written.

### 10. Modal auxiliaries

The nine core modals take the bare infinitive, with no exceptions and no variation.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | *will do* | *I **will call** you tonight.* |
| *would* | *would do* | *She **would know** better than I do.* |
| *can* | *can do* | *He **can swim** a mile.* |
| *could* | *could do* | *We **could take** the ferry.* |
| *may* | *may do* | *You **may leave** when you're finished.* |
| *might* | *might do* | *It **might rain** later.* |
| *must* | *must do* | *You **must sign** both copies.* |
| *shall* | *shall do* — formal, or a first-person offer | ***Shall I wait**?* |
| *should* | *should do* | *You **should get** that looked at.* |

Five properties come as a package, and a word that has all five is a modal:

```
  1. bare infinitive after it        he must GO          ✗ he must to go
  2. no -s in the third person       she can SWIM        ✗ she cans swim
  3. no do-support                   Can you swim?       ✗ Do you can swim?
  4. not in a row with another       will be able to     ✗ will can
  5. no infinitive, no -ing form     to be able to       ✗ to can, canning
```

> ✗ *I **must to leave** now.* → *I **must leave** now.* &middot; ✗ *She **cans drive**.* → *She **can drive**.*  
> ✗ *Do you **can help** me?* → ***Can you help** me?* &middot; ✗ *He **will can** finish it tomorrow.* → *He'**ll be able to** finish it tomorrow.*

**The perfect after a modal is also bare** — *have*, not *to have*:

> He **must have left** already. &middot; She **should have called**. &middot; They **might have missed** the exit.  
> ✗ *He must **to have** left.* &middot; ✗ *He must **of** left.* — a spelling of the spoken *must've*, and wrong on paper

#### The three that keep *to*, and the two that go both ways

***Ought to***, ***have to*** and ***used to*** mean what modals mean and are built differently: the *to* is part of them. They are handled in [§8](#8-be-to-and-the-semi-modals).

***Need*** and ***dare*** are genuinely double. Each has a modal life (bare infinitive, no *-s*, no *do*) that survives almost only in the negative and the question, and an ordinary-verb life (with *to*) that is the normal American choice.

| | As a modal — **bare** | As an ordinary verb — ***to*** |
|---|---|---|
| *need* | *You **needn't wait**.* &middot; ***Need I say** more?* | *You **don't need to wait**.* &middot; *I **need to go**.* |
| *dare* | *He **daren't tell** her.* &middot; *How **dare you speak** to me like that?* | *He **doesn't dare to tell** her.* &middot; *Nobody **dared to ask**.* |

In American English the modal forms are formal or frozen. *Needn't* is British-leaning, *daren't* is rare, and *How dare you* is an idiom nobody analyzes. Write *don't need to* and *didn't dare to*, and recognize the others when you read them.

The whole modal system — meanings, past forms, degrees of certainty, and the substitutes for the forms modals do not have — is in the **[modal verb dictionary](../../parts-of-speech/03-verbs/catalog/64-modal-verbs.md)**.

### 11. *let*, *make*, *have*, *bid*

Four verbs take **object + bare infinitive**. Three of them are in daily use and the fourth is worth recognizing.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *let* | *let sb do* — permit | *They **let me keep** the old one.* |
| *make* | *make sb do* — compel | *She **made him apologize**.* |
| *have* | *have sb do* — arrange for, instruct | *I'**ll have Marcus call** you back.* |
| *bid* | *bid sb do* — formal or literary | *He **bade them enter**.* |
| *help* | *help sb (to) do* — both work | *She **helped me carry** it in.* — see [§14](#14-help-which-takes-both) |

> ✗ *They **let me to keep** it.* → *They **let me keep** it.*  
> ✗ *She **made him to apologize**.* → *She **made him apologize**.*  
> ✗ *I'll **have him to call** you.* → *I'll **have him call** you.*

#### The causative table

Five constructions, nearly the same meaning, and exactly one of them takes *to*. This table is the reason the section exists.

| Construction | *to*? | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *have **something** done* | — | somebody else did it; who is irrelevant | *We **had the roof replaced**.* |
| *get **something** done* | — | the same, slightly more effort implied | *I **got my watch fixed**.* |
| *have **somebody** do it* | **no** | you arranged it; neutral authority | *I **had the shop replace** the belt.* |
| *get **somebody** to do it* | **yes** | you persuaded them; it took work | *I **got the shop to replace** the belt.* |
| *make **somebody** do it* | **no** | you forced them | *I **made them replace** the belt.* |
| *let **somebody** do it* | **no** | you permitted them | *I **let them replace** the belt.* |

**So: *get* is the odd one out.** *Have*, *make* and *let* take the bare form; *get* takes *to*. There is no logic to recover here — it is a fact about the verb *get*, and it is the fastest error to fix on this page because it is a single word.

> I **had him sign** it. &middot; I **got him to sign** it. — both correct, both normal  
> ✗ *I **had him to sign** it.* &middot; ✗ *I **got him sign** it.*

**Two more notes.** *Let's* is *let us* with a bare infinitive inside it — *Let's go*, *Let's not argue*, ✗ *Let's to go*. And *have somebody do* is distinctly **American**; British English prefers *get somebody to do* for the same job, which is one more reason the two get mixed.

### 12. The trap: *make* takes *to* in the passive

This is the single most missed item on the page, and it is a one-line fact.

> **Active:** They **made him pay** for the damage. — bare  
> **Passive:** He **was made to pay** for the damage. — ***to***

Nothing else in the sentence changed. The bare infinitive after *make* survives only while *make* has an object of its own; in the passive that object has become the subject, the verb has nothing to hold the clause together with, and English puts the *to* back.

> ✗ *He **was made pay**.* → *He **was made to pay**.*  
> ✗ *We **were made work** overtime.* → *We **were made to work** overtime.*

**And *let* has no passive at all.** You cannot flip *They let him go* — ✗ *He was let to go* and ✗ *He was let go to see her* are both broken. English substitutes a different verb entirely:

> *They **let him go** early.* → *He **was allowed to go** early.* — ✗ *was let to go*  
> *They **let us take** photos.* → *We **were permitted to take** photos.*

(*He was let go* does exist — but it is an idiom meaning *he was fired*, and it contains no infinitive at all.)

**The perception verbs do the same thing**, for the same reason. [§13](#13-verbs-of-perception) takes a bare infinitive in the active and a *to*-infinitive in the passive:

> *Two witnesses **saw him leave**.* → *He **was seen to leave** around ten.*  
> *Somebody **heard her scream**.* → *She **was heard to scream** twice.*  
> The *-ing* version passivizes with no *to*: *He **was seen leaving** around ten.* ✓

| Verb | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| *make* | *made him pay* | *was made **to** pay* |
| *see* | *saw him leave* | *was seen **to** leave* &middot; *was seen leaving* |
| *hear* | *heard her scream* | *was heard **to** scream* &middot; *was heard screaming* |
| *let* | *let him go* | — no passive; use *was allowed to go* |
| *have* | *had him call* | — no passive in this sense |

### 13. Verbs of perception

*See*, *hear*, *watch*, *notice*, *feel*, *observe*, *listen to* and *look at* take **object + bare infinitive** — and they also take **object + *-ing***, with a difference in meaning that is real and that English speakers observe without thinking about it.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *see* | *see sb do &middot; doing* | *I **saw him cross** the street.* |
| *hear* | *hear sb do &middot; doing* | *We **heard the window break**.* |
| *watch* | *watch sb do &middot; doing* | *She **watched them load** the truck.* |
| *notice* | *notice sb do &middot; doing* | *I **noticed him hesitate**.* |
| *feel* | *feel sth do &middot; doing* | *He **felt the floor shift**.* |
| *observe* | *observe sb do &middot; doing* — formal | *The team **observed the cells divide**.* |
| *listen to* | *listen to sb do &middot; doing* | *We **listened to him play** for an hour.* |
| *look at* | *look at sb do &middot; doing* | ***Look at him go**!* |
| *catch* &middot; *find* | *catch sb doing* — ***-ing* only** | *I **caught him going** through my desk.* |

#### Bare against *-ing*: the whole action or a slice of it

| | **Bare infinitive** | ***-ing*** |
|---|---|---|
| What you witnessed | the **complete** action, start to finish | the action **in progress**, a slice of it |
| Time | over, and you saw all of it | still going when you looked |
| Example | *I **saw him cross** the street.* — he got to the other side | *I **saw him crossing** the street.* — he was halfway |
| Example | *I **heard her sing** the anthem.* — the whole song | *I **heard her singing** in the kitchen.* — a snatch of it |
| Example | *We **watched the house burn** down.* — to the ground | *We **watched the house burning**.* — the fire, in progress |
| Repetition | a single completed event, or a habit | one continuing event |

> I **saw him leave**. — he left; I witnessed it  
> I **saw him leaving**. — I caught him on the way out  
> ✗ *I **saw him to cross** the street.* — no construction; this never takes *to* in the active

Two riders. **A short, punctual action prefers the bare form** — *I saw it fall*, *I heard the door slam* — because there is no progress to describe. And ***catch* and *find* take only *-ing***, because their whole meaning is interrupting something already underway: *I **caught him listening** at the door*, ✗ *caught him listen*.

For the passive of all of these, see [§12](#12-the-trap-make-takes-to-in-the-passive): *was seen **to** leave*.

### 14. *help*, which takes both

*Help* is the one verb in English where the bare infinitive and the *to*-infinitive are freely interchangeable after an object.

> She **helped me carry** it in. &middot; She **helped me to carry** it in. — both standard  
> Would you **help me move** this? &middot; Would you **help me to move** this?

**Americans drop the *to*.** The bare form is the overwhelming default in American speech and writing; the *to* form is commoner in British English and in formal American prose. Neither is an error, and in an American sentence the bare form is what you want.

*Help* also works with **no object at all**, and there the bare form is close to obligatory:

> That doesn't **help explain** it. &middot; A second coat would **help protect** the finish. &middot; The map **helped narrow** it down.

**And *can't help* is a different word.** *Can't help* + *-ing* means "be unable to stop," and it belongs to [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md). Its one variant, *can't help but*, takes a **bare** infinitive.

> I **can't help laughing** when he does the voice. ✓  
> I **can't help but laugh** when he does the voice. ✓  
> ✗ *I can't help but laughing.* &middot; ✗ *I can't help to laugh.*

### 15. *had better*, *would rather*, and friends

A set of fixed expressions, unrelated to each other except that every one of them is followed by a bare infinitive.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *had better* | *had better do* &middot; *had better not do* | *You'**d better leave** now.* |
| *would rather* | *would rather do (than do)* | *I'**d rather stay** home than drive in this.* |
| *would sooner* | *would sooner do (than do)* | *She'**d sooner quit than apologize**.* |
| *rather than* | *rather than do* — when balanced against an infinitive | *He chose **to walk rather than take** a cab.* |
| *sooner than* | *sooner than do* | ***Sooner than ask** him, she paid for it herself.* |
| *may as well* | *may as well do* | *We **may as well start** without him.* |
| *might as well* | *might as well do* | *You **might as well tell** me.* |
| *cannot but* | *cannot but do* — formal, dated | *One **cannot but admire** the nerve of it.* |
| *do nothing but* | *do nothing but do* | *He**'s done nothing but complain** all week.* |
| *all + do + be* | *all sb did was do* | ***All she did was smile**.* |

> ✗ *You'd better **to go**.* → *You'**d better go**.* &middot; ✗ *I'd rather **to stay**.* → *I'**d rather stay**.*  
> ✗ *We might as well **to start**.* → *We **might as well start**.* &middot; ✗ *All he did was **to complained**.* → ***All he did was complain**.*

Four notes:

***Had better*** is the one that looks past-tense and is not. It is advice about the present or future, it contracts to *'d better*, and its negative is ***had better not*** — never ✗ *hadn't better*.

> You'**d better not be** late again. &middot; We'**d better get** going. — ✗ *We'd better to get going*  
> In speech Americans drop *had* entirely: *You **better call** her.* That is spoken English, not writing.

***Would rather*** takes a bare infinitive when the subject stays the same and a **past-tense clause** when it changes. The two shapes are not interchangeable.

> I'**d rather walk**. — I do the walking &middot; I'**d rather you walked**. — you do it; past tense, present meaning  
> ✗ *I'd rather you **to walk**.* &middot; ✗ *I'd rather you **walk**.* — the second is heard, and the past form is standard

***Rather than*** takes whatever form balances what it is contrasted with — bare after an infinitive, *-ing* after a gerund, a noun after a noun. The rule is parallelism, and the gerund half is covered on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md).

> He decided **to walk rather than take** the bus. — bare, to match *to walk*  
> ***Rather than waiting**, we walked home.* &middot; ***Rather than go** home, he stayed.* — at the front, either works

***All I did was …*** and ***What he did was …*** are pseudo-cleft sentences, and the verb after *was* is bare. The *to* version exists and sounds formal: *What he did was to call the police.* In speech, leave it out.

> **All she did was smile** and hand me the form. &middot; **All I want to do is sleep.** &middot; **What you need to do is call** them back.

### 16. *why* and *why not*

*Why* is the only question word that takes an infinitive, and the infinitive is **bare**. The construction makes a rhetorical question out of it: *Why wait?* does not ask for a reason, it argues against waiting.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *why* | *Why do …?* | ***Why pay** more?* |
| *why not* | *Why not do …?* — a suggestion | ***Why not ask** her yourself?* |

> **Why wait**? The sale ends Friday. &middot; **Why bother**? Nobody reads it.  
> **Why not try** the other entrance? &middot; **Why not just call** them?

Both are complete sentences with no subject and no auxiliary, and neither takes *to*: ✗ *Why to wait?*, ✗ *Why not to ask her?* This is the same fact as the ✗ *why to* prohibition in [§7](#7-question-word-plus-to-infinitive) seen from the other side — *why* has already spent its infinitive on this construction.

### 17. *do*-support and emphatic *do*

*Do*, *does* and *did* as auxiliaries take the tense off the main verb and carry it themselves. The main verb is left bare — and that is the whole explanation for the most frequent beginner error in English.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *do / does / did* — question | *Did you do …?* | ***Did you go** to the meeting?* |
| *do / does / did* — negative | *don't do* | *She **doesn't drive**.* |
| *do* — emphasis | *I do do …* | *I **do like** it — I just wouldn't buy it.* |
| *do* — imperative emphasis | *Do do …* | ***Do sit down**.* |

> ✗ ***Did you went** to the meeting?* → ***Did you go** to the meeting?* &middot; ✗ *He **doesn't likes** coffee.* → *He **doesn't like** coffee.*  
> ✗ *She **didn't came** home.* → *She **didn't come** home.* &middot; ✗ *Do you **can** swim?* → ***Can you swim**?* — modals do their own inverting

The tense is in the auxiliary, so putting it on the main verb as well writes it twice. Once you see *did* at the front of the sentence, the verb after it is bare, permanently. The full machinery — question formation, negation, and what happens in the passive — is in **[the passive, questions and clauses](../04-tenses/11-passive-questions-and-clauses.md)** and across [the tenses](../04-tenses/README.md).

**Emphatic *do*** is the same auxiliary used in a positive sentence, where nothing requires it, purely to contradict:

> I **do want** to go — I just can't afford it. &middot; She **did call**. Twice. &middot; **Do let** me know if anything changes.

### 18. The American *come see*, *go get*

American English puts *come* and *go* directly in front of another bare verb, with nothing in between.

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *come* | *come do* | ***Come see** us when you're in town.* |
| *go* | *go do* | ***Go get** your coat.* |
| *let's go* | *let's go do* | ***Let's go eat**.* |
| *come on* | *come on do* | ***Come on help** me with this.* |

> **Come sit** with us. &middot; I'll **go check** the mailbox.  
> **Let's go grab** a coffee. &middot; **Come look** at what the dog did.

**British English uses *and*:** *come **and** see us*, *go **and** get your coat*. Americans use *and* too, and the bare version is the more casual of the two. Neither is wrong in American writing; the bare one is more spoken.

**There is a real restriction**, and it is the part learners miss. The pattern works only when *come* and *go* are in their **base form** — after *to*, after a modal, in the imperative, and in the plain present with *I/you/we/they*. The moment the verb inflects, the pattern dies.

> ✓ ***Come see** us.* &middot; ✓ *I'll **go get** it.* &middot; ✓ *They **come visit** every summer.*  
> ✗ *He **came see** us.* → *He **came to see** us.* &middot; *He **came and saw** us.*  
> ✗ *She **goes get** it.* → *She **goes and gets** it.* &middot; *She **goes to get** it.*  
> ✗ *We've **gone see** the house.* → *We've **gone to see** the house.*

And *go* + *-ing* is a completely different construction for leisure activities — *go swimming*, *go shopping* — which is on [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md). *Go swim* and *go swimming* are both English and they are not the same sentence.

## 19. What is not on either list

Everything above is a place where the infinitive — one kind or the other — is the **only** option. This section marks the boundary, because a list of infinitive triggers is dangerous if the reader walks away thinking *to* is always safe. It is not. Two groups belong to neither page, and between them they hold some of the commonest verbs in English.

### Both forms, and the meaning changes

These eight take a *to*-infinitive **and** an *-ing* form, and the difference is real. Getting them backwards produces a grammatical sentence that says something you did not mean — which is worse than an error, because nobody corrects it.

| Verb | + *to*-infinitive | + *-ing* |
|---|---|---|
| *remember* | *I **remembered to lock** it.* — I didn't forget the task | *I **remember locking** it.* — I have the memory |
| *forget* | *I **forgot to call**.* — I never called | *I'll never **forget seeing** it.* — the memory stays |
| *stop* | *He **stopped to smoke**.* — he paused in order to | *He **stopped smoking**.* — he quit |
| *try* | *I **tried to open** it.* — I attempted, and may have failed | *I **tried opening** a window.* — as an experiment |
| *regret* | *We **regret to inform** you…* — bad news, delivered now | *I **regret telling** him.* — sorry about the past |
| *go on* | *She **went on to describe** the car.* — a new topic | *She **went on describing** it.* — the same one, still |
| *mean* | *I **meant to call** you.* — intended | *A rewrite **means starting** over.* — entails |
| *need* | *I **need to wash** the car.* — I'll do it | *The car **needs washing**.* — somebody should |

> He **stopped to talk** to a neighbor. — he was walking, and he stopped  
> He **stopped talking** to that neighbor. — they no longer speak  
> Four words, two orders, and no context in which one covers for the other.

### Both forms, and the meaning is the same

With these the choice is free. The infinitive leans formal and the *-ing* leans spoken, and nobody will notice either.

| Verb | Both work |
|---|---|
| *begin* | *It **began to rain**.* &middot; *It **began raining**.* |
| *start* | *She **started to cry**.* &middot; *She **started crying**.* |
| *continue* | *They **continued to argue**.* &middot; *They **continued arguing**.* |
| *like* | *I **like to swim**.* &middot; *I **like swimming**.* |
| *love* | *She **loves to cook**.* &middot; *She **loves cooking**.* |
| *hate* | *I **hate to drive** in snow.* &middot; *I **hate driving** in snow.* |
| *prefer* | *I **prefer to walk**.* &middot; *I **prefer walking**.* |
| *attempt* | *She **attempted to explain**.* &middot; *…**attempted explaining**.* |
| *intend* | *We **intend to stay**.* &middot; *We **intend staying**.* — the infinitive is far commoner |
| *neglect* | *He **neglected to mention** it.* &middot; *…**neglected mentioning** it.* |

Three riders. ***Would like / would love / would hate / would prefer* take the infinitive only** — the moment *would* appears the gerund is gone: *I'**d like to go***, ✗ *I'd like going*. **Two *-ing* forms do not go in a row**: *It's **starting to rain***, ✗ *starting raining*. **And after *begin* and *start*, a state verb takes the infinitive**: *I **began to understand***, not ✗ *began understanding*.

The other half of the boundary — the eighty-one verbs, the prepositions, and every fixed expression that requires *-ing* — is **[the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md)**. Read together, the two pages cover the whole system; read alone, either one is half of it.

## 20. For Russian speakers

**Russian does this work with a conjunction and a clause; English does it with an infinitive and no conjunction.** *Чтобы* opens a subordinate clause with its own subject and its own verb — *Я хочу, чтобы ты пришёл* — and the English equivalent has neither: *I want you to come*. The word-for-word version, ✗ *I want that you come*, is the most recognizable single error a Russian speaker makes in English, and it is produced by a correct instinct about the source language.

**And Russian has no bare/*to* distinction at all.** *Заставить подождать*, *разрешить уйти*, *помочь донести*, *увидеть, как он переходит* — all one infinitive in Russian, three different English shapes. There is nothing to transfer, which is why Part B has to be learned as a closed list rather than reasoned out.

| Russian | English | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| Я хочу, чтобы ты пришёл | *I **want you to come**.* | ✗ *I want that you come* — *want* never takes a clause |
| Он заставил меня ждать | *He **made me wait**.* | ✗ *He made me to wait* — *make* takes the bare form |
| Меня заставили заплатить | *I **was made to pay**.* | The passive **adds** the *to* — [§12](#12-the-trap-make-takes-to-in-the-passive) |
| Позволь мне уйти | ***Let me go**.* | ✗ *Let me to go* |
| Мне разрешили уйти | *I **was allowed to leave**.* | ✗ *I was let to leave* — *let* has no passive |
| Я уговорил его остаться | *I **got him to stay**.* | ✗ *I got him stay* — *get* is the one that keeps *to* |
| Я приехал сюда учиться | *I came here **to study**.* | ✗ *for to study*, ✗ *for studying* |
| Чтобы не опоздать | ***In order not to be** late* | ✗ *for not to be late*, ✗ *not to be late* on its own |
| Он предложил помочь | *He **offered to help**.* | ✗ *He proposed to help me* reads as a marriage proposal |
| Он предложил уйти пораньше | *He **suggested leaving** early.* | ✗ *He suggested me to leave* — *suggest* refuses the infinitive |
| Он посоветовал мне подождать | *He **advised me to wait**.* / *He **advised waiting**.* | The object is the switch — [§2](#2-verb-object-to-infinitive) |
| Он согласился помочь | *He **agreed to help**.* | ✗ *He accepted to help* — *accept* takes a noun |
| Я должен идти | *I **must go**.* / *I **have to go**.* | ✗ *I must to go* &middot; ✗ *I have go* |
| Мне нужно идти | *I **need to go**.* | ✗ *I need go* — the lexical *need* keeps *to* |
| Тебе лучше пойти | *You'**d better go**.* | ✗ *You'd better to go* |
| Я бы предпочёл остаться | *I'**d rather stay**.* | ✗ *I'd rather to stay* &middot; ✗ *I'd rather staying* |
| Я видел, как он переходил улицу | *I **saw him cross** / **crossing** the street.* | ✗ *I saw how he crossed* — English has no *как* clause here |
| Он помог мне донести сумки | *He **helped me carry** the bags.* | Both *carry* and *to carry* work; Americans drop the *to* |
| Слишком поздно, чтобы звонить | *It's **too late to call**.* | ✗ *too late for calling*, ✗ *too late to call them* |
| Достаточно денег, чтобы купить | ***enough money to buy** it* | ✗ *money enough*, ✗ *enough money for buying* |
| Она первая, кто пришёл | *She was **the first to arrive**.* | ✗ *the first who came* is grammatical but not the idiom |
| Я не знаю, что делать | *I don't know **what to do**.* | This one transfers exactly — but ✗ *why to do* does not |
| Я не знаю, почему ждать | *I don't know **why I should wait**.* | ✗ *why to wait* — *why* is the one question word that refuses it |
| Говорят, что он уехал | *He **is said to have left**.* | Perfect infinitive for the earlier time — [§9](#9-passive-reporting-frames) |
| Это было любезно с твоей стороны | *It was **kind of you to** drive us.* | ✗ *kind for you* — judgment adjectives take *of* |

### The repair reflex

Four steps, and they take about two seconds once they are a habit. Run them every time the second verb in a sentence is about to be written.

```
  1.  Find the word IMMEDIATELY BEFORE the slot. That word decides, not you.

  2.  Is it will/would/can/could/may/might/must/shall/should,
      or let/make/have/bid, or see/hear/watch/notice/feel,
      or had better/would rather/why/why not, or do/does/did?
                                                    →  BARE.   he must GO · let him GO

  3.  Is it get, ought, have, need, used, or dare?  →  TO.     got him TO GO · ought TO GO
      (these four look like step 2 and are not)

  4.  Is there a PREPOSITION in front of the slot?  →  -ing, and this is the wrong page.
      good at COOKING · without SAYING · look forward to SEEING

  And once more, because it is the one nobody remembers:
      They made him pay.   →   He was made TO pay.
```

One more habit, and it is the same discipline as learning a Russian verb's case government: **whenever you learn a new verb, learn what comes after it.** *Decide + to*, *enjoy + -ing*, *make + bare*, *suggest + that*. It is the only thing that makes this page unnecessary.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **want that you come**.* | *I **want you to come**.* | *Want* takes object + infinitive, never a clause. |
| ✗ *He **made me to wait** an hour.* | *He **made me wait** an hour.* | *Make* takes the bare infinitive. |
| ✗ *He **was made pay** for it.* | *He **was made to pay** for it.* | The passive of *make* puts the *to* back. [§12](#12-the-trap-make-takes-to-in-the-passive) |
| ✗ ***Let me to explain**.* | ***Let me explain**.* | *Let* takes the bare infinitive. |
| ✗ *He **was let to go** early.* | *He **was allowed to go** early.* | *Let* has no passive; *allow* replaces it. |
| ✗ *I **got him sign** the form.* | *I **got him to sign** the form.* | *Get* is the causative that keeps *to*. |
| ✗ *I'll **have him to call** you.* | *I'll **have him call** you.* | *Have* is the causative that drops it. |
| ✗ *I **saw him to cross** the street.* | *I **saw him cross** the street.* | Perception verbs take the bare form. |
| ✗ *I **must to leave** now.* | *I **must leave** now.* | Modals take the bare infinitive. |
| ✗ *You **ought go** home.* | *You **ought to go** home.* | *Ought* is the semi-modal that keeps *to*. |
| ✗ ***Did you went** to the meeting?* | ***Did you go** to the meeting?* | The auxiliary holds the tense; the verb goes bare. |
| ✗ *You'd **better to call** first.* | *You'**d better call** first.* | *Had better* takes the bare infinitive. |
| ✗ *I'd **rather to stay** home.* | *I'**d rather stay** home.* | Same family. |
| ✗ *I came here **for to study**.* | *I came here **to study**.* | ✗ *for to* does not exist in English. |
| ✗ *I came here **for studying**.* | *I came here **to study**.* | *For* + *-ing* names a thing's function, not a purpose. |
| ✗ *He **suggested me to leave**.* | *He **suggested leaving**.* / *…**that I leave**.* | *Suggest* never takes object + infinitive. |
| ✗ *He **accepted to help** us.* | *He **agreed to help** us.* | *Accept* takes a noun, not an infinitive. |
| ✗ *We **decided waiting** a week.* | *We **decided to wait** a week.* | *Decide* is infinitive-only. |
| ✗ *I'm **ready for go**.* | *I'm **ready to go**.* | Adjective with no preposition → infinitive. |
| ✗ *It was **kind for you** to drive.* | *It was **kind of you** to drive.* | Judgment adjectives take *of*. |
| ✗ *She's **enough old** to vote.* | *She's **old enough** to vote.* | *Enough* follows an adjective and precedes a noun. |
| ✗ *The box is **too heavy to lift it**.* | *The box is **too heavy to lift**.* | The object is already the subject. |
| ✗ *I don't know **why to go**.* | *I don't know **why I should go**.* | Every question word but *why* takes an infinitive. |
| ✗ *I decided **to not call**.* | *I decided **not to call**.* | The negative goes in front of *to*. |
| ✗ *He **came see** us last week.* | *He **came to see** us.* / *He **came and saw** us.* | *Come see* works only in the base form. |
| ✗ *The fire is believed **to start** in the kitchen.* | *…**to have started** in the kitchen.* | The perfect infinitive moves the time back. |
| ✗ *I'm **used to work** nights.* | *I'm **used to working** nights.* / *I **used to work** nights.* | Two different words, two different meanings. |

## Quick reference

**Part A — everything on this page that takes *to* + verb.** Anything in this block is followed by *to do* and never by the bare verb.

```
A   able · ability · advise (+obj) · afford · afraid (to) · agree · aim · allow (+obj) · ambition · anxious ·
    appear · arrange · ashamed (to) · ask · attempt (verb and noun)
B   be able to · be about to · be apt to · be bound to · be due to · be likely to · be set to · be supposed to ·
    be to · beg · bother · bound
C   can't afford · care · cause · certain · challenge · chance (to) · choose · claim · command · compel ·
    consent · content · convince
D   dare (sb) · decide · decision · delighted · demand · desire · determination · determine · determined ·
    difficult · due · duty
E   eager · easy · effort · enable · encourage · enough (+to) · entitle · entitled · expect
F   fail · failure · the first to · forbid (+obj) · force · fortunate
G   get (sb to) · glad            H   happen · happy · hard · hesitate · hire · hope · how to
I   impossible · in order (not) to · instruct · intend · invite · it's + adj + (for / of sb) + to
K   keen (American)               L   the last to · learn · liable · likely · long · lucky
M   manage · mean (= intend)      N   need (to and noun) · neglect · the next to · nothing to do
O   oblige · offer · the only to · opportunity · order · ought to
P   permission · permit (+obj) · persuade · plan (verb and noun) · prepare · prepared · pretend · proceed ·
    promise · proposal · prove
R   ready · reason (to) · refusal · refuse · reluctance · reluctant · remind · request · require (+obj) · resolve
S   sad · be said / believed / thought / known / expected / reported / considered / alleged to · seek · seem ·
    so as (not) to · something to do · sorry (to) · strive · struggle · sure · swear
T   teach · tell · tempt · tendency · threaten · time to · too + adj + to · train · turn out
U   unable · undertake · unlikely · urge (+obj) · used to (past habit)     V   volunteer · vow
W   wait · want · warn · way to · welcome · what / when / where / whether / who to · willing · willingness ·
    wish · would like
```

**Part B — everything on this page that takes the bare verb.** Short enough to memorize, and worth memorizing.

```
MODALS          will · would · can · could · may · might · must · shall · should
                …and the perfect after them:  must HAVE left · should HAVE called

CAUSATIVES      let · make · have (causative) · bid          but GET takes to
PERCEPTION      see · hear · watch · notice · feel · observe · listen to · look at · overhear
HELP            help sb (to) do — both; Americans drop the to
IDIOMS          had better · would rather · would sooner · rather than · sooner than ·
                may as well · might as well · cannot but · do nothing but · all I did was
QUESTIONS       Why wait? · Why not ask?
AUXILIARY DO    Did you go? · She doesn't drive · I DO like it · Do sit down
AMERICAN        come see · go get · let's go eat            British: come AND see

TWO-WAY         need (needn't wait / don't need to wait) · dare (daren't ask / didn't dare to ask)
```

**The passive flip — the one fact to carry out of Part B.**

```
   They made him pay.        →   He was made TO pay.
   Two witnesses saw him leave.  →   He was seen TO leave.
   Somebody heard her scream.    →   She was heard TO scream.
   They let him go.          →   He was ALLOWED TO go.      (let has no passive)
```

**The three lists, kept apart.**

| List | Members | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***to*-infinitive only** — Part A | ~250 verbs, adjectives, nouns and frames | *decided to wait* &middot; *too late to call* |
| **Bare infinitive only** — Part B | modals, *let / make / have*, perception verbs, *had better* | *must go* &middot; *made him wait* |
| ***-ing* only** — [the gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md) | 81 verbs + every preposition | *enjoy reading* &middot; *good at cooking* |
| **Both, meaning changes** — [§19](#19-what-is-not-on-either-list) | *remember, forget, stop, try, regret, go on, mean, need* | *stopped to smoke* ≠ *stopped smoking* |

## Practice

**A. Put the verb in brackets into the right form — *to* + base, bare, or *-ing*.**

1. They finally let us (go) home at six.
2. She persuaded him (apply) for the transfer.
3. I saw her (lock) the door and walk away.
4. We can't afford (replace) the whole system this year.
5. You'd better (call) before you drive out there.
6. I got my neighbor (feed) the cat while we were gone.
7. The doctor advised (wait) another week.
8. The doctor advised me (wait) another week.
9. He was made (pay) for the damage himself.
10. Do you mind (wait) ten minutes?
11. Why (bother) with the long version?
12. I had the shop (replace) the belt.
13. She helped me (carry) the boxes upstairs.
14. It's too dark (read) in here.
15. He's old enough (know) better.

**B. Fix the sentence.**

16. ✗ *I want that you come to the meeting.*
17. ✗ *He made me to wait in the hall for an hour.*
18. ✗ *Let me to show you how it works.*
19. ✗ *I came here for to learn English.*
20. ✗ *She suggested me to take the later train.*
21. ✗ *Did you went to the closing?*
22. ✗ *You ought call them before five.*
23. ✗ *I don't know why to tell him.*
24. ✗ *It was very kind for you to drive us.*
25. ✗ *This chapter is easy to read it.*

**C. Choose the form, and be ready to say what the other one would have meant.**

26. I saw him (cross / crossing) the street and get into a blue car.
27. He stopped (to smoke / smoking) in 2011, after thirty years.
28. The fire is believed (to start / to have started) in the kitchen.
29. I remembered (to lock / locking) it, which is why the open door surprised me.
30. I'd rather you (don't / didn't) mention it to her.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *let us **go*** | *Let* + object + bare infinitive. |
| 2 | *persuaded him **to apply*** | Verb + object + *to*-infinitive. [§2](#2-verb-object-to-infinitive) |
| 3 | *saw her **lock*** | Perception verb, complete action witnessed. *Locking* would be the act in progress. |
| 4 | *can't afford **to replace*** | *Afford* is infinitive-only. ✗ *afford replacing* |
| 5 | *better **call*** | *Had better* + bare infinitive. ✗ *better to call* |
| 6 | *got my neighbor **to feed*** | *Get* is the causative that keeps *to*. |
| 7 | *advised **waiting*** | No object → *-ing*. [The gerund page](../06-gerund-only/README.md) |
| 8 | *advised me **to wait*** | An object → *to*-infinitive. The same verb, both patterns. |
| 9 | *was made **to pay*** | The passive of *make* restores the *to*. [§12](#12-the-trap-make-takes-to-in-the-passive) |
| 10 | *mind **waiting*** | *Mind* is gerund-only and not on this page at all. |
| 11 | *Why **bother**?* | *Why* + bare infinitive. ✗ *Why to bother?* |
| 12 | *had the shop **replace*** | *Have* + object + bare infinitive. ✗ *had the shop to replace* |
| 13 | *helped me **carry*** | Both work; the bare form is the American default. |
| 14 | *too dark **to read*** | *Too* + adjective + *to*. And no object after it: ✗ *to read it*. |
| 15 | *old enough **to know*** | *Enough* follows the adjective. ✗ *enough old* |
| 16 | *I **want you to come** to the meeting.* | *Want* takes object + infinitive, never a *that*-clause. |
| 17 | *He **made me wait** in the hall.* | *Make* takes the bare infinitive in the active. |
| 18 | ***Let me show** you how it works.* | *Let* takes the bare infinitive. |
| 19 | *I came here **to learn** English.* | ✗ *for to* does not exist; ✗ *for learning* would name a function. |
| 20 | *She **suggested taking** the later train.* / *…**that I take**…* | *Suggest* never takes object + infinitive. |
| 21 | ***Did you go** to the closing?* | *Did* holds the tense; the main verb goes bare. |
| 22 | *You **ought to call** them before five.* | *Ought* keeps its *to*; *should* would not. |
| 23 | *I don't know **why I should tell** him.* | *Why* is the one question word with no infinitive pattern. |
| 24 | *It was very **kind of you** to drive us.* | Judgment adjectives take *of*, not *for*. |
| 25 | *This chapter is **easy to read**.* | The subject is already the object of *read*. |
| 26 | ***cross*** | The whole action, start to finish — he crossed and got in. *Crossing* would catch him mid-street. |
| 27 | ***smoking*** | *Stop* + *-ing* = quit. *Stopped to smoke* would mean he paused in order to have one. |
| 28 | ***to have started*** | The fire started before the believing. The simple infinitive would say it starts there regularly. |
| 29 | ***locking*** | *Remember* + *-ing* = the memory of the act. *Remembered to lock* would mean the task was not forgotten. |
| 30 | ***didn't*** | *Would rather* + a different subject takes a past-tense clause. With the same subject it takes a bare infinitive: *I'd rather not mention it.* |
