Infinitive only#

Every word in English after which a verb has to appear as to do — and, kept strictly apart, every word after which it has to appear as plain do, because persuade him to wait and make him wait are built by two different rules and mixing them is the error.

← All grammar topics · Library home

English has three things it can put in the slot after a word when what goes there is another verb: the -ing form (*enjoy **going), the to*-infinitive** (decide to go), and the bare infinitive (let him go). The gerund page is the first of the three. This page is the other two — and its whole design is that it never lets them touch, because the sentence that gets a Russian speaker caught is almost never ✗ enjoy to read. It is ✗ He made me to wait, which is the two infinitives confused with each other.

So this page has two halves, and they are labeled:

  • Part A — the words that require to + verb: decide to go, persuade him to go, eager to go, a chance to go, too late to go.
  • Part B — the words that require the verb alone: must go, let him go, made him go, saw him go, had better go, Why go?

The organizing idea is two sentences long, one per part.

The to-infinitive points forward. It is the same to as in to Denver, worn down to a grammatical particle, and it has kept the direction. Everything in Part A names something not done yet — an intention (plan to go), a push toward an act (urge him to go), a readiness for one (willing to go), an opportunity for one (a chance to go), a purpose (came here to go). That is why the forward-looking half of English verbs takes to and the backward-looking half takes -ing: I hope to see it against I remember seeing it.

The bare infinitive appears when the word in front has already done the grammatical work. A modal carries tense and mood by itself, so must needs no help: must go. A causative or perception verb has swallowed a whole small clause — him go, her leave — and a subject with its verb does not take to. An auxiliary do has stripped the tense off the main verb and is holding it: Did you go? The bare form is simply what is left over when another word is carrying the load.

How to read the page. Six questions, in the order worth asking them:

  Is the word in front a modal — will, can, must, should…?    →  bare verb.    §10
  Is it let, make, have (causative) or bid?                   →  bare verb.    §11   but GET takes to
  Is it see, hear, watch, notice, feel…?                      →  bare verb.    §13   or -ing, with a difference
  Is it had better, would rather, why (not), all I did was?   →  bare verb.    §15 §16
  Is it do / does / did doing auxiliary work?                 →  bare verb.    §17
  Is it anything else on this page?                           →  to + verb.    §1–§9
  Is it on none of these lists?                               →  ../06-gerund-only/README.md  or  §19

Section §19 is the safety rail, and it matters more here than on the gerund page. Remember, stop, try, regret, go on, mean and need take both an infinitive and a gerund with a real change of meaning, and begin, start, continue, like, love, hate and prefer take both with no change at all. None of them are on either list here. A reader who walks away thinking the infinitive is always safe has been made worse, not better.

The form itself#

Before the lists: the infinitive is not one shape. It is six, and three of them are the reason an advanced sentence goes wrong.

Four mechanical facts go with the table.

1. The negative goes in front of to, not after it. This is the single most reliable spelling error on the page.

I decided not to call. — ✗ I decided to not call is heard, but not to is the standard order He asked me not to wait. — ✗ asked me to not wait · In order not to wake the baby, we used the side door.

2. The perfect infinitive is how English says the second verb happened first. It is not a fancy alternative to the simple one; it moves the time.

He seems to be tired. — now · He seems to have been tired. — then I'm sorry to have kept you waiting. — the keeping is over · She was to have chaired the meeting. — she was scheduled to, and did not

3. The split infinitive is fine in American English. To boldly go, to really understand, to more than double. The rule against it was invented in the nineteenth century by analogy with Latin, where the infinitive is one word and cannot be split. Put the adverb where the meaning wants it, and only move it if the split sounds clumsy — to quickly and without any further discussion adjourn is bad writing, not bad grammar.

4. The to can stand alone when the verb is recoverable. English deletes the verb and keeps the particle; this is called to-ellipsis, and it is everywhere in speech.

"Are you coming?" "I'd like to." — not ✗ I'd like. · I never went, though I meant to. You don't have to if you don't want to. — and after a bare trigger there is nothing to strand: "Will you go?" "I might."

Part A — only a to-infinitive#

Nine lists. The first four are inventories of words — verbs, verbs with an object, adjectives, nouns — and the last five are constructions, which means they are rules and can be applied to words you have never seen.

1. Verbs that take a to-infinitive#

No object in between, and no gerund ever. This is the part of Part A that is not a rule: decide takes to and consider takes -ing, refuse takes to and deny takes -ing, offer takes to and suggest takes -ing, and nothing in the meaning predicts it.

What makes the list learnable is the forward lean described above. Almost every verb here is about an act that has not happened yet at the moment the main verb is true — deciding on it, hoping for it, agreeing to it, failing at it, promising it, refusing it. Compare the gerund list, which is dominated by stopping, avoiding, enjoying and admitting things that already exist as events.

Four notes that decide sentences#

1. Want, wish, would like and expect never take a that-clause. This is the Russian speaker's signature error and it gets its own row in §20. Russian чтобы opens a clause; English opens an object plus an infinitive.

✗ I want that you come tomorrow. → I want you to come tomorrow. ✗ We expect that he signs it today. → We expect him to sign it today. · ✗ She'd like that we stay. → She'd like us to stay.

Wish does take a clause, but only in the unreal sense, with a past-tense verb — I wish you were here — which is a different construction entirely and belongs to conditionals.

2. Seem, appear, happen, prove, tend and turn out do not describe what the subject does. They are raising verbs: the subject belongs to the infinitive, not to them. She seems to like it does not mean she is doing any seeming; it means It seems that she likes it. The practical consequence is that these verbs accept weather it and existential there, which real action verbs do not.

It seems to be raining. · There appear to be two copies. He happened to be standing right there. = It happened that he was standing there. · The story turned out to be true.

3. Ask splits by who does the act. Ask to do = ask permission for yourself. Ask somebody to do = make a request of them. Both are correct and they are not synonyms.

He asked to leave early. — he wanted permission · He asked me to leave early. — he wanted me gone The same split runs through beg, expect and want — the ◆ verbs above, all covered in §2.

4. Mean is on this list only in one of its two senses. Mean to do = intend. Mean doing = involve, entail — and that one is on the gerund page. See §19.

I meant to tell you. — I intended to · A rewrite means starting over. — it entails starting over

2. Verb, object, to-infinitive#

The frame is verb + somebody + to + verb, and what it means, nearly always, is that the subject is doing something to get the object to act: advising them, allowing them, forcing them, reminding them, paying them. The person named is the one who performs the second verb.

The doctor advised him to rest. — he rests They forced us to sign. — we sign I reminded her to bring the receipt. — she brings it

The ◆ verbs: the one place this page and the gerund page touch#

Five of these verbs have two patterns, and the object is the switch. Put a person in the slot and you get an infinitive; leave the slot empty and you get -ing. This is not a choice and not a register difference — it is one verb with two grammars.

VerbNo object → -ingWith an object → to-infinitive
adviseI'd advise waiting a week.I'd advise you to wait a week.
allowWe don't allow smoking in here.They allowed us to smoke on the deck.
permitWe don't permit parking in the fire lane.The guard permitted us to park out back.

forbidThe lease forbids subletting.She forbade him to sublet. · …from subletting.

The full -ing halves of those rows live on the gerund page, which carries the same table from the other side. Between the two pages the verb is covered; on either page alone it is half covered, which is why both pages point here.

And the flat prohibition. Suggest and recommend do not take object + infinitive, however natural it sounds and however many of the verbs beside them do. There are exactly two correct constructions and neither uses to.

✗ He suggested me to leave early. · ✓ He suggested leaving early. · ✓ He suggested that I leave early. ✗ They recommended us to try the pier. · ✓ They recommended trying the pier. · ✓ They recommended that we try the pier.

The that-clause takes the base form — that I leave, not ✗ that I leaves — because it is a subjunctive. The mechanics are in reported speech, which is also where tell somebody to do, ask somebody to do, order somebody to do and warn somebody not to do are handled as reporting patterns rather than as infinitive patterns.

Three more things the object slot does#

1. The negative goes in front of to. Always, and this is where learners put it in the wrong place most often.

She told me not to wait. — ✗ told me to not wait, ✗ didn't tell me to wait (a different meaning) They warned us not to swim out there. He asked me not to mention it.

2. Get takes to and make, have and let do not. This is the fault line between Part A and Part B, and it runs through four verbs that mean nearly the same thing. It is laid out in full in §11.

I got him to answer the phone. — to · I made him answer the phone. — no to I had him answer the phone. — no to · I let him answer the phone. — no to

3. The passive puts the object in front and keeps the to. Most of the verbs in this section passivize cleanly, and the result is one of the commonest shapes in written English.

We were instructed to wait in the hall. · Passengers are required to show ID at the gate. · He was ordered to pay restitution.

Make is the one verb where the passive adds a to that the active did not have, and that is §12, the most missed item on this page.

3. Adjectives that take a to-infinitive#

An adjective with a preposition after it takes -ing — good at cooking, afraid of flying — and that is the gerund page. An adjective with nothing after it takes an infinitive. The presence or absence of a preposition decides it, and there is no third possibility.

She's good at fixing things. — preposition → -ing She's ready to fix it. — no preposition → to

Afraid, sorry and the pairs that split#

Four adjectives take both a preposition + -ing and a bare infinitive, and the two mean different things. This is the boundary between this page and the gerund page drawn inside a single word.

Easy, hard, difficult, impossible: the pattern with the missing object#

These four behave in a way no other adjectives do. The book is easy to read has a subject, the book, that is really the object of read — and English will not let you say it twice.

✓ This book is easy to read. ✗ This book is easy to read it. — the object is already the subject ✓ It's easy to read this book. — the other arrangement, with it holding the subject slot

The same pattern runs through hard, difficult, impossible, tough, simple, fun, a pleasure, nice and dangerous:

The instructions are impossible to follow. — ✗ impossible to follow them He's hard to get hold of. · That chair is dangerous to sit in. She's a pleasure to work with. — the preposition stays stranded at the end, and that is correct

Keen, and the prediction adjectives#

American English says keen to do; British English says keen on doing. Both are grammatical everywhere and each marks the writer. Americans do use keen on — but for liking a thing, not for wanting to act: I'm not keen on the new logo.

And sure, certain, bound, likely, unlikely and liable take an infinitive when the prediction is the speaker's, and a clause when the confidence is the subject's. The two sentences look identical and are not.

American: We're keen to hear what they propose. · British: We're keen on hearing what they propose. She's sure to win. — I am predicting it · She's sure that she'll win. — she is the confident one

The nouns that take both, and mean different things#

Five of these also appear on the gerund page with a preposition and -ing, and the two versions are not synonyms: the chance of winning is the probability and a chance to win is the opportunity; his reason for leaving is the motive he had and a reason to leave is a justification that exists. For and of: the split that gets missed#

Both frames put a person into It's + adjective + to do, and the preposition is chosen by what the adjective is describing.

  • for — the adjective describes the situation: important, easy, hard, necessary, possible, unusual, dangerous, cheaper, better.
  • of — the adjective describes the person: kind, nice, generous, rude, stupid, careless, brave, clever, silly, thoughtful, wrong.

It's important for you to be on time. — being on time is important · It was kind of you to be on time. — you were kind ✗ It was kind for you to drive us. → It was kind of you to drive us. · ✗ It's difficult of me to say. → It's difficult for me to say.

The of frame has a second form that says the same thing with the person as subject: You were kind to drive us. The for frame does not: ✗ You are important to be on time means something else entirely.

Too: the negative that is built in#

Too does not mean very. It means more than is workable, so the sentence is already negative and never needs not.

He's too tired to drive. = he is not going to drive · It's too late to call them. ✗ He's too tired to not drive. — nothing to fix; the negative is in too ✗ He's very tired to drive. — very is not too, and this is not a sentence

And, exactly as with easy to read in §3, the object is not repeated:

The box is too heavy to lift. — ✗ too heavy to lift it The coffee's too hot to drink. — ✗ too hot to drink it But when a subject is named, the object still stays out: The box is too heavy for me to lift. — ✗ for me to lift it

Enough: the word that moves#

Enough goes after an adjective or adverb and before a noun. Getting it backwards is the commonest word-order error in this section.

She's old enough to vote. — ✗ enough old · He didn't run fast enough to qualify. — ✗ enough fast We have enough money to cover it. — ✗ money enough, except as an archaism · There aren't enough chairs for everyone to sit down.

There's nothing to do#

Nothing, something, anything, a lot, plenty, much and little all take an infinitive directly, and the infinitive is again a reduced relative clause: nothing to do = nothing that you can do.

There's nothing to eat in the house. · I've got something to tell you. Is there anything to drink? · There's a lot to think about. · There's plenty to do. ✗ There's nothing for doing. → There's nothing to do.

6. Purpose#

English answers why? with an infinitive. This is the single most frequent use of to + verb in the language, and it is also where Russian чтобы does the most damage.

TriggerPatternExample
toto doI came here to study engineering.
in order toin order to do — explicit, slightly formalWe left early in order to beat the traffic.
so as toso as to do — formal, mostly writtenShe spoke quietly so as to avoid waking him.
in order not toin order not to doHe took the stairs in order not to run into her.
so as not toso as not to doI closed it slowly so as not to make a sound.
so thatso that + clause — when the subject changesI called so that she would know.

Plain to is the default. In order to and so as to are available when you want the purpose marked explicitly, and they are the only option in the negative: ✗ I took the stairs not to run into her is not English, and ✗ I took the stairs to not run into her is a spoken hedge at best.

I stopped to ask directions. — purpose · I stopped asking directions. — a different sentence: I quit doing it We moved the meeting in order to give everyone time to read it. · She turned the radio down so as not to wake the baby.

The two wrong ways, and they are both common#

✗ for to is the literal translation of для того чтобы and does not exist in modern English. ✗ for doing exists, but it names the function of a thing, not the purpose of an action.

✗ I came here for to study English. → I came here to study English. ✗ I came here for studying English. → I came here to study English. ✗ He stopped for asking directions. → He stopped to ask directions. · ✗ She called for to confirm. → She called to confirm.

What for + -ing really marks is the function of a thing, where the infinitive marks the purpose of an action — and the subject usually gives it away, a thing for the first and a person for the second.

A corkscrew is for opening bottles. · This app is for tracking expenses. — what the thing does I went out to buy a corkscrew. · I downloaded it to track my expenses. — why the person acted

For + a noun is perfectly fine. The error is for + a verb, not for itself.

I came here for the conference. ✓ She went out for milk. ✓ He's here for an interview. ✓

For somebody to do: purpose with a different actor#

When the second verb has a different subject, English keeps for and adds the person before the infinitive. This is the pattern that looks like the error and is not.

I bought a book for you to read on the plane. · There's no need for anyone to stay late. He held the door open for us to get the couch through. · It would be better for her to hear it from you.

And when the sentence needs a modal — can, could, would — the infinitive cannot carry it, so so that takes over:

I wrote it down so that I wouldn't forget. — ✗ so that not to forget Leave the light on so that they can find the door.

7. Question word plus to-infinitive#

A question word takes an infinitive after verbs of knowing, deciding, asking and explaining. The whole phrase is a compressed indirect question: I don't know what to say = I don't know what I should say.

TriggerPatternExample
how tohow to doNobody showed me how to reset it.
what towhat to doI have no idea what to tell him.
where towhere to doWe haven't decided where to eat.
when towhen to doShe knows when to stop talking.
whether towhether to doThey're debating whether to appeal.
who to · whom towho to doI didn't know who to call.
which + noun towhich one to doHe couldn't decide which model to buy.
how much / many tohow much to doTell me how much to pour.

The verbs in front of this pattern are a small, closed set: know, learn, decide, wonder, ask, tell, show, teach, explain, forget, remember, discuss, consider, figure out, work out, have no idea, be unsure.

Can you show me how to fold it? · We still have to figure out where to park. · She's teaching him how to swim.

✗ Why to does not exist#

Every other question word takes an infinitive. Why does not — and the reason is §16: why already has a construction with the bare infinitive, and the two cannot both be live.

✗ I don't know why to go. → I don't know why I should go. ✗ He couldn't explain why to wait. → …why we had to wait. But: Why wait? ✓ — a direct question, bare infinitive, §16

Whether, not if#

This pattern takes whether. If is fine in an indirect question with a full clause and impossible in front of an infinitive.

✓ I don't know whether to call him. · ✗ I don't know if to call him. ✓ I don't know if I should call him. — a clause, so if is back in play Whether or not to appeal is the emphatic form, and or not can also go at the end.

8. be to and the semi-modals#

Two families, joined by the fact that both start with a word that looks like an ordinary verb and end with a to that cannot be dropped. Between them they cover most of what English says about obligation, schedule and likelihood — which is exactly what the modals in Part B say without a to. That is the whole reason this section and §10 have to be read against each other.

The be family#

TriggerPatternExample
be tobe to do — arrangement, instruction, formalThe president is to visit Ottawa in June.
be about tobe about to do — immediate futureI was about to call you.
be due tobe due to do — scheduledThe flight is due to land at 9:15.
be supposed tobe supposed to do — expected of youYou were supposed to call first.
be likely tobe likely to doRates are likely to hold steady.
be bound tobe bound to do — it will certainly happenSomebody was bound to complain.
be apt tobe apt to do — has a habit ofThat door is apt to stick in humid weather.
be set tobe set to do — journalisticThe company is set to announce layoffs.

Four notes:

Be to is the formal arrangement form, and in American English it is mostly news writing and official instructions. In the past it also carries a specific meaning — the plan and its failure.

All visitors are to sign in at the desk. — an instruction · The two sides are to meet again Thursday. — a scheduled arrangement She was to have chaired the panel. — she was scheduled to, and did not · If we are to finish by Friday, we need two more people. — the conditional use

Be supposed to has two meanings and they are not related. One is obligation; the other is reputation.

You're supposed to be here at eight. — that is the rule The new place on Fifth is supposed to be good. — that is what people say It wasn't supposed to rain today. — the forecast said otherwise

Be due to is scheduled to, and it has an identical twin that means because of. The twin takes a noun and is not an infinitive pattern at all.

The train is due to arrive at 6:40. — scheduled The delay was due to ice on the wires. — because of; ✗ due to arrive means nothing here

Be about to is the tightest future English has — We're about to sit down to dinner — and its negative is idiomatic: I'm not about to pay for their mistake does not mean "not immediately," it means "absolutely not."

The semi-modals that keep to#

These behave like modals in meaning and like ordinary verbs in grammar. They inflect, they need do for questions and negatives, and they keep the to — which is precisely what the real modals in §10 do not do.

✗ I must to go. → I must go. / I have to go. — must is a modal, have to is not · ✗ You ought go. → You ought to go. ✗ He needs go now. → He needs to go now. — the lexical need; the modal need is in §10 ✗ She used work here. → She used to work here.

The two used tos are the one trap this family shares with the gerund page. Used to do is a finished past habit; be used to doing is being accustomed, and its to is a preposition, which is why -ing follows it. The full treatment is on the gerund page.

I used to work nights. — and I don't now I'm used to working nights. — it stopped bothering me ✗ I'm used to work nights. — a real sentence that means neither one

9. Passive reporting frames#

English has two ways to attribute a claim without naming who made it, and the second one is an infinitive pattern. It is the dominant shape in news writing, academic prose and obituaries.

It is said that he left the country in March. — the clause version He is said to have left the country in March. — the infinitive version

The subject of the reported clause is hauled up to the front of the sentence and the rest becomes an infinitive. The meaning is identical; the second is shorter and more formal.

TriggerPatternExample
be said tosb is said to doThe house is said to be haunted.
be believed tosb is believed to doThe fire is believed to have started in the kitchen.
be thought tosb is thought to doShe is thought to be the oldest resident.
be known tosb is known to doThe drug is known to cause drowsiness.
be expected tosb is expected to doRates are expected to rise again.
be reported tosb is reported to doTwo hikers are reported to be missing.
be considered tosb is considered to doHe is considered to be the front-runner.
be alleged tosb is alleged to doThe firm is alleged to have falsified the tests.
be supposed tosth is supposed to be — reputationThe chowder is supposed to be excellent.

The perfect infinitive is what shifts the time back. This is the only moving part in the construction, and it is the one learners drop.

It is said that he is a millionaire. → He is said to be a millionaire. — same time It is said that he was a millionaire. → He is said to have been a millionaire. — earlier It is believed that the fire started in the kitchen. → The fire is believed to have started in the kitchen. ✗ The fire is believed to start in the kitchen. — that says it starts there regularly

And there is said to be. When the reported clause begins with there, there is what gets raised, and the infinitive keeps the be.

It is said that there are three copies. → There are said to be three copies. There is believed to be no connection between the two cases. There are thought to have been as many as forty people inside.

More on the whole attribution system, including which verbs allow which patterns, is in reported speech.

Part B — only a bare infinitive, no to#

Nine lists again, and they are shorter, because the bare infinitive is a small, closed system. Nothing here is a matter of vocabulary: if you know the nine triggers, you know the whole pattern, and every one of them is a place where a Russian speaker's hand reaches for a to that must not be written.

10. Modal auxiliaries#

The nine core modals take the bare infinitive, with no exceptions and no variation.

Five properties come as a package, and a word that has all five is a modal:

  1. bare infinitive after it        he must GO          ✗ he must to go

Ought to, have to and used to mean what modals mean and are built differently: the to is part of them. They are handled in §8.

Need and dare are genuinely double. Each has a modal life (bare infinitive, no -s, no do) that survives almost only in the negative and the question, and an ordinary-verb life (with to) that is the normal American choice.

The whole modal system — meanings, past forms, degrees of certainty, and the substitutes for the forms modals do not have — is in the modal verb dictionary.

12. The trap: make takes to in the passive#

This is the single most missed item on the page, and it is a one-line fact.

Active
They made him pay for the damage. — bare
Passive
He was made to pay for the damage. — to

Nothing else in the sentence changed. The bare infinitive after make survives only while make has an object of its own; in the passive that object has become the subject, the verb has nothing to hold the clause together with, and English puts the to back.

✗ He was made pay. → He was made to pay. ✗ We were made work overtime. → We were made to work overtime.

And let has no passive at all. You cannot flip They let him go — ✗ He was let to go and ✗ He was let go to see her are both broken. English substitutes a different verb entirely:

They let him go early. → He was allowed to go early. — ✗ was let to go They let us take photos. → We were permitted to take photos.

(He was let go does exist — but it is an idiom meaning he was fired, and it contains no infinitive at all.)

The perception verbs do the same thing, for the same reason. §13 takes a bare infinitive in the active and a to-infinitive in the passive:

Two witnesses saw him leave. → He was seen to leave around ten. Somebody heard her scream. → She was heard to scream twice. The -ing version passivizes with no to: He was seen leaving around ten. ✓

14. help, which takes both#

Help is the one verb in English where the bare infinitive and the to-infinitive are freely interchangeable after an object.

She helped me carry it in. · She helped me to carry it in. — both standard Would you help me move this? · Would you help me to move this?

Americans drop the to. The bare form is the overwhelming default in American speech and writing; the to form is commoner in British English and in formal American prose. Neither is an error, and in an American sentence the bare form is what you want.

Help also works with no object at all, and there the bare form is close to obligatory:

That doesn't help explain it. · A second coat would help protect the finish. · The map helped narrow it down.

And can't help is a different word. Can't help + -ing means "be unable to stop," and it belongs to the gerund page. Its one variant, can't help but, takes a bare infinitive.

I can't help laughing when he does the voice. ✓ I can't help but laugh when he does the voice. ✓ ✗ I can't help but laughing. · ✗ I can't help to laugh.

A set of fixed expressions, unrelated to each other except that every one of them is followed by a bare infinitive.

Four notes:

Had better is the one that looks past-tense and is not. It is advice about the present or future, it contracts to 'd better, and its negative is had better not — never ✗ hadn't better.

You'd better not be late again. · We'd better get going. — ✗ We'd better to get going In speech Americans drop had entirely: You better call her. That is spoken English, not writing.

Would rather takes a bare infinitive when the subject stays the same and a past-tense clause when it changes. The two shapes are not interchangeable.

He decided to walk rather than take the bus. — bare, to match to walk Rather than waiting, we walked home. · Rather than go home, he stayed. — at the front, either works

All I did was … and What he did was … are pseudo-cleft sentences, and the verb after was is bare. The to version exists and sounds formal: What he did was to call the police. In speech, leave it out.

All she did was smile and hand me the form. · All I want to do is sleep. · What you need to do is call them back.

16. why and why not#

Why is the only question word that takes an infinitive, and the infinitive is bare. The construction makes a rhetorical question out of it: Why wait? does not ask for a reason, it argues against waiting.

TriggerPatternExample
whyWhy do …?Why pay more?
why notWhy not do …? — a suggestionWhy not ask her yourself?

Why wait? The sale ends Friday. · Why bother? Nobody reads it. Why not try the other entrance? · Why not just call them?

Both are complete sentences with no subject and no auxiliary, and neither takes to: ✗ Why to wait?, ✗ Why not to ask her? This is the same fact as the ✗ why to prohibition in §7 seen from the other side — why has already spent its infinitive on this construction.

17. do-support and emphatic do#

Do, does and did as auxiliaries take the tense off the main verb and carry it themselves. The main verb is left bare — and that is the whole explanation for the most frequent beginner error in English.

TriggerPatternExample
do / does / did — questionDid you do …?Did you go to the meeting?
do / does / did — negativedon't doShe doesn't drive.
do — emphasisI do do …I do like it — I just wouldn't buy it.
do — imperative emphasisDo do …Do sit down.

✗ Did you went to the meeting? → Did you go to the meeting? · ✗ He doesn't likes coffee. → He doesn't like coffee. ✗ She didn't came home. → She didn't come home. · ✗ Do you can swim? → Can you swim? — modals do their own inverting

The tense is in the auxiliary, so putting it on the main verb as well writes it twice. Once you see did at the front of the sentence, the verb after it is bare, permanently. The full machinery — question formation, negation, and what happens in the passive — is in the passive, questions and clauses and across the tenses.

Emphatic do is the same auxiliary used in a positive sentence, where nothing requires it, purely to contradict:

I do want to go — I just can't afford it. · She did call. Twice. · Do let me know if anything changes.

18. The American come see, go get#

American English puts come and go directly in front of another bare verb, with nothing in between.

TriggerPatternExample
comecome doCome see us when you're in town.
gogo doGo get your coat.
let's golet's go doLet's go eat.
come oncome on doCome on help me with this.

Come sit with us. · I'll go check the mailbox. Let's go grab a coffee. · Come look at what the dog did.

British English uses and: come and see us, go and get your coat. Americans use and too, and the bare version is the more casual of the two. Neither is wrong in American writing; the bare one is more spoken.

There is a real restriction, and it is the part learners miss. The pattern works only when come and go are in their base form — after to, after a modal, in the imperative, and in the plain present with I/you/we/they. The moment the verb inflects, the pattern dies.

✓ Come see us. · ✓ I'll go get it. · ✓ They come visit every summer. ✗ He came see us. → He came to see us. · He came and saw us. ✗ She goes get it. → She goes and gets it. · She goes to get it. ✗ We've gone see the house. → We've gone to see the house.

And go + -ing is a completely different construction for leisure activities — go swimming, go shopping — which is on the gerund page. Go swim and go swimming are both English and they are not the same sentence.

19. What is not on either list#

Everything above is a place where the infinitive — one kind or the other — is the only option. Two groups belong to neither page, and between them they hold some of the commonest verbs in English.

20. For Russian speakers#

Russian does this work with a conjunction and a clause; English does it with an infinitive and no conjunction. The repair reflex#

Four steps, and they take about two seconds once they are a habit. Run them every time the second verb in a sentence is about to be written.

  1.  Find the word IMMEDIATELY BEFORE the slot. That word decides, not you.

  2.  Is it will/would/can/could/may/might/must/shall/should,
      or let/make/have/bid, or see/hear/watch/notice/feel,
      or had better/would rather/why/why not, or do/does/did?
                                                    →  BARE.   he must GO · let him GO

  3.  Is it get, ought, have, need, used, or dare?  →  TO.     got him TO GO · ought TO GO
      (these four look like step 2 and are not)

  4.  Is there a PREPOSITION in front of the slot?  →  -ing, and this is the wrong page.
      good at COOKING · without SAYING · look forward to SEEING

  And once more, because it is the one nobody remembers:
      They made him pay.   →   He was made TO pay.

One more habit, and it is the same discipline as learning a Russian verb's case government: whenever you learn a new verb, learn what comes after it. Decide + to, enjoy + -ing, make + bare, suggest + that. It is the only thing that makes this page unnecessary.

Common mistakes#

WrongRightWhy
✗ I want that you come.I want you to come.Want takes object + infinitive, never a clause.
✗ He made me to wait an hour.He made me wait an hour.Make takes the bare infinitive.
✗ He was made pay for it.He was made to pay for it.The passive of make puts the to back. §12
✗ Let me to explain.Let me explain.Let takes the bare infinitive.
✗ He was let to go early.He was allowed to go early.Let has no passive; allow replaces it.
✗ I got him sign the form.I got him to sign the form.Get is the causative that keeps to.
✗ I'll have him to call you.I'll have him call you.Have is the causative that drops it.
✗ I saw him to cross the street.I saw him cross the street.Perception verbs take the bare form.
✗ I must to leave now.I must leave now.Modals take the bare infinitive.
✗ You ought go home.You ought to go home.Ought is the semi-modal that keeps to.
✗ Did you went to the meeting?Did you go to the meeting?The auxiliary holds the tense; the verb goes bare.
✗ You'd better to call first.You'd better call first.Had better takes the bare infinitive.
✗ I'd rather to stay home.I'd rather stay home.Same family.
✗ I came here for to study.I came here to study.✗ for to does not exist in English.
✗ I came here for studying.I came here to study.For + -ing names a thing's function, not a purpose.
✗ He suggested me to leave.He suggested leaving. / …that I leave.Suggest never takes object + infinitive.
✗ He accepted to help us.He agreed to help us.Accept takes a noun, not an infinitive.
✗ We decided waiting a week.We decided to wait a week.Decide is infinitive-only.
✗ I'm ready for go.I'm ready to go.Adjective with no preposition → infinitive.
✗ It was kind for you to drive.It was kind of you to drive.Judgment adjectives take of.
✗ She's enough old to vote.She's old enough to vote.Enough follows an adjective and precedes a noun.
✗ The box is too heavy to lift it.The box is too heavy to lift.The object is already the subject.
✗ I don't know why to go.I don't know why I should go.Every question word but why takes an infinitive.
✗ I decided to not call.I decided not to call.The negative goes in front of to.
✗ He came see us last week.He came to see us. / He came and saw us.Come see works only in the base form.
✗ The fire is believed to start in the kitchen.…to have started in the kitchen.The perfect infinitive moves the time back.
✗ I'm used to work nights.I'm used to working nights. / I used to work nights.Two different words, two different meanings.
