Contractions#

Two words pushed into one, with an apostrophe marking what fell out — and a set of rules about which ones you may write down.

1. What a contraction is#

A contraction joins two words and drops one or more sounds, marking the gap with an apostrophe: I am → I'm, will not → won't, of the clock → o'clock. English has about ninety of them in ordinary use.

They are not a word class. I'm is a pronoun plus a verb, won't is a modal plus an adverb, let's is a verb plus a pronoun — which is why they live here, under grammar, rather than in any one dictionary of parts of speech.

Two things make them worth a chapter. First, the apostrophe marks a missing sound, never possession — and the four commonest spelling errors in English come from confusing the two. Second, contractions are not optional decoration: in speech they are the default, and a sentence full of uncontracted forms sounds stiff or angry rather than polite. I do not think that is right is not more correct than I don't think that's right; it is differently pitched.

3. The two ambiguous forms#

Written English has two contractions that each stand for more than one word. Only what follows tells you which.

WrittenCan beTest
'sis / has / usHe's tired = is · He's gone = has · Let's = us
'dwould / hadI'd go (bare verb) = would · I'd gone (participle) = had

Two fixed phrases settle themselves: I'd better go is always had, and I'd rather go is always would.

4. The apostrophe rule#

If you can expand it into two words, it takes an apostrophe. If it is a possessive pronoun, it never does.

ContractionPossessiveTest
it's = it is / it hasitsIt's raining → It is raining ✓
you're = you areyourYou're late → You are late ✓
they're = they aretheir (and there = place)They're here → They are here ✓
who's = who is / who haswhoseWho's calling? → Who is calling? ✓

Possessive pronouns — its, yours, hers, ours, theirs, whose — never take an apostrophe. Only nouns do: the dog's bowl.

5. Forms English does not have#

  • ✗ amn't. English has no contraction for am not. In a statement, use I'm not. In a question, the form is aren't I? — historically odd, completely standard today.
  • ✗ mayn't, ✗ shalln't. Not American English. Shan't exists but has all but vanished here; Americans say won't.
  • ✗ I'mn't, ✗ willn't. English contracts once per pair, and will not is irregular: won't.
  • Double contractions — shouldn't've, I'd've — are said constantly and written almost never, outside dialogue.

6. When not to contract#

  1. At the end of a clause. A contraction cannot carry the stress a final word needs: Yes, I am, never ✗ Yes, I'm. Likewise I know he is, ✗ I know he's.
  2. Under emphasis. I did not say that denies more firmly than I didn't say that.
  3. In formal writing — academic papers, contracts, official letters. Though modern American style guides accept contractions in journalism, business writing and most nonfiction.
  4. Where the reader may misread the ambiguity — if he'd could be had or would and the sentence does not settle it, write the full form.

7. isn't or 's not?#

Where the verb is be or have, English gives you two contractions and lets you choose which word to shorten:

He isn't coming. / He's not coming. They haven't finished. / They've not finished.

Both are standard. The -n't form is the American default; the 's not form throws slightly more weight onto the negative. After I, only one is available: I'm not, never ✗ I amn't.

8. Questions#

The negative contraction inverts as a single word, which is what makes it useful:

Isn't he coming? · Don't you want it? · Won't you sit down?

The uncontracted version separates the two parts and sounds formal or indignant: Is he not coming? Tag questions run on the same machinery: You're coming, aren't you?, He didn't call, did he?

10. What Russian speakers get wrong most#

  1. ✗ could of. Could've sounds exactly like could of, but of is never a verb. Also ✗ would of, ✗ should of, ✗ must of.
  2. ✗ It's color is red. It's = it is. The possessive is its, with no apostrophe.
  3. Missing the future. 'll is nearly inaudible, so I'll call you is heard as I call you. Russian has no auxiliary here, which makes the gap easy to miss.
  4. Over-contracting at the end. ✗ Yes, I'm. → Yes, I am.
  5. Under-contracting everywhere else. Full forms in casual speech sound cold. I am not sure that is a good idea reads as displeasure; I'm not sure that's a good idea is the neutral sentence.
  6. ✗ He no is coming or ✗ He not coming — the negative needs an auxiliary to attach to: He isn't coming.
