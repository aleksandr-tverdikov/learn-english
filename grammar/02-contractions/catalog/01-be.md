# Contractions of *be*

> *I'm*, *you're*, *it's*, *that's* — the commonest contractions in the language, and the ones that hide an apostrophe trap.

[← Contractions](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Every one of these joins a subject to a form of *be*. Two of them are the most-corrected spellings in English: ***it's*** is *it is* or *it has* and never a possessive (*its* is the possessive), and ***you're*** is *you are*, not *your*. The last, *let's*, is the odd one — the *'s* there is *us*, not *is*.

---
### I'm

**Pronunciation:** /aɪm/ &middot; *YM*
**Русский:** я (сокращение от I am)
**Expands to:** *I am*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *I* + *am*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** A contraction can never end a clause. *Are you ready?* — *Yes, I am*, never ✗ *Yes, I'm*. The same rule blocks ✗ *I don't know where he's* and ✗ *Tell me what it's*: with nothing after it, the verb has to be written out in full.

The contraction of *I am*.

1. *I'm* from Chicago, but I live in Austin now.
2. *I'm* not sure that's the right address.
3. Sorry, *I'm* going to be about ten minutes late.

---

### you're

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr/ &middot; *YOOR*
**Русский:** ты, вы (сокращение от you are)
**Expands to:** *you are*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *you* + *are*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *you're* vs *your*. If you can expand it to two words, it takes an apostrophe: *You're late* works as *You are late*, so it is *you're*; *your flight* cannot become *you are flight*, so it is *your* (твой, ваш). One sentence holds both: *You're going to miss your flight.* And *yours* never takes an apostrophe at all.

The contraction of *you are*.

1. *You're* the only one who noticed.
2. *You're* going to need a bigger box.
3. I think *you're* right about that.

---

### he's

**Pronunciation:** /hiːz/ &middot; *HEEZ*
**Русский:** он есть, он уже (сокращение от he is или he has)
**Expands to:** *he is* or *he has*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *he* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*, and the word after it decides. Before a noun, an adjective or an *-ing* form it is *is*: *He's tired*, *He's working*. Before a past participle it is *has*: *He's gone*, *He's finished*. Plain *has* meaning possession does not contract — *He has a car*, never ✗ *He's a car*, which would be read as *he is a car*. Possession does contract in *has got*: *He's got a car*.

he's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *he is*.** (он (есть))

1. *He's* tired — he worked a double shift.
2. *He's* a pediatrician at the children's hospital.
3. *He's* waiting for you downstairs.

**2. The contraction of *he has*, before a past participle.** (он уже (+ причастие))

4. *He's* already left for the airport.
5. *He's* been sick all week.
6. *He's* got a new car.

### she's

**Pronunciation:** /ʃiːz/ &middot; *SHEEZ*
**Русский:** она есть, она уже (сокращение от she is или she has)
**Expands to:** *she is* or *she has*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *she* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. *She's ready* and *She's leaving* are *is*; *She's left* and *She's been here* are *has*. The test is the word after it: a past participle means *has*, anything else means *is*.

she's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *she is*.** (она (есть))

1. *She's* my sister's roommate.
2. *She's* really good at math.
3. *She's* running late again.

**2. The contraction of *she has*, before a past participle.** (она уже (+ причастие))

4. *She's* gone back to school.
5. *She's* just finished her shift.
6. *She's* had a rough year.

### it's

**Pronunciation:** /ɪts/ &middot; *ITS*
**Русский:** это есть, оно уже (сокращение от it is или it has)
**Expands to:** *it is* or *it has*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *it* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*: *It's cold* is *is*, *It's been cold* is *has*. It is never a possessive.
**Contrast:** *it's* vs *its* — the most-corrected pair in English. If you can expand it to two words, it takes an apostrophe: *It's cold* works as *It is cold*, so *it's*; *the dog wagged its tail* cannot become *it is tail*, so *its* (его, её). Possessives made from pronouns never take an apostrophe — *its*, *his*, *hers*, *ours*, *yours*, *theirs*. And there is no word *its'*.

it's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *it is*.** (это, оно (есть))

1. *It's* colder than the forecast said.
2. *It's* a twenty-minute walk from here.
3. *It's* raining again.

**2. The contraction of *it has*, before a past participle.** (оно уже (+ причастие))

4. *It's* been three years since we moved.
5. *It's* stopped raining, so we can walk.
6. *It's* gotten a lot worse since Monday.

---

### we're

**Pronunciation:** /wɪr/ &middot; *WEER*
**Русский:** мы (сокращение от we are)
**Expands to:** *we are*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *we* + *are*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *we're* vs *were*. *We're* is *we are*, the present; *were* with no apostrophe is the past of *be*: *We were ready then, and we're ready now.* Many Americans reduce *we're* until it sounds just like *were*, so in writing the apostrophe is what carries the tense.

The contraction of *we are*.

1. *We're* almost out of coffee.
2. *We're* leaving at six sharp.
3. *We're* the ones who called you.

---

### they're

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛr/ &middot; *THAIR*
**Русский:** они (сокращение от they are)
**Expands to:** *they are*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *they* + *are*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *they're* vs *their* vs *there* — all three sound the same in American English. If you can expand it to two words, it takes an apostrophe: *They're late* works as *They are late*. *Their* is the possessive (их): *their house*. *There* is the place word and the empty subject of *there is*: *over there*, *There are two left*. One sentence with all three: *They're over there with their kids.*

The contraction of *they are*.

1. *They're* the ones who organized the whole thing.
2. *They're* not answering their phones.
3. *They're* over there with their kids.

### that's

**Pronunciation:** /ðæts/ &middot; *THATS*
**Русский:** это (сокращение от that is или that has)
**Expands to:** *that is* or *that has*
**Type:** be — the pronoun *that* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing; one of the commonest words in spoken American English
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. *That's a problem* is *is*; *That's been a problem* is *has*. As usual, a past participle after it means *has*.

that's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *that is*.** (это (есть))

1. *That's* the last train until morning.
2. *That's* not what I meant.
3. *That's* why I called you.

**2. The contraction of *that has*, before a past participle.** (это уже (+ причастие))

4. *That's* been true for years.
5. *That's* always struck me as odd.
6. *That's* turned out to be the hard part.

### there's

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrz/ &middot; *THAIRZ*
**Русский:** есть, имеется (сокращение от there is или there has)
**Expands to:** *there is* or *there has*
**Type:** be — the empty subject *there* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in writing, with one catch: *there's* in front of a plural (*There's two seats left*, *There's a lot of people here*) is everyday spoken American English but is marked wrong in school and in edited prose, which want *There are two seats left*
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. Before a noun it is *is*: *There's a problem*. Before a past participle it is *has*: *There's been a problem*.
**Contrast:** *there's* vs *theirs*. *Theirs* is the possessive pronoun (*The blue car is theirs*) and never takes an apostrophe. Note also that the *there* of *there's* is not the place word: it is the empty subject that lets a sentence introduce something new.

there's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *there is*.** (есть, имеется)

1. *There's* someone at the door.
2. *There's* no easy answer to that.
3. *There's* a gas station two blocks up.

**2. The contraction of *there has*, before a past participle.** (уже было, произошло)

4. *There's* been an accident on the highway.
5. *There's* never been a better time to buy.
6. *There's* been talk of closing the plant.

---

### here's

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrz/ &middot; *HEERZ*
**Русский:** вот (сокращение от here is)
**Expands to:** *here is*
**Type:** be — the adverb *here* + *is*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** Unlike *there's*, *that's* and *he's*, this *'s* is only ever *is*. *There has been a delay* contracts to *There's been a delay*, but *here has been* is not English, so *here's* has a single expansion. In speech it is also used in front of a plural — *Here's your keys* — which writing corrects to *Here are your keys*.

The contraction of *here is*, used to hand something over or to announce what comes next.

1. *Here's* your receipt — have a good one.
2. *Here's* what I think we should do.
3. *Here's* the thing: nobody told the client.

### what's

**Pronunciation:** /wʌts/ &middot; *WUTS*
**Русский:** что есть, что случилось (сокращение от what is или what has)
**Expands to:** *what is* or *what has*
**Type:** be — the question word *what* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*: *What's the problem?* is *is*, *What's happened?* is *has*. In casual speech it can also stand for *does* — *What's it cost?*, *What's he do for a living?* — but that one belongs to speech only and is not written outside dialogue.

what's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *what is*.** (что (есть), какой)

1. *What's* your last name again?
2. *What's* the matter?
3. *What's* on TV tonight?

**2. The contraction of *what has*, before a past participle.** (что произошло, что случилось)

4. *What's* happened to the printer?
5. *What's* gotten into you?
6. *What's* he done now?

### where's

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrz/ &middot; *WAIRZ*
**Русский:** где (сокращение от where is или where has)
**Expands to:** *where is* or *where has*
**Type:** be — the question word *where* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. *Where's my phone?* is *is*; *Where's she been all morning?* is *has*, because a past participle follows.
**Contrast:** *where's* sounds exactly like *wears* (*he wears glasses*) and *wares* (goods for sale). Only the apostrophe form is a contraction, and there is no word *wheres*.

where's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *where is*.** (где (находится))

1. *Where's* the nearest pharmacy?
2. *Where's* my charger?
3. *Where's* that noise coming from?

**2. The contraction of *where has*, before a past participle.** (где был, куда делся)

4. *Where's* she been all morning?
5. *Where's* the time gone?
6. *Where's* he run off to now?

### who's

**Pronunciation:** /huːz/ &middot; *HOOZ*
**Русский:** кто (сокращение от who is или who has)
**Expands to:** *who is* or *who has*
**Type:** be — the question word *who* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. *Who's coming?* is *is*; *Who's taken my coat?* is *has*.
**Contrast:** *who's* vs *whose*. If you can expand it to two words, it takes an apostrophe: *Who's coming?* works as *Who is coming?* *Whose* is the possessive (чей) and cannot be expanded — *Whose keys are these?* is not ✗ *Who is keys are these?* The two often land in one sentence: *Who's the guy whose car is blocking mine?*

who's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *who is*.** (кто (есть))

1. *Who's* at the door?
2. *Who's* in charge here?
3. *Who's* coming with us?

**2. The contraction of *who has*, before a past participle.** (кто уже (+ причастие))

4. *Who's* been using my laptop?
5. *Who's* got the keys?
6. *Who's* finished the test?

### how's

**Pronunciation:** /haʊz/ &middot; *HOWZ*
**Русский:** как (сокращение от how is или how has)
**Expands to:** *how is* or *how has*
**Type:** be — the question word *how* + *is* (or + the auxiliary *has*)
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* is *is* or *has*. *How's the new job?* is *is*; *How's it been?* is *has*. *How's* is also the standard American greeting frame — *How's it going?* — where nobody expects a detailed answer.

how's stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *how is*.** (как (есть), как дела)

1. *How's* the new job treating you?
2. *How's* your mom doing?
3. *How's* the weather out there?

**2. The contraction of *how has*, before a past participle.** (как прошло, как было)

4. *How's* your week been so far?
5. *How's* she been since the surgery?
6. *How's* that worked out for you?

### let's

**Pronunciation:** /lɛts/ &middot; *LETS*
**Русский:** давай, давайте (сокращение от let us)
**Expands to:** *let us*
**Type:** fixed — the *'s* is *us*, not *is*: the one contraction built on an object pronoun
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** The *'s* of *let's* is *us*, the object pronoun — the one contraction in English where it is neither *is* nor *has*. And *let's* has no everyday full form: written out, *let us* is either formal and old-fashioned (*Let us pray*) or a literal request for permission (*Let us in*, *Let us know when you land*), and neither of those contracts — ✗ *Let's in*, ✗ *Let's know*.
**Question form:** *Let's go, shall we?* — the tag on a *let's* sentence is *shall we?*, never *don't we?* or *won't we?* The negative is *let's not*: *Let's not argue about it.* The British *don't let's* sounds odd in American English.
**Contrast:** *let's* vs *lets*. *Lets* with no apostrophe is the ordinary third-person verb — *She lets the dog out at seven*, *This ticket lets you in twice*. *Let's* is always a proposal to do something together, so the apostrophe marks the missing *us*.

The contraction of *let us*, used to propose doing something together.

1. *Let's* grab lunch before the meeting.
2. *Let's* not talk about work tonight.
3. *Let's* say it takes three hours — we'd still get there by six.
