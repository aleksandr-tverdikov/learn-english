# Negative contractions

> *don't*, *isn't*, *won't*, *can't* — *not* joined to the verb, and the forms English does not have.

[← Contractions](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

These join *not* to an auxiliary or modal. Three are irregular in sound or spelling: ***won't*** (not *willn't*), ***can't***, and ***shan't***, which has all but vanished from American English. The question form is where learners slip: the contraction inverts as one word — *Isn't he coming?* — and the first-person question is ***aren't I?***, since English has no *amn't*.

---
### isn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪzənt/ &middot; *IZ-uhnt*
**Русский:** не является, не (сокращение от is not)
**Expands to:** *is not*
**Type:** negative — *is* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Question form:** *Isn't he coming?* — the contraction inverts as one word, in front of the subject. ✗ *Is not he coming?* is not English, and *Is he not coming?* is formal and emphatic.
**Contrast:** Two syllables: the *-n't* is a syllabic /ənt/, IZ-uhnt. And *He isn't ready* and *He's not ready* say the same thing — the second puts the weight on *not*. Both are standard.

The contraction of *is not*.

1. This *isn't* the right address.
2. *Isn't* it strange that nobody called?
3. It's cold in here, *isn't* it?

---

### aren't

**Pronunciation:** /ɑːrnt/ &middot; *ARNT*
**Русский:** не являются, не (сокращение от are not; в вопросе aren't I? — от am not)
**Expands to:** *are not* — and, in the question *aren't I?*, *am not*
**Type:** negative — *are* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Ambiguity:** Nearly always *are not*. Only in the first-person question does it stand for *am not*: *I'm late, aren't I?* The statement stays *I'm not late* — never ✗ *I aren't late*.
**Question form:** The first-person tag is *aren't I?*, because English has no *amn't*. *Am I not?* exists but is formal.
**Contrast:** One syllable, ARNT — the *-n't* adds no extra syllable here, unlike the two-syllable *isn't* and *wasn't*. Keep it apart from the nonstandard *ain't* /eɪnt/, which is a different word entirely.

aren't stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *are not*.** (не являются, не)

1. These *aren't* my keys.
2. *Aren't* you cold in that jacket?
3. We *aren't* going to make it on time.

**2. In the first-person question, the contraction of *am not*.** (разве я не)

4. I'm on the list too, *aren't* I?
5. *Aren't* I supposed to sign this part?

---

### wasn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌzənt/ &middot; *WUZ-uhnt*
**Русский:** не был, не была, не было (сокращение от was not)
**Expands to:** *was not*
**Type:** negative — *was* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *wasn't* goes with *I*, *he*, *she* and *it*; after *you*, *we* and *they* it is *weren't*.

The contraction of *was not*.

1. It *wasn't* my fault.
2. She *wasn't* home when I called.
3. That *wasn't* what I meant to say.

---

### weren't

**Pronunciation:** /wɜːrnt/ &middot; *WURNT*
**Русский:** не были (сокращение от were not)
**Expands to:** *were not*
**Type:** negative — *were* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** One syllable, WURNT — unlike most of these, there is no *-uhnt*. In the unreal conditional the verb is *were* for every subject, so the negative is *weren't*: *If I weren't so tired, I'd come.* *If I wasn't* is common in speech and avoided in writing.

The contraction of *were not*.

1. They *weren't* expecting us.
2. *Weren't* you supposed to call me?
3. If it *weren't* so late, I'd stay for another coffee.

---

### don't

**Pronunciation:** /doʊnt/ &middot; *DOHNT*
**Русский:** не делаю, не делают; не надо (сокращение от do not)
**Expands to:** *do not*
**Type:** negative — *do* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Question form:** *Don't you like it?* — one word in front of the subject. ✗ *Do not you like it?* is not English; *Do you not like it?* is formal.
**Contrast:** One syllable, /doʊnt/, rhyming with *won't*. Use it with *I*, *you*, *we* and *they*; with *he*, *she* and *it* the form is *doesn't*. *He don't care* is everywhere in speech and song and still counts as an error in writing.

The contraction of *do not* — the ordinary negative of the present tense and of the imperative.

1. I *don't* know what happened.
2. *Don't* touch that — it's hot.
3. We *don't* have to leave yet.

---

### doesn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʌzənt/ &middot; *DUZ-uhnt*
**Русский:** не делает (сокращение от does not, 3-е лицо ед. ч.)
**Expands to:** *does not*
**Type:** negative — *does* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Question form:** *Doesn't she know?* — the contraction inverts as one word, and the main verb stays bare: ✗ *Doesn't she knows?*
**Contrast:** Spelled from *does*, but said with the vowel of *was*: DUZ-uhnt, never 'DOZE-uhnt'. The verb after it loses its *-s*: *She doesn't work here*, not ✗ *She doesn't works here*.

The contraction of *does not*.

1. He *doesn't* speak Spanish.
2. It *doesn't* matter now.
3. *Doesn't* the store close at nine?

---

### didn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪdənt/ &middot; *DID-uhnt*
**Русский:** не делал, не сделал (сокращение от did not)
**Expands to:** *did not*
**Type:** negative — *did* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** The past is already marked in *didn't*, so the verb that follows stays in its bare form: *I didn't go*, never ✗ *I didn't went*.

The contraction of *did not* — the negative of the simple past.

1. I *didn't* see your message.
2. We *didn't* mean to wake you.
3. *Didn't* you get my email?

---

### haven't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhævənt/ &middot; *HAV-uhnt*
**Русский:** не имею, не имеют; ещё не (сокращение от have not)
**Expands to:** *have not*
**Type:** negative — *have* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** As an auxiliary it makes the negative perfect: *I haven't finished*. For possession, American English says *I don't have a car*; *I haven't got a car* is understood but sounds British.

The contraction of *have not*.

1. I *haven't* finished the report yet.
2. We *haven't* seen each other since college.
3. *Haven't* you heard the news?

---

### hasn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhæzənt/ &middot; *HAZ-uhnt*
**Русский:** не имеет; ещё не (сокращение от has not)
**Expands to:** *has not*
**Type:** negative — *has* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *He's gone* has two negatives: *He hasn't gone* and *He's not gone*. Both are standard, and the second stresses the *not*.

The contraction of *has not*.

1. She *hasn't* called me back.
2. The package *hasn't* arrived yet.
3. *Hasn't* he told you already?

---

### hadn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhædənt/ &middot; *HAD-uhnt*
**Русский:** не имел; ещё не (сокращение от had not)
**Expands to:** *had not*
**Type:** negative — *had* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** The negative of *had better* is *had better not*, never ✗ *hadn't better*: *You'd better not be late.*

The contraction of *had not* — the negative of the past perfect.

1. I *hadn't* met her before that evening.
2. If we *hadn't* left early, we'd have missed the flight.
3. He said he *hadn't* heard anything about it.

---

### won't

**Pronunciation:** /woʊnt/ &middot; *WOHNT*
**Русский:** не буду, не будет; не хочет (сокращение от will not)
**Expands to:** *will not*
**Type:** negative — *will* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Question form:** *Won't you sit down?* — the contracted question is the natural one; *Will you not sit down?* is stiff and marked.
**Contrast:** The sound has nothing of *will* in it: /woʊnt/, one syllable, rhyming with *don't*, not 'will-not' run together. Keep it apart from *want* /wɑːnt/ — *He won't eat* and *He wants to eat* are opposites.

The contraction of *will not*.

1. I *won't* be at the meeting on Thursday.
2. The car *won't* start in this cold.
3. She *won't* answer her phone.

---

### wouldn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʊdənt/ &middot; *WOOD-uhnt*
**Русский:** не стал бы, не хотел (сокращение от would not)
**Expands to:** *would not*
**Type:** negative — *would* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** The *l* is silent and the *-n't* is a syllabic /ənt/: WOOD-uhnt, two syllables. In fast speech the final *t* is barely there, so it can sound like *wooden* with something after it.

The contraction of *would not*.

1. I *wouldn't* do that if I were you.
2. The door *wouldn't* open.
3. She said she *wouldn't* be late.

---

### can't

**Pronunciation:** /kænt/ &middot; *KANT*
**Русский:** не могу, не может; нельзя (сокращение от cannot)
**Expands to:** *cannot* — that is, *can* plus *not*
**Type:** negative — *can* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Question form:** *Can't you hear me?* — one word in front of the subject. ✗ *Cannot you hear me?* is not English; the formal version is *Can you not hear me?*
**Contrast:** American /kænt/ rhymes with *ant*; the British /kɑːnt/ is the other vowel. In American speech the real difference between *can* and *can't* is not the final *t*, which often becomes a catch in the throat, but the vowel: *I can go* reduces *can* to /kən/, while *can't* keeps its full vowel and takes the stress.

The contraction of *cannot*.

1. I *can't* hear you — there's too much noise.
2. We *can't* afford that right now.
3. *Can't* you come a little earlier?

---

### cannot

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkænɑːt/ &middot; *KAN-aht*
**Русский:** не могу, не может; нельзя (can + not, пишется слитно)
**Expands to:** *can not*, written solid as one word
**Type:** negative — one word, and not a contraction: it is simply how *can* plus *not* is written
**Register:** standard — the full written form; *can't* is the everyday spoken one
**Question form:** There is no question form. The question is *Can't you…?*, or the formal *Can you not…?*; ✗ *Cannot you…?* is not English.
**Contrast:** Write it solid. Two words, *can not*, is correct only when the *not* belongs to what follows: *You can not only sing but also dance.* Everywhere else ✗ *I can not swim* is an error for *I cannot swim*. Of the modals, only *can* does this — *will not* and *should not* stay two words.

The negative of *can*, written as one word.

1. I *cannot* accept these terms.
2. The committee *cannot* meet before Friday.
3. Applicants who *cannot* attend in person may send a representative.

---

### couldn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkʊdənt/ &middot; *KOOD-uhnt*
**Русский:** не мог, не смог; не мог бы (сокращение от could not)
**Expands to:** *could not*
**Type:** negative — *could* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** The *l* is silent: KOOD-uhnt. The idiom is *I couldn't care less*; Americans say *I could care less* constantly and mean the same thing, but it is illogical and gets corrected in writing.

The contraction of *could not*.

1. I *couldn't* find your house.
2. We *couldn't* believe our eyes.
3. *Couldn't* you have told me earlier?

---

### shouldn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʃʊdənt/ &middot; *SHOOD-uhnt*
**Русский:** не следует, не должен (сокращение от should not)
**Expands to:** *should not*
**Type:** negative — *should* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
**Contrast:** *shouldn't* plus a past participle is a reproach or a regret about something already done: *You shouldn't have told him.* It is also the everyday American replacement for *oughtn't* and, in prohibitions, for *mustn't*.

The contraction of *should not*.

1. You *shouldn't* work so late.
2. I *shouldn't* have said that.
3. *Shouldn't* we ask first?

---

### mustn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌsənt/ &middot; *MUS-uhnt*
**Русский:** нельзя, не должен (сокращение от must not; запрет)
**Expands to:** *must not*
**Type:** negative — *must* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — correct, but uncommon in American English, which says *can't*, *don't* or *not allowed to*
**Question form:** Americans do not ask *Mustn't we?* The question is *Don't we have to?* or *Can't we?*
**Contrast:** The first *t* is silent: MUS-uhnt. And *mustn't* and *don't have to* are opposites — *You mustn't tell her* forbids it, *You don't have to tell her* leaves it up to you. American English usually reaches for *can't* instead: *You can't tell her.*

The contraction of *must not* — a prohibition, not a lack of obligation.

1. You *mustn't* say anything to your sister.
2. We *mustn't* lose this chance.
3. Visitors *mustn't* feed the animals.

---

### shan't

**Pronunciation:** /ʃænt/ &middot; *SHANT*
**Русский:** не буду (сокращение от shall not; британское и устаревшее)
**Expands to:** *shall not*
**Type:** negative — *shall* plus *not*
**Register:** dated — British and old-fashioned; in American English use *won't*
**Question form:** There is no American question form. *Shan't we?* is British; the American tag is *Won't we?*, and the offer is *Shall we?* with no negative at all.
**Contrast:** In American English *shall* itself has almost disappeared outside *Shall we?* and legal writing, so *shan't* reads as a quotation, a period piece or a joke. Say *won't*. British /ʃɑːnt/, American /ʃænt/ on the rare occasions it is said.

The contraction of *shall not* — rare in American English.

1. 'I *shan't* be a moment,' she called from the hallway.
2. We *shan't* be seeing them again this summer.
3. He *shan't* have the satisfaction of hearing me complain.

---

### needn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniːdənt/ &middot; *NEED-uhnt*
**Русский:** не нужно, не обязательно (сокращение от need not)
**Expands to:** *need not*
**Type:** negative — semi-modal *need* plus *not*
**Register:** standard — correct but formal and uncommon in American English, which says *don't need to* or *don't have to*
**Question form:** Americans do not ask *Needn't we?* The question is *Don't we need to?*
**Contrast:** Semi-modal, so the verb after it is a bare infinitive: *You needn't wait*, never ✗ *You needn't to wait*. Used as an ordinary verb, *need* takes *to* and the *do* forms instead: *You don't need to wait.*

The contraction of *need not* — it is not necessary.

1. You *needn't* wait for me.
2. We *needn't* have hurried — the train was late.
3. She *needn't* know about any of this.

---

### oughtn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔːtənt/ &middot; *AWT-uhnt*
**Русский:** не следует (сокращение от ought not)
**Expands to:** *ought not*
**Type:** negative — *ought* plus *not*
**Register:** dated — rare in American English, which says *shouldn't*
**Question form:** *Oughtn't we?* sounds British and bookish; the American tag is *Shouldn't we?*
**Contrast:** *ought* keeps *to* before the verb: *You oughtn't to say that*, not ✗ *You oughtn't say that* — though the *to* is often dropped in speech, which is one more reason Americans simply say *You shouldn't say that*.

The contraction of *ought not* — rare in American English.

1. You *oughtn't* to speak to her like that.
2. We *oughtn't* to have left him alone.
3. *Oughtn't* somebody to tell him?

---

### daren't

**Pronunciation:** /dɛrnt/ &middot; *DAIRNT*
**Русский:** не смею, не осмеливается (сокращение от dare not)
**Expands to:** *dare not*
**Type:** negative — semi-modal *dare* plus *not*
**Register:** dated — chiefly British and literary; American English says *doesn't dare*
**Question form:** *Daren't we?* does not occur in American English; the question is *Do we dare?*
**Contrast:** Semi-modal, so a bare infinitive follows and there is no *-s* in the third person: *She daren't ask*, never ✗ *She daren't to ask* or ✗ *She daren'ts ask*. The ordinary American sentence is *She doesn't dare ask* or *She doesn't dare to ask*.

The contraction of *dare not* — to lack the nerve to do something.

1. I *daren't* tell him what it cost.
2. She *daren't* look down.
3. We *daren't* move until the noise stopped.

---

### mightn't

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmaɪtənt/ &middot; *MITE-uhnt*
**Русский:** может не, вряд ли (сокращение от might not)
**Expands to:** *might not*
**Type:** negative — modal *might* plus *not*
**Register:** dated — chiefly British; American English writes the two words *might not*
**Question form:** *Mightn't it be simpler to ask?* is British. An American asks *Wouldn't it be simpler to ask?* — and there is no ✗ *mayn't* in any variety.
**Contrast:** American English leaves *might not* uncontracted: *He might not come.* Note the meaning too — *mightn't* is uncertainty, not prohibition; *mustn't* is the prohibition.

The contraction of *might not* — rare in American English.

1. *Mightn't* it be better to wait?
2. She *mightn't* have heard you.
3. I thought you *mightn't* come.

---

### ain't

**Pronunciation:** /eɪnt/ &middot; *AYNT*
**Русский:** не есть, не является; не имеет (просторечное сокращение от am/is/are/has/have not)
**Expands to:** *am not*, *is not*, *are not*, *has not* or *have not*
**Type:** negative — *be* or *have* plus *not*
**Register:** nonstandard — everywhere in speech, song and dialogue, and marked as an error in school and in any formal writing
**Ambiguity:** One form for five. With a subject alone it is *be*: *I ain't* is *am not*, *he ain't* is *is not*, *they ain't* is *are not*. Before a past participle it is *have*: *He ain't finished* is *hasn't finished*. Only the rest of the sentence tells you which.
**Question form:** *Ain't I?* and *Ain't he?* both occur in speech; the standard equivalents are *Aren't I?* and *Isn't he?*
**Contrast:** Recognize it; do not write it. In an essay, an email to a stranger or an exam it will be counted wrong, while in song lyrics, dialogue and everyday talk it is completely ordinary. It also travels with a second negative — *I ain't got no money* — where standard English has *I don't have any money*. The library also lists the fixed expressions *ain't no*, *ain't nobody* and *ain't that the truth*.

ain't stands for 2 different things.

**1. The contraction of *am not*, *is not* or *are not*.** (не есть, не является)

1. I *ain't* going anywhere.
2. That *ain't* what I heard.
3. They *ain't* from around here.

**2. The contraction of *has not* or *have not*, before a past participle.** (не имеет, ещё не)

4. You *ain't* seen nothing yet.
5. He *ain't* finished yet.
6. I *ain't* had a day off in weeks.
