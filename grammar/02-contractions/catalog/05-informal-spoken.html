Spoken reductions and informal forms#

gonna, wanna, y'all, 'em — what English actually sounds like, and where you may write it.

← Contractions · All groups

These are not contractions of the neat kind: they are spellings of ordinary fast speech. Every American says gonna and wanna; almost nobody writes them outside dialogue and text messages. They are worth knowing because you will hear them constantly and because a few have grammar of their own — gonna only works for the future, never for movement: I'm gonna leave is fine, but I'm gonna the store is not English.

gonna#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡʌnə/ · GUH-nuh
Русский
собираюсь, буду (сокращение от going to — только о будущем)
Expands to
going to, the future marker only
Type
informal — a spoken reduction, written only in dialogue, texts and lyrics
Register
informal — universal in American speech, out of place in anything edited
Question form
You gonna eat that? — in a casual question the are vanishes along with the reduction; the full form is Are you going to eat that?
Contrast
Gonna is only the future going to. When going to means literal movement it never reduces: I'm going to the store stays as it is, and ✗ I'm gonna the store is not English. The test is simple — a verb has to follow (gonna leave, gonna rain, gonna be fine), never a noun or a place. Compare wanna, which has the mirror-image rule.

The reduced going to of the future.

  1. I'm gonna call her back in a minute.
  2. It's gonna rain before we get home.
  3. What are you gonna do about it?

wanna#

Pronunciation
/ˈwɑːnə/ · WAH-nuh
Русский
хочу, хочешь (сокращение от want to)
Expands to
want to
Type
informal — a spoken reduction, written only in dialogue, texts and lyrics
Register
informal — heard in nearly every conversation, never used in edited prose
Question form
Wanna grab lunch? — the whole Do you is dropped, which is the ordinary American way of proposing something.
Contrast
Wanna is want to and nothing else, so a verb has to follow: I wanna go, but ✗ I wanna a coffee. In speech I want a coffee reduces to almost the same sound, yet it is written want a — the noun version has no wanna spelling. There is a second, subtler block: when a subject stands between the two words, the reduction is impossible. Who do you want to win? cannot become ✗ Who do you wanna win?, because who is the subject of win.

The reduced want to before a verb.

  1. I wanna leave before the traffic starts.
  2. Do you wanna come with us?
  3. She doesn't wanna talk about it yet.

gotta#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡɑːɾə/ · GAH-duh
Русский
надо, должен; а также «есть у меня» (от have got to и have got a)
Expands to
have got to or have got a
Type
informal — a spoken reduction of the two uses of have got
Register
informal — normal in speech, written only in dialogue and texts
Ambiguity
Two different sentences reduce to the same gotta. Before a verb it is got to and means obligation: I gotta go is I have got to go. Before a noun the final -a is itself the article a: I gotta minute is I have got a minute. That is why ✗ I gotta a minute? is wrong — it writes the article twice.
Question form
Gotta minute? — the Have you is dropped completely. The full form is Have you got a minute?
Contrast
Gotta reduces have got, never the past tense got: I got a letter yesterday has nothing to do with it. Americans usually drop the have as well (I gotta go, rarely I've gotta go), which is fine in speech; in writing use have to or have got to. Compare hafta, which carries the same obligation through have to.

gotta stands for 2 different things.

1. Obligation — the reduced have got to. (надо, должен)

  1. I gotta go — my bus leaves in five minutes.
  2. You gotta see the new place, it's enormous.
  3. We gotta be at the airport by six.

2. Possession — the reduced have got a, with the article already inside it. (у меня есть)

  1. I gotta friend who does exactly this kind of work.
  2. You gotta minute? I need to ask you something.
  3. He's gotta new job downtown.

gimme#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡɪmi/ · GIM-ee
Русский
дай, дай мне (сокращение от give me)
Expands to
give me
Type
informal — a verb fused with its object pronoun
Register
informal — normal in speech, blunt on paper
Contrast
Only me fuses this way: there is no ✗ gimme him or ✗ gimme you. Gimme has also hardened into set expressions — gimme a break is disbelief, not a request for rest, and in sports talk a gimme is a noun meaning something so easy it is granted without being played. Written out, give me is always correct.

The reduced give me.

  1. Gimme a call when you land.
  2. Gimme that wrench — I can reach it from here.
  3. Gimme a second, I'm almost done.

lemme#

Pronunciation
/ˈlɛmi/ · LEM-ee
Русский
дай мне, дай-ка (сокращение от let me)
Expands to
let me
Type
informal — a verb fused with its object pronoun
Register
informal — normal in speech, out of place in edited writing
Contrast
Only let me reduces this way; let him and let her do not. Let us has its own contraction, let's, and that one is fully standard writing. Lemme is always followed by a bare verb: lemme know, lemme see, lemme think.

The reduced let me.

  1. Lemme know what time you're getting in.
  2. Lemme see that picture again.
  3. Lemme think about it overnight.

dunno#

Pronunciation
/dəˈnoʊ/ · duh-NOH
Русский
не знаю (сокращение от (I) don't know)
Expands to
don't know, usually with the I swallowed into it
Type
informal — a whole clause reduced to two syllables
Register
informal — speech and text messages only
Contrast
Dunno normally stands for the entire sentence I don't know: the subject is gone, not merely reduced. It does not conjugate, so ✗ he dunno is not the pattern in General American — for a third person say he doesn't know. In writing it reads as a shrug, which is sometimes exactly wrong in an email.

The reduced (I) don't know.

  1. Dunno — ask Marcy, she was there.
  2. I dunno if the store is still open.
  3. I dunno what he wants me to say.

kinda#

Pronunciation
/ˈkaɪndə/ · KYN-duh
Русский
вроде, как бы, довольно (сокращение от kind of)
Expands to
kind of
Type
informal — a preposition swallowed into the noun before it
Register
informal — texts and casual writing, not essays or business email
Contrast
The library also lists kinda as an adverb of degree, and that softening use is the commoner one: kinda tired, I kinda like him. The literal kind of reduces the same way after a noun or a question word: what kinda car is that? In edited writing, use kind of, somewhat or a little. Its twin is sorta.

The reduced kind of, both as a hedge and in its literal sense.

  1. I'm kinda tired — can we do this tomorrow?
  2. That's kinda what I was afraid of.
  3. What kinda car did she end up buying?

sorta#

Pronunciation
/ˈsɔːrtə/ · SOR-tuh
Русский
вроде, отчасти (сокращение от sort of)
Expands to
sort of
Type
informal — a preposition swallowed into the noun before it
Register
informal — texts and casual writing only
Contrast
Sorta and kinda are interchangeable as hedges, with kinda the commoner of the two in American speech. Neither belongs in edited prose — write sort of, somewhat, or drop the hedge altogether.

The reduced sort of.

  1. It sorta works, but not the way we planned.
  2. I sorta promised I'd be there.
  3. She's sorta my boss and sorta not.

outta#

Pronunciation
/ˈaʊɾə/ · OW-duh
Русский
из, вон из (сокращение от out of)
Expands to
out of
Type
informal — a two-word preposition run together
Register
informal — speech, dialogue and lyrics
Contrast
Outta is out of, not ought to — that one is spelled oughta and is a different word. It lives mostly in fixed phrases: get outta here, outta luck, outta nowhere, outta my way. Written out of is always available and always safe.

The reduced out of.

  1. Get outta here before he sees you.
  2. We're outta milk again.
  3. That came outta nowhere.

lotta#

Pronunciation
/ˈlɑːɾə/ · LAH-duh
Русский
много (сокращение от lot of в обороте a lot of)
Expands to
lot of, always after a
Type
informal — a quantifier phrase run together
Register
informal — speech and casual writing
Contrast
Lotta carries only the lot of part, so the article in front stays: a lotta people, not ✗ lotta people. The plural lots of reduces the same way, to lotsa. Both are speech spellings; a lot of is the written form, and alot is never a word.

The reduced lot of inside a lot of.

  1. There were a lotta people at the funeral.
  2. That's a lotta money for a used car.
  3. He's got a lotta nerve showing up here.

hafta#

Pronunciation
/ˈhæftə/ · HAF-tuh
Русский
надо, должен (сокращение от have to)
Expands to
have to
Type
informal — the obligation have to run together
Register
informal — the spelling is casual, the pronunciation is universal
Contrast
Listen for the /f/: the obligation have to is pronounced with a voiceless /f/ and /t/, which is why it is spelled hafta and not ✗ havta. The possessive have keeps its /v/ — I hafta go but I have a car. The third person reduces the same way, to hasta. Compare gotta, which delivers the same obligation through have got to.

The reduced have to of obligation.

  1. I hafta be up at five tomorrow.
  2. Do you hafta leave so early?
  3. You don't hafta answer that.

coulda#

Pronunciation
/ˈkʊdə/ · KUUD-uh
Русский
мог бы (сокращение от could have)
Expands to
could have
Type
informal — a modal plus a reduced have
Register
informal — speech and dialogue; in writing use could have or could've
Contrast
Coulda is what could've actually sounds like, and it is the source of the commonest spelling mistake in American English: ✗ could of. There is no of in it — the -a is a reduced have, which is why the participle follows (coulda gone, coulda known). The set woulda, coulda, shoulda is a fixed phrase for pointless hindsight.

The reduced could have before a past participle.

  1. I coulda told you that a week ago.
  2. We coulda been there by now if we'd left on time.
  3. You coulda gotten hurt doing that.

woulda#

Pronunciation
/ˈwʊdə/ · WUUD-uh
Русский
сделал бы (сокращение от would have)
Expands to
would have
Type
informal — a modal plus a reduced have
Register
informal — speech and dialogue; in writing use would have or would've
Contrast
Same trap as coulda: the -a is have, never of, so ✗ would of is always an error. Woulda belongs to the unreal past — it needs a condition somewhere, stated or understood: I woulda called implies if I could have. Written out it is would have or would've.

The reduced would have before a past participle.

  1. I woulda called, but my phone was dead.
  2. She woulda said something if she'd known.
  3. That woulda been a lot easier.

shoulda#

Pronunciation
/ˈʃʊdə/ · SHUUD-uh
Русский
надо было, следовало (сокращение от should have)
Expands to
should have
Type
informal — a modal plus a reduced have
Register
informal — speech and dialogue; in writing use should have or should've
Contrast
The -a is have, so ✗ should of is an error here too. Shoulda is almost always regret about something that did not happen: I shoulda known means I did not know and wish I had. Regret about someone else's behavior comes out as blame — You shoulda called.

The reduced should have before a past participle.

  1. I shoulda known better than to trust him.
  2. You shoulda seen his face.
  3. We shoulda booked the earlier flight.

'cause#

Pronunciation
/kəz/ · KUHZ
Русский
потому что (сокращение от because)
Expands to
because
Type
informal — a conjunction with its first syllable gone
Register
informal — dialogue, lyrics and texts; write because in anything edited
Contrast
The apostrophe stands for the missing be-, so the careful spelling is 'cause. The same word is also written cuz, and both are equally informal. Do not confuse it with the noun cause (a reason, a purpose), which rhymes with laws and is a different word entirely: the cause of the fire has nothing to do with 'cause it was cold.

The reduced because.

  1. I left early 'cause I had a headache.
  2. We're not going, 'cause the roads are bad.
  3. Just 'cause he said it doesn't make it true.

cuz#

Pronunciation
/kʌz/ · KUHZ
Русский
потому что (разговорное написание because)
Expands to
because
Type
informal — a conjunction with its first syllable gone
Register
informal — texting and lyrics above all
Ambiguity
Cuz also writes the clipped cousin — my cuz is a relative, not a reason, and in some circles it is a friendly term of address. What follows tells you which: the conjunction takes a whole clause, the noun takes nothing.
Contrast
Cuz, 'cause and the British cos are three spellings of one reduced because. Cuz is the texting spelling; 'cause keeps the apostrophe that marks the missing syllable and looks slightly more careful on the page. Neither belongs in school or business writing, where the full because is the only option.

The reduced because, spelled as it sounds.

  1. I'm staying in cuz it's freezing out.
  2. He quit cuz they wouldn't give him weekends off.
  3. Not cuz I want to — cuz I have to.

y'all#

Pronunciation
/jɔːl/ · YAWL
Русский
вы, вы все (сокращение от you all; южное и афроамер.)
Expands to
you all
Type
dialect — the second-person plural that standard English lacks
Register
regional — Southern and African American English, and now widely used far beyond them
Contrast
The apostrophe replaces the missing letters of you, so the spelling is y'all, never ✗ ya'll. English lost its plural you when thou died, and y'all is the most successful of the forms that fill the hole, beside you guys (national), youse (northeastern) and you'uns or yinz (Pittsburgh). All y'all is not redundant: it forces the reading 'every single one of you'. The possessive is y'all's. The library also lists y'all as a personal pronoun, where its associative use — Is y'all coming Sunday? said to one person but meaning her whole household — is treated in full.

The contracted you all, used as a plural you.

  1. Y'all want to grab something to eat after?
  2. I'll see y'all at the game on Saturday.
  3. All y'all need to be on the bus by seven.

ya#

Pronunciation
/jə/ · YUH
Русский
ты, вы (редуцированное you)
Expands to
you
Type
informal — the weak form of a pronoun, not a dialect form
Register
informal — the spelling is casual, though the sound is what everyone produces
Contrast
Ya is nothing but unstressed you written down: not Southern, not plural, not uneducated. After a /t/ or /d/ it stops being a separate syllable at all and fuses with the word before it — got you becomes gotcha, did you becomes didja, what are you becomes whatcha, bet you becomes betcha. It is never a possessive; the reduced your is yer. The library also lists ya among the personal pronouns.

The reduced you, subject or object alike.

  1. See ya Monday — I'll leave the keys at the desk.
  2. I'll call ya as soon as I land.
  3. Ya never know, it might clear up by noon.

'em#

Pronunciation
/əm/ · UHM
Русский
их, им (редуцированное them)
Expands to
them
Type
informal — a reduced object pronoun, leaning on the verb before it
Register
informal — in writing only; in speech it is simply the normal unstressed form
Contrast
The surprise is that 'em is not a clipping of them at all. It descends from Middle English hem, the native plural object pronoun that the Norse-borrowed them pushed out of the written language while the old form survived in the mouth. The apostrophe writes down a history nobody remembers. Fixed phrases keep it: give 'em hell, knock 'em dead, round 'em up, you can't win 'em all. When the pronoun carries stress it is always them. The library also lists 'em among the personal pronouns.

The unstressed them, written as it sounds.

  1. Just tell 'em we'll be ten minutes late.
  2. I saw 'em leaving as I pulled in.
  3. Round 'em up and load 'em on the truck.

o'clock#

Pronunciation
/əˈklɑːk/ · uh-KLAHK
Русский
час, часов (о времени по часам; из of the clock)
Expands to
of the clock
Type
fixed — frozen from of the clock, the last survivor of a whole family
Register
standard — fully standard everywhere, in speech and in the most formal writing
Contrast
The apostrophe does exactly what it does in can't: it marks something missing, here the whole of of the. Nothing else survives of the old pattern — five of the clock has been gone for two centuries. Use it only with whole hours from one to twelve: at six o'clock, but at 6:30 and never ✗ 6:30 o'clock. Never combine it with a.m. or p.m. (✗ seven o'clock p.m.); say seven p.m. or seven o'clock in the evening. Russian в шесть часов is at six o'clock or simply at six. The library also lists o'clock as an adverb of time.

The contracted of the clock, used after a number to name a whole hour.

  1. The store opens at nine o'clock.
  2. It's almost three o'clock — we should get going.
  3. I'll meet you at the station at six o'clock.

ma'am#

Pronunciation
/mæm/ · MAM
Русский
мэм, мадам (вежливое обращение; сокращение от madam)
Expands to
madam
Type
fixed — frozen from madam
Register
standard — polite address, near-obligatory in the South and in the military
Contrast
The apostrophe replaces the -d- of madam, so the spelling is ma'am, not ✗ mam or ✗ maam. Its counterpart is sir, which is not a contraction of anything. Yes, ma'am is plain courtesy in the South, in the armed forces and in service work; in the Northeast and on the West Coast it can land badly on a younger woman, who may hear it as a comment on her age. The unreduced madam now survives only in titles like Madam President and in the salutation Dear Madam.

The contracted madam, used to address a woman politely.

  1. Yes, ma'am, I'll have that ready by Friday.
  2. Excuse me, ma'am — you dropped your scarf.
  3. Can I get you anything else, ma'am?

s'pose#

Pronunciation
/spoʊz/ · SPOHZ
Русский
полагаю, наверное (сокращение от suppose)
Expands to
suppose
Type
informal — a verb with its first syllable gone
Register
informal — dialogue and casual speech; write suppose otherwise
Contrast
S'pose loses the whole first syllable of suppose. It turns up in two places above all: the lukewarm agreement I s'pose so, and s'posed to, which is how supposed to actually sounds — note that the -d goes silent too. Do not mix s'posed to up with hafta and gotta: supposed to reports a rule or expectation somebody else set, and often hints that it was broken.

The reduced suppose, including the supposed to of expectation.

  1. I s'pose so, if there's no other way.
  2. You're not s'posed to park there.
  3. What am I s'posed to tell her?

whatcha#

Pronunciation
/ˈwʌtʃə/ · WUH-chuh
Русский
что ты…? (сокращение от what are you или what do you)
Expands to
what are you or what do you
Type
informal — a question word fused with its auxiliary and pronoun
Register
informal — speech, dialogue and lyrics only
Ambiguity
The same whatcha hides two different auxiliaries. Before an -ing form it is what are you: Whatcha doing? Before a bare verb it is what do you: Whatcha want? Nothing in the spelling tells you which — only the verb that follows.
Question form
Whatcha exists only in questions; there is no statement form. The full versions are What are you…? and What do you…?, and the ya inside it is the reduced you fusing with the /t/ before it.

whatcha stands for 2 different things.

1. Standing for what are you, before an -ing form. (что ты (делаешь)…?)

  1. Whatcha doing this weekend?
  2. Whatcha looking at?
  3. Whatcha laughing about?

2. Standing for what do you, before a bare verb. (что ты (хочешь)…?)

  1. Whatcha want on your burger?
  2. Whatcha think of the new place?

betcha#

Pronunciation
/ˈbɛtʃə/ · BECH-uh
Русский
спорим, точно (сокращение от bet you)
Expands to
bet you
Type
informal — a verb fused with a reduced you
Register
informal — speech and dialogue
Contrast
Betcha is bet you with the /t/ and the /j/ of ya fused into a /tʃ/ — the same change that turns got you into gotcha and what are you into whatcha. Two patterns account for nearly all its uses: I betcha introduces a prediction, and You betcha is a fixed interjection meaning an emphatic yes, associated with the Upper Midwest and with nobody's actual wager.

The reduced bet you, in predictions and in the fixed You betcha.

  1. I betcha he shows up twenty minutes late.
  2. You betcha I'll be there.
  3. Betcha can't guess who I ran into today.
