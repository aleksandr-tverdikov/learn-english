Contractions of have and had#

I've, could've, I'd — including the 'd that is sometimes had and sometimes would.

← Contractions · All groups

've is always have. 'd is the ambiguous one: I'd gone is had, I'd go is would, and only the verb that follows tells you which. The -'ve of could've sounds exactly like of, which is why could of is the most common spelling error in written American English — it is never correct.

I've#

Pronunciation
/aɪv/ · YV
Русский
я уже (сделал) — сокращение от I have
Expands to
I have
Type
have — a subject plus the auxiliary have
Register
standard — normal in speech and in all but the most formal writing
Contrast
've contracts the auxiliary only. American English does not contract have meaning 'own': I have a car, never ✗ I've a car (that is British and dated). And no contraction can end a clause: Yes, I have, never ✗ Yes, I've.

The contraction of I have, the auxiliary of the present perfect.

  1. I've lived in Chicago since 2015.
  2. I've already eaten, thanks.
  3. I've got to leave by six.

you've#

Pronunciation
/juːv/ · YOOV
Русский
ты/вы уже (сделал) — сокращение от you have
Expands to
you have
Type
have — a subject plus the auxiliary have
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Contrast
Unstressed, you've is said /jəv/ and the 've is easy to miss: You've seen it and You saw it differ by one weak syllable. The written trap is the pronoun — you've is you have, you're is you are, and your is the possessive.

The contraction of you have, the auxiliary of the present perfect.

  1. You've been working too hard lately.
  2. You've got mail.
  3. If you've finished the form, bring it to the window.

we've#

/wiːv/ · WEEV

we have
Type
have — a subject plus the auxiliary have

We've is pronounced exactly like weave. Do not confuse it with we'd (we would or we had) or we're (we are).

The contraction of we have, the auxiliary of the present perfect.

  1. We've known each other since college.
  2. We've got a problem with the heater.
  3. We've never been to Texas.

they've#

Pronunciation
/ðeɪv/ · THAYV
Русский
они уже (сделали) — сокращение от they have
Expands to
they have
Type
have — a subject plus the auxiliary have
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Contrast
The three they contractions are different words: they're is they are, they'd is they would or they had, and they've is they have. None of them is their.

The contraction of they have, the auxiliary of the present perfect.

  1. They've lived in that house for thirty years.
  2. They've already left for the airport.
  3. I don't think they've met before.

who've#

Pronunciation
/huːv/ · HOOV
Русский
которые уже (сделали) — сокращение от who have
Expands to
who have
Type
have — the relative or question word who plus the auxiliary have
Register
standard — but far rarer than who's, and mostly heard rather than written
Question form
Who've you told? is normal in speech; in writing most Americans spell it out — Who have you told?
Contrast
Who've is who have; who's is who is or who has; whose is the possessive and takes no apostrophe.

The contraction of who have.

  1. Students who've missed three classes can't take the final.
  2. There are plenty of people who've never heard of him.
  3. Who've you been talking to?

could've#

Pronunciation
/ˈkʊdəv/ · KUUD-uhv
Русский
мог бы (тогда) — сокращение от could have
Expands to
could have
Type
modal — could plus a contracted have
Register
informal — normal in speech; formal writing spells it out as could have
Question form
There is no contracted question: Could you have known?, never ✗ Could've you known?
Contrast
The -'ve is unstressed /əv/ and sounds exactly like of, which is why ✗ could of is the most common spelling error in written American English. It is never correct, in any register — if you cannot hear which it is, expand it to could have.

The contraction of could have: what was possible but did not happen.

  1. You could've told me you were bringing someone.
  2. The whole thing could've been avoided.
  3. I could've sworn I left my keys on the table.

would've#

Pronunciation
/ˈwʊdəv/ · WUUD-uhv
Русский
сделал бы (тогда) — сокращение от would have
Expands to
would have
Type
modal — would plus a contracted have
Register
informal — normal in speech; formal writing spells it out as would have
Question form
There is no contracted question: Would you have come?, never ✗ Would've you come?
Contrast
✗ would of is never correct — the -'ve is unstressed /əv/ and only sounds like of. Watch the halves of the third conditional: If I'd known, I would've called — had in the if clause, would have in the main clause, never ✗ If I would've known.

The contraction of would have: what was going to be true but was not.

  1. I would've called, but my phone was dead.
  2. She would've made a great teacher.
  3. Nobody would've guessed he was nervous.

should've#

Pronunciation
/ˈʃʊdəv/ · SHUUD-uhv
Русский
надо было (сделать) — сокращение от should have
Expands to
should have
Type
modal — should plus a contracted have
Register
informal — normal in speech; formal writing spells it out as should have
Question form
There is no contracted question: Should I have said something?, never ✗ Should've I said something?
Contrast
✗ should of is never correct, however much the -'ve sounds like of. Note what the form means: should've is always a reproach or a regret about the past — You should've called says that you did not call.

The contraction of should have: what was the right thing to do and was not done.

  1. I should've studied more for that test.
  2. You should've seen the look on his face.
  3. We should've left an hour ago.

might've#

Pronunciation
/ˈmaɪtəv/ · MYT-uhv
Русский
возможно, сделал — сокращение от might have
Expands to
might have
Type
modal — might plus a contracted have
Register
informal — normal in speech; formal writing spells it out as might have
Contrast
✗ might of is never correct. Keep the two deductions apart: might've is a guess (He might've left — maybe, maybe not), while must've is a conclusion (He must've left — I am sure of it).

The contraction of might have: a guess about the past, or about what nearly happened.

  1. He might've left already — his car is gone.
  2. I might've put it in the other drawer.
  3. You might've warned me before I signed it.

must've#

Pronunciation
/ˈmʌstəv/ · MUHST-uhv
Русский
наверняка сделал — сокращение от must have
Expands to
must have
Type
modal — must plus a contracted have
Register
informal — normal in speech; formal writing spells it out as must have
Contrast
✗ must of is never correct. And must've is never obligation in the past: He must've left means 'I am sure he left'; for obligation you say he had to leave.

The contraction of must have: a confident conclusion about the past.

  1. He must've left — the lights are off.
  2. You must've been exhausted after that drive.
  3. I must've missed the turn somewhere.

I'd#

Pronunciation
/aɪd/ · YD
Русский
я бы; я уже — сокращение от I would или I had
Expands to
I would or I had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
I'd is I would before a bare verb (I'd go) and I had before a past participle (I'd gone). Two fixed phrases settle it by themselves: I'd better is always had, I'd rather is always would.
Contrast
The third conditional uses both in one sentence: If I'd known, I'd have said something — the first I'd is had, the second is would.

I'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of I would. (я бы)

  1. I'd love to come to the party.
  2. I'd rather stay home tonight.
  3. If I had the money, I'd buy it.

2. The contraction of I had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (я уже (сделал))

  1. By the time she called, I'd already gone to bed.
  2. I'd never seen snow before that winter.
  3. I'd better get going.

you'd#

Pronunciation
/juːd/ · YOOD
Русский
ты бы; ты уже — сокращение от you would или you had
Expands to
you would or you had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
You'd like it is would; You'd finished is had. You'd better is had — and in speech it shrinks further, to you better or even a bare better.
Contrast
You'd is you would or you had, never you are — that is you're.

you'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of you would. (ты бы, вы бы)

  1. You'd like her — everybody does.
  2. You'd be surprised how often that happens.
  3. If you asked him, you'd get the same answer.

2. The contraction of you had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (ты уже (сделал))

  1. You'd better call your mother.
  2. She said you'd already paid for the tickets.
  3. By then you'd been waiting almost an hour.

he'd#

Pronunciation
/hiːd/ · HEED
Русский
он бы; он уже — сокращение от he would или he had
Expands to
he would or he had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
He'd go is would; He'd gone is had. He'd better is had, he'd rather is would.
Contrast
He'd is a homophone of heed, and in the middle of a sentence the h often drops, so what he'd said runs together as one word. Do not confuse it with he's, which is he is or he has.

he'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of he would. (он бы)

  1. He'd never admit it, but he was scared.
  2. I asked if he'd help us move.
  3. He'd rather walk than wait for the bus.

2. The contraction of he had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (он уже (сделал))

  1. By the time we got there, he'd eaten.
  2. She told me he'd been sick all week.
  3. He'd better have a good excuse.

she'd#

Pronunciation
/ʃiːd/ · SHEED
Русский
она бы; она уже — сокращение от she would или she had
Expands to
she would or she had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
She'd like it is would; She'd liked it is had. The participle is the whole test: she'd take it (would) against she'd taken it (had).
Contrast
She'd is she would or she had, never she is or she has — those are she's.

she'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of she would. (она бы)

  1. She'd like to meet you sometime.
  2. She'd rather not talk about it.
  3. I knew she'd say no.

2. The contraction of she had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (она уже (сделала))

  1. She'd already left when I got there.
  2. She'd never flown before that trip.
  3. He mentioned that she'd been promoted twice.

it'd#

Pronunciation
/ˈɪtəd/ · IT-uhd
Русский
это было бы; это уже — сокращение от it would или it had
Expands to
it would or it had
Type
would — or had, though the had reading is rare
Register
informal — common in speech, seldom written outside dialogue; in print, spell out it would
Ambiguity
It'd take an hour is would; It'd rained all night is had. With it, the would reading is far commoner.
Contrast
Unlike the one-syllable it's, it'd has two syllables, and the American flapped t makes it sound close to 'id-ud'. It is a spoken form: write it would.

it'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of it would. (это было бы)

  1. It'd be great if you could come.
  2. It'd take about two hours to drive there.
  3. I thought it'd be colder than this.

2. The contraction of it had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (это уже (было))

  1. The ground was soaked — it'd rained all night.
  2. By morning it'd stopped snowing.

they'd#

Pronunciation
/ðeɪd/ · THAYD
Русский
они бы; они уже — сокращение от they would или they had
Expands to
they would or they had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
They'd agree is would; They'd agreed is had — and here the two differ by one consonant, so listen for the -ed.
Contrast
They'd is they would or they had; they've is they have; they're is they are; their and there are not contractions at all.

they'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of they would. (они бы)

  1. They'd never agree to that.
  2. I figured they'd call back by now.
  3. They'd rather pay cash.

2. The contraction of they had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (они уже (сделали))

  1. They'd already sold the house when we called.
  2. She said they'd been friends since high school.

there'd#

Pronunciation
/ðɛrd/ · THAIRD
Русский
было бы; уже было — сокращение от there would или there had
Expands to
there would or there had
Type
would — or had; with the existential there
Register
standard — much commoner in speech than in writing
Ambiguity
There'd be trouble is would; There'd been trouble is had. After there, the had reading is always followed by been.
Contrast
This is the existential there of there is and there was, not the place word — and never their or they're.

there'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of there would. (было бы, нашлось бы)

  1. There'd be a lot less traffic if we left at six.
  2. Nobody warned us there'd be a fee.
  3. If we canceled now, there'd be no refund.

2. The contraction of there had, always before been. (уже было)

  1. There'd been a fire in the building the week before.
  2. She had no idea there'd been any complaints.

who'd#

Pronunciation
/huːd/ · HOOD
Русский
кто бы; который уже — сокращение от who would или who had
Expands to
who would or who had
Type
would — or had; the verb that follows tells you which
Register
standard — normal in speech and in ordinary writing
Ambiguity
Who'd want that? is would; the man who'd left is had. Casual speech adds a third reading that is never written outside dialogue: Who'd you call? is who did.
Contrast
Who'd is a homophone of hood. It is not who's (who is or who has), and not whose, the possessive, which takes no apostrophe.

who'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of who would. (кто бы)

  1. Who'd have thought it would end like that?
  2. Who'd want to drive four hours for one meeting?

2. The contraction of who had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (который уже (сделал))

  1. The witness who'd seen the car never came forward.
  2. He introduced me to a cousin who'd just moved to town.

that'd#

Pronunciation
/ˈðætəd/ · THAT-uhd
Русский
это было бы — сокращение от that would или that had
Expands to
that would or that had
Type
would — or had, though the had reading is rare
Register
informal — speech and dialogue only; in writing, spell out that would
Ambiguity
That'd be great is would; that'd been settled is had. With that, 'd is almost always would.
Contrast
Two syllables, with the American flapped t, so it sounds close to 'thad-ud'. It is not that's, which is that is or that has.

that'd stands for 2 different things.

1. The contraction of that would. (это было бы)

  1. That'd be great, thanks.
  2. That'd cost more than the car is worth.
  3. I didn't think that'd work.

2. The contraction of that had, the auxiliary of the past perfect. (это уже (было))

  1. He said that'd already been decided.
  2. We assumed that'd been taken care of.
