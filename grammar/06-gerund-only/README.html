Gerund only#

Every word in English after which a verb has to appear as -ing and can never appear as to do — the one rule that generates most of them, the eighty-one verbs that are simply facts, and the shorter list this one is constantly confused with.

← All grammar topics · Library home

English has three things it can put in the slot after a word when what goes there is another verb: the bare infinitive (let him go, make him wait), the to-infinitive** (want to go, decide to wait), and the -ing form** (enjoy going, avoid waiting). Nothing about the meaning of the sentence tells you which one a given word takes. I want to leave and I avoid leaving are built the same way and mean related things, and yet ✗ I avoid to leave is as wrong as ✗ I want leaving.

This page is the third slot, and only the third: the words after which -ing is the only possibility. It is the page to open when you are halfway through a sentence, your finger is on the t of to, and you want to know whether that to is allowed.

The organizing idea is one sentence long:

The -ing form is the shape an English verb takes when it has to behave like a noun. That is what the grammatical name gerund means. A gerund does every job a noun does — it is a subject (Swimming is good for you), an object (I enjoy swimming), a complement (My job is teaching), and, above all, the object of a preposition (good at swimming). Wherever the grammar has carved out a noun-shaped hole and you want to put a verb in it, the -ing form is what comes out.

Most of this page follows from that one fact. A smaller part of it — the verb list in §2 — does not follow from anything and has to be learned the way irregular verbs are learned, whole.

1. The rule behind most of the list: after a preposition, a verb becomes -ing#

A preposition is a word that takes a noun after it. In the car. After lunch. Without a coat. Good at math. That is the whole definition, and it has a consequence that nobody states loudly enough:

If you want to put a verb after a preposition, the preposition does not care. It still wants a noun, so the verb has to arrive in its noun shape — the -ing form.

I'm good at math. → I'm good at cooking. — ✗ good at to cook He left without his coat. → He left without saying goodbye. — ✗ without to say We talked about the budget. → We talked about hiring somebody. — ✗ about to hire Thanks for the ride. → Thanks for driving me. — ✗ for to drive

There is no exception to this. Not one preposition in English takes a to-infinitive after it. That single fact covers §3, §4, §5 and §7 of this page — four of the six lists here are not separate rules at all, only four places where prepositions turn up.

The noun test#

This is the test to run when you are unsure, and it takes two seconds.

Put a plain noun in the slot. If a noun fits, the word in front is a preposition, and the verb must be -ing. If a noun does not fit, the word in front is something else and this page probably does not apply.

SentenceNoun in the slotVerdict
She's interested in ___…in art ✓preposition → interested in learning
I apologized for ___…for the delay ✓preposition → apologized for being late
He insisted on ___…on a receipt ✓preposition → insisted on paying
They decided ___✗ decided the receiptnot a preposition → decided to pay
I hope ___✗ hope the receiptnot a preposition → hope to pay

The test works because it is not a test about verbs at all. It asks a question about the word before the slot, which is the word that is actually making the decision.

The to trap#

Here is the reason this page exists at all. English spells two completely different words t-o.

One is a preposition — the same word as in go to Denver, give it to her, from nine to five. Like every preposition, it takes a noun, so a verb after it comes out as -ing.

The other is the infinitive marker, a grammatical particle with no meaning of its own that exists only to introduce the base form of a verb: want to go, need to leave, able to swim. It is not a preposition, it takes no noun, and the verb after it stays bare.

They are spelled identically, pronounced identically, and there is nothing in the sentence to tell them apart — except the noun test.

Used to: two words, one spelling#

This pair is worth its own block, because both members are extremely common and they mean nothing alike.

FormMeaningExample

The give-away is the verb in front. If there is a form of be or get before used, to is a preposition and -ing follows. If used stands alone as the past tense of a verb, to is the infinitive marker.

He used to smoke a pack a day. — and then he quit He's used to smoking outside now. — the new rule stopped bothering him It took a month to get used to driving on the other side. ✗ I'm used to work here. → I'm used to working here. — or, for the habit sense, I used to work here.

Both sentences in that last line are correct English about entirely different situations, which is exactly why the error survives correction.

2. The verbs that take only a gerund#

This is the part of the page that is not a rule. These verbs take an -ing object and refuse a to-infinitive, and there is no meaning, no ending and no pattern that predicts membership. Enjoy takes -ing and like takes both; avoid takes -ing and refuse takes the infinitive; finish takes -ing and complete the job of takes a noun. The list has to be learned.

Two things make it learnable. First, a loose semantic family runs through the middle of it: a large share of these verbs are about stopping, avoiding, postponing, enduring, disliking, or reporting something that already exists as an event. The gerund names a whole activity, not a step to be taken, which is why to-infinitives — which point forward at something not yet done — do not fit. Second, once you know the verb takes -ing, no further decisions follow: there is no object variant, no register variant and no American–British split, except where the table flags one.

Reading the table. A ◆ marks a verb whose pattern changes when an object is inserted — these are the ones that produce half-correct sentences and are worked out in full under the table. A ◇ marks a verb that also allows a to-infinitive with no real change of meaning.

Eighty-one entries, and not one of them takes to. ✗ enjoy to read, ✗ avoid to drive, ✗ finish to paint, ✗ suggest to leave, ✗ consider to move, ✗ deny to know, ✗ practice to speak, ✗ risk to lose — every one is the same error, and it is the single most recognizable marker of a Russian speaker's English.

The ◆ verbs: what an object does to the pattern#

Eight of the verbs above look like counterexamples the first time a learner sees They allowed us to leave. They are not. Insert a person, and a different construction takes over. The verb has two patterns, and which one you get depends entirely on whether the slot after the verb holds a person.

Three notes that decide whether the sentence is right or wrong:

1. Suggest and recommend never take an object + infinitive. This is worth memorizing as a flat prohibition, because the wrong version is fluent, confident and wrong: ✗ He suggested me to leave, ✗ I recommend you to book early. English has two ways to say it and neither uses an infinitive.

✗ He suggested me to leave early. ✓ He suggested leaving early. — no person named ✓ He suggested that I leave early. — the person goes in a that-clause ✗ They recommended us to try the pier. ✓ They recommended trying the pier. · ✓ They recommended that we try the pier.

The that-clause takes the base form of the verb (that I leave, not ✗ that I leaves) because it is a subjunctive; the mechanics are in reported speech, and the same trap is catalogued there because it is a reporting verb before it is anything else.

2. The from family. Prevent, stop, prohibit, ban, discourage, deter and keep all take object + from + -ing, never an infinitive. From is a preposition, so §1 does the rest of the work.

The rain prevented us from finishing. — ✗ prevented us to finish Nothing is stopping you from applying. — ✗ stopping you to apply They discouraged her from reapplying. The fence keeps the deer from getting into the garden.

Prevent is the one that also works without from (prevented us finishing), which is why it is the one learners over-generalize from. The others need it.

3. Explain, describe, mention and report take no person as a direct object at all. These verbs are on the list above, but they carry a second, unrelated trap: you say something to somebody.

✗ Can you explain me the difference? → Can you explain the difference to me? ✗ She described me the apartment. → She described the apartment to me. ✗ He mentioned me that he was leaving. → He mentioned to me that he was leaving.

Three verbs on the list that behave oddly#

Dread is gerund-only except in one frozen phrase: I dread to think what that cost. Nothing else survives — dread telling, dread going, dread finding out.

Understand takes a gerund only in the sense "see why somebody would do that," and it almost always carries a possessive: I can understand his wanting to leave. In its ordinary sense it takes a that-clause: I understand that you're leaving.

Keep means two different things with the same shape. He keeps calling can be "he does it repeatedly" or "he has not stopped," and the sentence rarely needs to distinguish them. What it never means is purpose — for that, English uses a different verb entirely: ✗ He keeps to call is not a sentence with a different meaning, it is not a sentence.

3. Phrasal verbs and verb + preposition#

Two different structures end up in this section, and from the point of view of this page they behave identically.

  • Particle verbs: give up, put off, carry on, end up. The second word is a particle, and what follows it is an object slot.
  • Prepositional verbs: insist on, succeed in, object to. The second word is a plain preposition and the verb has simply chosen it.

Either way, the slot at the end wants a noun — give up cigarettes, insist on a receipt — so a verb landing there arrives as -ing. Nothing new is happening; §1 is doing all of the work.

Four of these need a note#

Burst out takes exactly two verbs: burst out laughing and burst out crying. Nothing else is idiomatic — ✗ burst out singing, ✗ burst out running are not English, and the general verb for that meaning is start.

Feel like has two lives. I feel like getting a coffee is a wish — -ing is required. I feel like I'm getting sick is a comparison and takes a whole clause. ✗ I feel like to get a coffee is the error, and it is common.

End up and wind up describe an unintended destination and take -ing or a noun phrase: We ended up walking, We ended up at a diner. ✗ ended up to walk.

Can't help belongs to §6 but is worth flagging here for its one exception: can't help + -ing (I can't help laughing) has a variant can't help but + bare infinitive (I can't help but laugh). Both are standard American English; what is wrong is mixing them — ✗ can't help but laughing, ✗ can't help to laugh.

The one adjective pair that goes the other way#

Some adjectives take a to-infinitive instead, and they are the reason a learner hesitates: happy to help, ready to go, able to swim, likely to rain, difficult to explain, easy to use, the first to arrive. These have no preposition, so §1 never fires. The test is the same as everywhere else: if there is no preposition, this page does not apply.

Afraid is the pair that shows the split inside one word:

I'm afraid of falling. — the fear itself, a general dread I'm afraid to look. — too frightened to take a specific action

Both are correct, and the difference is real: afraid of + -ing names what frightens you, afraid to + infinitive names what the fear stops you doing.

The in that can disappear. After difficulty, trouble, a problem and a hard time, the preposition is optional and Americans usually drop it. Both are correct; the bare version is commoner in speech.

Go + -ing#

English has one construction for leisure activities you go somewhere to do, and it is an -ing form with no preposition and no article:

We're going fishing on Saturday. She goes running before work. They went camping in the Sawtooths.

The inventory is large and open, and nearly all of it is recreation or errands: go swimming, shopping, fishing, hunting, hiking, camping, running, jogging, walking, climbing, skiing, snowboarding, skating, surfing, sailing, kayaking, bowling, golfing, riding, sightseeing, dancing, drinking, clubbing, birdwatching, house-hunting, job-hunting, window-shopping.

Two errors go with it. The first is inserting to: ✗ go to shopping, ✗ go for swimming. The second is the preposition after it: you do not go swimming to the lake, you go swimming in the lake; you do not go shopping to the mall, you go shopping at the mall. The place preposition describes where the activity happens, not where you went.

✗ We went to fishing. → We went fishing. ✗ She goes shopping to the outlet mall. → …shopping at the outlet mall.

There's no + -ing#

A small, fixed family meaning "it is impossible to." The -ing form is the only one available, and the whole expression is a single unit.

There's no telling what he'll say. There's no knowing how the vote will go. There's no denying it was a good deal. There's no getting around the deadline. There's no pleasing some people. There's no accounting for taste.

✗ There's no to tell is not a near miss; it is not a structure. The alternative with an infinitive uses different words entirely: It's impossible to tell.

Mind#

Mind deserves a line of its own because it is the standard polite request, and both halves of it are -ing:

Would you mind opening the window? — you do it Would you mind my opening the window? — I do it; see §9 Do you mind waiting a few minutes?

And the answer is a trap in both languages: Would you mind…? asks whether it bothers you, so no means yes, go ahead. Americans usually sidestep it with Not at all or Go right ahead.

The reduced clause#

Before, after, since, while, when and on are the interesting ones, because with -ing they are not really prepositions at all: they are conjunctions introducing a clause with the subject and the tensed verb squeezed out of it.

After he finished the draft, he went home. — full clause After finishing the draft, he went home. — reduced

The reduction buys compactness and costs one thing: the subject. Since it is no longer written, the reader recovers it from the main clause, and English enforces that rigidly.

The same-subject requirement. The understood subject of the -ing clause must be the subject of the main clause. If it is not, the sentence is a dangling participle — grammatically broken, and frequently funny.

✓ After finishing the draft, she sent the report. — she finished, she sent ✗ After finishing the draft, the report was sent. — this says the report finished the draft ✗ While driving home, the phone rang. — the phone was not driving ✗ Before signing, the terms should be read carefully. — the terms are not signing anything

The repairs are always the same two:

Put the real subject back into the main clause: After finishing the draft, she sent the report. Or write the clause out in full: After the draft was finished, the report was sent.

This is one of the few errors in English that a native speaker's ear catches instantly and a learner's does not, because the learner is parsing the sentence and the native speaker is picturing it.

Negation goes in front of the -ing, not in front of the preposition:

Not knowing what else to do, I called the police. He left without saying anything — the standard way to express "and he did not say anything"

Perfect gerunds are available when the time gap matters, and are mostly formal: After having finished the draft, she… In most sentences the plain -ing already implies the earlier time, so after finishing is the normal choice and the perfect is reserved for cases where a contrast could be misread.

Rather than, and the exception#

Rather than is the one entry in the table that does not always take -ing. It takes whatever form balances the thing it is being contrasted with.

ContextForm after rather thanExample

At the start of the sentencebare infinitive or -ingRather than go home, he stayed. · Rather than going home, he stayed.
Followed by a nouna nounHe ordered tea rather than coffee.

The rule underneath it is parallelism: whatever shape is on one side of rather than, put the same shape on the other. The same applies to as well as, which takes -ing after a finite verb (He runs the shop as well as doing the books) but a bare infinitive after an infinitive (He agreed to run the shop as well as do the books).

8. need, want, require, deserve + -ing: the passive pattern#

A small pattern with a strange property: the -ing form is active in shape and passive in meaning.

The car needs washing. = The car needs to be washed. These shirts need ironing. = …need to be ironed. The proposal requires rewriting. = …requires to be rewritten. That fence wants fixing. = …needs to be fixed. The whole idea deserves looking into. = …deserves to be looked into.

Nobody is washing in needs washing; the car is being washed. The pattern works only when the subject of the sentence is the thing being acted on, and it exists because English found it shorter than the passive infinitive.

VerbFrequencyNote

One regional form to recognize but not copy. In western Pennsylvania, Ohio and parts of the Midland, people say The car needs washed — need + past participle, with the to be omitted entirely. It is a genuine regional feature of American English, not an error by the people who say it, and it will be read as an error everywhere else. Write needs washing or needs to be washed.

9. The possessive before a gerund#

When a gerund has a subject of its own — somebody other than the subject of the main verb is doing it — that subject can appear in two forms:

I don't mind his leaving early. — possessive, the traditional form I don't mind him leaving early. — object form, what people say

Both are current. The difference is register, not correctness, and the rule of thumb that survives contact with real usage is:

SituationPreferred formExample
Formal or edited writingpossessiveWe object to his being named in the report.
Ordinary speech and emailobject formWe're fine with him being there.
The gerund phrase is the subject of the sentencepossessive, stronglyHis leaving surprised everyone.
A proper nameeither; 's is more formalDana's quitting … · Dana quitting …
A long or plural noun phraseplain noun, no 's…the chance of the two departments merging
An inanimate subjectplain noun, no 's…the risk of the roof leaking

What do you think of my taking the early flight? — formal What do you think of me taking the early flight? — normal ✗ What do you think of I taking… — the subject pronoun is never an option Their refusing to sign killed the deal. — subject position; the object form here (Them refusing) reads as speech We hadn't considered the possibility of the bridge closing. — never ✗ the bridge's closing

The practical advice, which is what a learner actually needs: use the possessive with pronouns and names in anything you would call writing, and use the object form in speech. The possessive in an email will read as careful; the object form in a legal brief will read as loose. Neither will read as foreign.

One place the distinction is not stylistic. After verbs of perception — see, hear, watch, feel, notice, find — the -ing word is a participle, not a gerund, and the object form is the only possibility:

I saw him leaving. — I saw him; he was leaving ✗ I saw his leaving. — not English

Compare I appreciate his coming (gerund; I appreciate the act) with I saw him coming (participle; I saw the person). The test: if you can replace the whole phrase with it, it is a gerund and the possessive is available. I appreciate it. ✗ I saw it does not mean the same thing.

11. For Russian speakers#

Russian has no gerund in the English sense, and the gap is filled by the infinitive almost everywhere. «Я люблю читать», «Я закончил читать», «Вместо того чтобы идти» — three different English structures, one Russian form. The learner reaches for the infinitive because that is what the source language offers, and English accepts it in about half the cases, which is exactly the wrong amount: enough to keep the habit alive, not enough to be right.

Three facts about the mismatch are worth having in front of you:

1. Russian's деепричастие is not the gerund. It looks like a match — читая, закончив, не сказав — and for one section of this page it really is one: the reduced clauses in §7 map onto it almost perfectly. While waiting is ожидая, without saying is не сказав, after finishing is закончив. That is the friendly part of the page, and Russian speakers handle it well — including the same-subject rule, because Russian enforces it too and a dangling деепричастие is an error there as well. The hostile parts are §2 and §4, where Russian has nothing at all.

2. Russian's closest thing to a gerund is a real noun. Чтение, плавание, ожидание are nouns, and they take genitive objects: чтение книг. An English gerund is a verb wearing a noun's coat — it keeps its object in the ordinary place (reading books), it takes adverbs rather than adjectives (reading quickly, not ✗ quick reading for the same meaning), and it can be negated (not reading). That is why -ing cannot be translated as a noun and back again.

3. There is no Russian preposition that takes an infinitive. Вместо takes a noun (вместо поездки) and needs a whole conjunction to take a verb (вместо того чтобы поехать). English does the same job with instead of + -ing, and the learner, having translated чтобы as to, produces ✗ instead of to go. The fix is to notice that instead of already is the чтобы; nothing more is needed.

The repair reflex#

Three steps, and they take about two seconds once they are a habit. Run them every time your hand reaches for to.

  1.  Look at the word IMMEDIATELY BEFORE the slot — not the verb you are about to write.
      That word is making the decision, not you.

  2.  Could a plain noun go in the slot?
         "...at CHESS"   "...for THE RIDE"   "...of THE FEE"      →  yes  →  -ing
         "...decided THE FEE"   "...hope THE FEE"                 →  no   →  to + verb

  3.  If the word before the slot is "to", run step 2 on the "to" itself.
         look forward to THE WEEKEND ✓  →  preposition  →  look forward to SEEING
         want to THE WEEKEND ✗          →  marker       →  want to SEE

And one habit worth building separately: whenever you learn a new verb, learn what it takes after it. Enjoy + -ing, decide + to, suggest + that. It is the same discipline as learning a Russian verb's case government — помогать кому, ждать чего — and it is the only thing that makes this page unnecessary.

✗ It's starting raining.It's starting to rain.Avoid two -ing forms in a row.
✗ I'd like going to the movies.I'd like to go to the movies.Would like takes the infinitive only.
✗ He stopped to smoke in 2011.He stopped smoking in 2011.Stop to = paused in order to; stop + -ing = quit.
✗ Can you explain me why?Can you explain to me why?Explain, describe, mention and report take to + person.

The decision, in four lines.

   preposition in front?        →  -ing        good at cooking · without saying · despite having
   one of the 81 verbs?         →  -ing        enjoy reading · avoid driving · finish painting
   fixed expression?            →  -ing        no use arguing · worth calling · go swimming
   subject is being acted on?   →  -ing        the car needs washing
   anything else                →  not here    decided to wait · remember to lock

17We look forward to hearing from you.Preposition to; the noun test settles it.
18He suggested taking the train. / He suggested that I take the train.Suggest never takes object + infinitive.
19I'm interested in learning Spanish.Adjective + preposition.
20She spent two hours cleaning the kitchen.Spend + time + -ing.
21Thank you for helping me move.For is a preposition — here meaning the reason.
22After finishing the draft, she sent the report.Dangling gerund: the report did not finish the draft. Or write it out: After the draft was finished…
23Is it worth buying the annual pass?Worth takes -ing, with no preposition and no infinitive.
24We went shopping at the outlet mall.Go + -ing takes no to — and the place preposition is at, not to.
25The rain prevented us from finishing the game.Prevent / stop / keep + object + from + -ing.
26lockingRemember + -ing = the memory of having done it. Remembered to lock would mean the task was not forgotten.
27smokingStop + -ing = quit. Stopped to smoke would mean he paused in order to have one.
28restartingTry + -ing = an experiment worth making. Try to restart would mean it may not succeed.
29to informRegret + infinitive is the formal announcement of bad news being delivered now. Regret informing would mean they are sorry they told you.
30describingGo on + -ing = continued the same thing. Went on to describe would mean she moved to a new topic.
