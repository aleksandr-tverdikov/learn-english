# Gerund only

> Every word in English after which a verb has to appear as *-ing* and can never appear as *to do* — the one rule that generates most of them, the eighty-one verbs that are simply facts, and the shorter list this one is constantly confused with.

[← All grammar topics](../README.md) &middot; [Library home](../../README.md)

English has three things it can put in the slot after a word when what goes there is another verb: the **bare infinitive** (*let him **go***, *make him **wait***), the ***to*-infinitive** (*want **to go***, *decide **to wait***), and the ***-ing* form** (*enjoy **going***, *avoid **waiting***). Nothing about the meaning of the sentence tells you which one a given word takes. *I want to leave* and *I avoid leaving* are built the same way and mean related things, and yet ✗ *I avoid to leave* is as wrong as ✗ *I want leaving*.

This page is the third slot, and only the third: **the words after which *-ing* is the only possibility.** It is the page to open when you are halfway through a sentence, your finger is on the *t* of *to*, and you want to know whether that *to* is allowed.

The organizing idea is one sentence long:

**The *-ing* form is the shape an English verb takes when it has to behave like a noun.** That is what the grammatical name *gerund* means. A gerund does every job a noun does — it is a subject (***Swimming*** *is good for you*), an object (*I enjoy* ***swimming***), a complement (*My job is* ***teaching***), and, above all, the object of a preposition (*good at* ***swimming***). Wherever the grammar has carved out a noun-shaped hole and you want to put a verb in it, the *-ing* form is what comes out.

Most of this page follows from that one fact. A smaller part of it — the verb list in [§2](#2-the-verbs-that-take-only-a-gerund) — does not follow from anything and has to be learned the way irregular verbs are learned, whole.

**How to read the page.** Five questions, in the order worth asking them:

```
  Is there a preposition in front of the slot?     →  -ing, always.       §1 §3 §4 §5 §7
  Is the word before it one of the 81 verbs?       →  -ing, always.       §2
  Is the whole thing a fixed expression?           →  -ing, always.       §6
  Is the subject the thing being acted on?         →  The car needs washing.  §8
  None of the above?                               →  not this page.      §10
```

Section [§10](#10-what-is-not-on-this-list) is the safety rail. This page lists words that take **only** the gerund; the verbs that take both forms — *remember*, *stop*, *try*, *regret*, *begin*, *like* — are not here, and treating them as if they were is the one way a page like this can make somebody's English worse instead of better.

### The form itself

Before the lists, the spelling, since half of these entries have to be written before they can be looked up.

| Rule | Examples |
|---|---|
| Add **-ing** to most verbs | work → work**ing** &middot; read → read**ing** &middot; carry → carry**ing** &middot; fix → fix**ing** |
| Drop a silent **-e**, then add *-ing* | make → mak**ing** &middot; write → writ**ing** &middot; leave → leav**ing** &middot; come → com**ing** — but *see → seeing*, *agree → agreeing* |
| One-syllable, one vowel, one final consonant → **double it** | sit → si**tt**ing &middot; run → ru**nn**ing &middot; stop → sto**pp**ing &middot; get → ge**tt**ing &middot; swim → swi**mm**ing |
| Two syllables → double only if the **stress is on the last one** | begin → begi**nn**ing &middot; forget → forge**tt**ing &middot; prefer → prefe**rr**ing — but *ópen → opening*, *vísit → visiting* |
| **-ie** → **-y** | die → d**y**ing &middot; lie → l**y**ing &middot; tie → t**y**ing |

Final *-l* is the one American–British split: Americans write *traveling*, *canceling*, *modeling* with one *l*; the British double it. Both are readable everywhere, but in American writing use one.

## 1. The rule behind most of the list: after a preposition, a verb becomes *-ing*

A preposition is a word that takes a **noun** after it. *In the car. After lunch. Without a coat. Good at math.* That is the whole definition, and it has a consequence that nobody states loudly enough:

**If you want to put a verb after a preposition, the preposition does not care. It still wants a noun, so the verb has to arrive in its noun shape — the *-ing* form.**

> I'm good at **math**. → I'm good at **cooking**. — ✗ *good at to cook*  
> He left without **his coat**. → He left without **saying** goodbye. — ✗ *without to say*  
> We talked about **the budget**. → We talked about **hiring** somebody. — ✗ *about to hire*  
> Thanks for **the ride**. → Thanks for **driving** me. — ✗ *for to drive*

There is no exception to this. Not one preposition in English takes a *to*-infinitive after it. That single fact covers [§3](#3-phrasal-verbs-and-verb--preposition), [§4](#4-adjective--preposition---ing), [§5](#5-noun--preposition---ing) and [§7](#7-prepositions-and-conjunctions-that-take--ing-directly) of this page — four of the six lists here are not separate rules at all, only four places where prepositions turn up.

### The noun test

This is the test to run when you are unsure, and it takes two seconds.

**Put a plain noun in the slot.** If a noun fits, the word in front is a preposition, and the verb must be *-ing*. If a noun does not fit, the word in front is something else and this page probably does not apply.

| Sentence | Noun in the slot | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| *She's interested in ___* | *…in **art*** ✓ | preposition → *interested in **learning*** |
| *I apologized for ___* | *…for **the delay*** ✓ | preposition → *apologized for **being** late* |
| *He insisted on ___* | *…on **a receipt*** ✓ | preposition → *insisted on **paying*** |
| *They decided ___* | ✗ *decided the receipt* | not a preposition → *decided **to pay*** |
| *I hope ___* | ✗ *hope the receipt* | not a preposition → *hope **to pay*** |

The test works because it is not a test about verbs at all. It asks a question about the word **before** the slot, which is the word that is actually making the decision.

### The *to* trap

Here is the reason this page exists at all. **English spells two completely different words *t-o*.**

One is a **preposition** — the same word as in *go to Denver*, *give it to her*, *from nine to five*. Like every preposition, it takes a noun, so a verb after it comes out as *-ing*.

The other is the **infinitive marker**, a grammatical particle with no meaning of its own that exists only to introduce the base form of a verb: *want **to** go*, *need **to** leave*, *able **to** swim*. It is not a preposition, it takes no noun, and the verb after it stays bare.

They are spelled identically, pronounced identically, and there is nothing in the sentence to tell them apart — **except the noun test.**

| Expression | Which *to*? | Noun test | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| *look forward **to*** | preposition | *look forward to **the weekend*** ✓ | *look forward to **seeing** you* |
| *want **to*** | infinitive marker | ✗ *want to the weekend* | *want **to see** you* |
| *be used **to*** | preposition | *used to **the noise*** ✓ | *used to **working** nights* |
| *used **to*** (past habit) | infinitive marker | ✗ *used to the noise* — different word | *used **to work** nights* |
| *object **to*** | preposition | *object to **the fee*** ✓ | *object to **paying** a fee* |
| *refuse **to*** | infinitive marker | ✗ *refuse to the fee* | *refuse **to pay*** |
| *be opposed **to*** | preposition | *opposed to **the plan*** ✓ | *opposed to **changing** it* |
| *be about **to*** | infinitive marker | ✗ *about to the plan* | *about **to change** it* |
| *get around **to*** | preposition | *got around to **the dishes*** ✓ | *got around to **doing** the dishes* |
| *manage **to*** | infinitive marker | ✗ *manage to the dishes* | *manage **to do** it* |

**The *to*-as-preposition inventory.** Every item below takes *-ing*, and every one of them is a place where an intelligent learner writes the infinitive. This is the single most productive error on the page.

| Group | Members |
|---|---|
| **Verbs** | *look forward to, object to, admit to, confess to, own up to, commit to, adapt to, adjust to, resort to, revert to, take to, warm to, amount to, lead to, contribute to, react to, respond to, see to, attend to, get around to, come close to, face up to, live up to, stick to, own up to* |
| **Adjectives** | *used to, accustomed to, opposed to, averse to, committed to, dedicated to, devoted to, resigned to, essential to, central to, close to, equal to, similar to, preferable to, open to, given to* |
| **Nouns** | *the key to, the secret to, the solution to, an obstacle to, a barrier to, an alternative to, an objection to, an approach to, a commitment to, an introduction to* |
| **Complex prepositions** | *in addition to, with a view to, when it comes to, prior to, subsequent to, with regard to, owing to, due to, up to* |

> I **look forward to hearing** from you. — ✗ *look forward to hear*  
> She's **committed to finishing** the degree. — ✗ *committed to finish*  
> It **amounts to admitting** we were wrong. — ✗ *amounts to admit*  
> The **key to staying** warm is layers. — ✗ *the key to stay warm*  
> **In addition to teaching**, he runs the lab. — ✗ *in addition to teach*  
> I finally **got around to calling** them back. — ✗ *got around to call*

### *Used to*: two words, one spelling

This pair is worth its own block, because both members are extremely common and they mean nothing alike.

| | Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***used to*** + base verb | *used to work* | a **past habit** that has stopped | *I **used to work** nights.* — I don't now |
| ***be / get used to*** + *-ing* | *be used to working* | **accustomed**; it no longer bothers me | *I**'m used to working** nights.* — it's normal for me |

The give-away is the verb in front. If there is a form of *be* or *get* before *used*, *to* is a preposition and *-ing* follows. If *used* stands alone as the past tense of a verb, *to* is the infinitive marker.

> He **used to smoke** a pack a day. — and then he quit  
> He**'s used to smoking** outside now. — the new rule stopped bothering him  
> It took a month to **get used to driving** on the other side.  
> ✗ *I'm used to **work** here.* → *I'm used to **working** here.* — or, for the habit sense, *I **used to work** here.*

Both sentences in that last line are correct English about entirely different situations, which is exactly why the error survives correction.

## 2. The verbs that take only a gerund

This is the part of the page that is not a rule. These verbs take an *-ing* object and refuse a *to*-infinitive, and there is no meaning, no ending and no pattern that predicts membership. *Enjoy* takes *-ing* and *like* takes both; *avoid* takes *-ing* and *refuse* takes the infinitive; *finish* takes *-ing* and *complete the job of* takes a noun. The list has to be learned.

Two things make it learnable. First, a loose semantic family runs through the middle of it: **a large share of these verbs are about stopping, avoiding, postponing, enduring, disliking, or reporting something that already exists as an event.** The gerund names a whole activity, not a step to be taken, which is why *to*-infinitives — which point forward at something not yet done — do not fit. Second, once you know the verb takes *-ing*, no further decisions follow: there is no object variant, no register variant and no American–British split, except where the table flags one.

**Reading the table.** A ◆ marks a verb whose pattern **changes when an object is inserted** — these are the ones that produce half-correct sentences and are worked out in full under the table. A ◇ marks a verb that also allows a *to*-infinitive with no real change of meaning.

### The ◆ verbs: what an object does to the pattern

Eight of the verbs above look like counterexamples the first time a learner sees *They allowed us to leave*. They are not. **Insert a person, and a different construction takes over.** The verb has two patterns, and which one you get depends entirely on whether the slot after the verb holds a person.

| Verb | No object → **gerund** | With an object → **something else** |
|---|---|---|
| *advise* | *I **advise waiting**.* | *I **advised him to wait**.* |
| *allow* | *We don't **allow smoking**.* | *They **allowed us to smoke** on the deck.* |
| *permit* | *We don't **permit parking** here.* | *The guard **permitted us to park** out back.* |
| *require* | *This **requires rewriting**.* | *The law **requires employers to post** it.* |
| *urge* | *He **urged postponing** the vote.* | *He **urged them to postpone** the vote.* |
| *forbid* | *The lease **forbids subletting**.* | *She **forbade him to sublet**.* &middot; *…**forbade him from subletting**.* |
| *ban* &middot; *prohibit* | *The rules **prohibit smoking**.* | *The rules **prohibit staff from smoking**.* — *from* + *-ing*, never *to* |
| *recommend* | *I **recommend booking** early.* | *I **recommend that you book** early.* — see below |
| *suggest* | *He **suggested leaving**.* | *He **suggested that we leave**.* — see below |

Three notes that decide whether the sentence is right or wrong:

**1. *Suggest* and *recommend* never take an object + infinitive.** This is worth memorizing as a flat prohibition, because the wrong version is fluent, confident and wrong: ✗ *He suggested me to leave*, ✗ *I recommend you to book early*. English has two ways to say it and neither uses an infinitive.

> ✗ *He **suggested me to leave** early.*  
> ✓ *He **suggested leaving** early.* — no person named  
> ✓ *He **suggested that I leave** early.* — the person goes in a *that*-clause  
> ✗ *They **recommended us to try** the pier.*  
> ✓ *They **recommended trying** the pier.* &middot; ✓ *They **recommended that we try** the pier.*

The *that*-clause takes the base form of the verb (*that I **leave***, not ✗ *that I leaves*) because it is a subjunctive; the mechanics are in [reported speech](../01-reported-speech/README.md), and the same trap is catalogued there because it is a reporting verb before it is anything else.

**2. The *from* family.** *Prevent*, *stop*, *prohibit*, *ban*, *discourage*, *deter* and *keep* all take **object + *from* + *-ing***, never an infinitive. *From* is a preposition, so [§1](#1-the-rule-behind-most-of-the-list-after-a-preposition-a-verb-becomes--ing) does the rest of the work.

> The rain **prevented us from finishing**. — ✗ *prevented us to finish*  
> Nothing is **stopping you from applying**. — ✗ *stopping you to apply*  
> They **discouraged her from reapplying**.  
> The fence **keeps the deer from getting** into the garden.

*Prevent* is the one that also works without *from* (*prevented us finishing*), which is why it is the one learners over-generalize from. The others need it.

**3. *Explain*, *describe*, *mention* and *report* take no person as a direct object at all.** These verbs are on the list above, but they carry a second, unrelated trap: you say something **to** somebody.

> ✗ *Can you **explain me** the difference?* → *Can you **explain** the difference **to me**?*  
> ✗ *She **described me** the apartment.* → *She **described** the apartment **to me**.*  
> ✗ *He **mentioned me** that he was leaving.* → *He **mentioned to me** that he was leaving.*

### Three verbs on the list that behave oddly

***Dread*** is gerund-only except in one frozen phrase: *I **dread to think** what that cost.* Nothing else survives — *dread telling*, *dread going*, *dread finding out*.

***Understand*** takes a gerund only in the sense "see why somebody would do that," and it almost always carries a possessive: *I can **understand his wanting** to leave.* In its ordinary sense it takes a *that*-clause: *I understand that you're leaving.*

***Keep*** means two different things with the same shape. *He **keeps calling*** can be "he does it repeatedly" or "he has not stopped," and the sentence rarely needs to distinguish them. What it never means is purpose — for that, English uses a different verb entirely: ✗ *He keeps to call* is not a sentence with a different meaning, it is not a sentence.

## 3. Phrasal verbs and verb + preposition

Two different structures end up in this section, and from the point of view of this page they behave identically.

- **Particle verbs**: *give **up***, *put **off***, *carry **on***, *end **up***. The second word is a particle, and what follows it is an object slot.
- **Prepositional verbs**: *insist **on***, *succeed **in***, *object **to***. The second word is a plain preposition and the verb has simply chosen it.

Either way, the slot at the end wants a noun — *give up **cigarettes***, *insist on **a receipt*** — so a verb landing there arrives as *-ing*. Nothing new is happening; [§1](#1-the-rule-behind-most-of-the-list-after-a-preposition-a-verb-becomes--ing) is doing all of the work.

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *admit to* | confess | *He **admitted to taking** the file.* |
| *adapt to* | get used to | *She **adapted to working** from home quickly.* |
| *amount to* | be equivalent to | *That **amounts to accusing** him of lying.* |
| *approve of* | think well of | *They don't **approve of my leaving** school.* |
| *apologize for* | say sorry | *He **apologized for interrupting**.* |
| *believe in* | think is right or real | *She **believes in getting** up early.* |
| *benefit from* | gain by | *You'd **benefit from taking** the course.* |
| *blame (someone) for* | hold responsible | *They **blamed me for losing** the account.* |
| *burst out* | start suddenly (only *laughing* / *crying*) | *He **burst out laughing**.* |
| *carry on* | continue | *They **carried on talking** as if nothing had happened.* |
| *commit to* | bind yourself to | *We've **committed to finishing** by Friday.* |
| *concentrate on* | give attention to | ***Concentrate on getting** it right, not fast.* |
| *confess to* | own up to | *She **confessed to sending** the email.* |
| *consist of* | be made up of | *The job **consists of answering** phones.* |
| *contribute to* | help bring about | *The rain **contributed to closing** the road.* |
| *cope with* | manage | *He's **coping with being** alone better than we expected.* |
| *count on* | rely on | *Don't **count on getting** a refund.* |
| *do away with* | abolish | *They **did away with charging** for checked bags.* |
| *dream of* &middot; *dream about* | fantasize about | *She **dreams of living** in Santa Fe.* |
| *end up* | finish by, unintentionally | *We **ended up driving** all night.* |
| *feel like* | want | *I **feel like ordering** in.* |
| *focus on* | direct attention to | ***Focus on getting** the first draft down.* |
| *get around to* | finally do | *I **got around to fixing** the gate.* |
| *get on with* | continue with | ***Get on with writing** it; the meeting is at four.* |
| *give up* | stop for good | *He **gave up trying** to explain.* |
| *go about* | set about; tackle | *How would you **go about finding** an electrician?* |
| *insist on* | demand | *She **insisted on paying** for lunch.* |
| *keep on* | continue, insistently | *He **kept on apologizing**.* |
| *lead to* | result in | *One drink **led to staying** out until two.* |
| *look forward to* | anticipate with pleasure | *I **look forward to hearing** from you.* |
| *look into* | investigate | *We're **looking into moving** the servers.* |

| *participate in* | take part in | *She **participated in organizing** the drive.* |

### Four of these need a note

***Burst out*** takes exactly two verbs: *burst out **laughing*** and *burst out **crying***. Nothing else is idiomatic — ✗ *burst out singing*, ✗ *burst out running* are not English, and the general verb for that meaning is *start*.

***Feel like*** has two lives. *I **feel like getting** a coffee* is a wish — *-ing* is required. *I **feel like I'm getting** sick* is a comparison and takes a whole clause. ✗ *I feel like to get a coffee* is the error, and it is common.

***End up*** and ***wind up*** describe an unintended destination and take *-ing* or a noun phrase: *We **ended up walking***, *We **ended up at a diner***. ✗ *ended up to walk*.

***Can't help*** belongs to [§6](#6-fixed-expressions-that-require--ing) but is worth flagging here for its one exception: *can't help* + *-ing* (*I **can't help laughing***) has a variant *can't help **but*** + **bare infinitive** (*I **can't help but laugh***). Both are standard American English; what is wrong is mixing them — ✗ *can't help but laughing*, ✗ *can't help to laugh*.

## 4. Adjective + preposition + *-ing*

Hundreds of English adjectives drag a preposition along behind them — *good **at***, *afraid **of***, *keen **on*** — and the preposition is not chosen by the writer; it is part of the adjective's dictionary entry. > She's afraid of **spiders**. → She's afraid of **flying**. — ✗ *afraid of to fly*  
> He's good at **chess**. → He's good at **remembering** names. — ✗ *good at to remember*

### The one adjective pair that goes the other way

Some adjectives take a *to*-infinitive instead, and they are the reason a learner hesitates: *happy **to help***, *ready **to go***, *able **to swim***, *likely **to rain***, *difficult **to explain***, *easy **to use***, *the first **to arrive***. These have no preposition, so [§1](#1-the-rule-behind-most-of-the-list-after-a-preposition-a-verb-becomes--ing) never fires. The test is the same as everywhere else: if there is no preposition, this page does not apply.

***Afraid*** is the pair that shows the split inside one word:

> I'm **afraid of falling**. — the fear itself, a general dread  
> I'm **afraid to look**. — too frightened to take a specific action

Both are correct, and the difference is real: *afraid of* + *-ing* names what frightens you, *afraid to* + infinitive names what the fear stops you doing.

## 5. Noun + preposition + *-ing*

Nouns take dependent prepositions too — *a reason **for***, *the risk **of***, *no point **in*** — and the consequence is the same. This is the section that catches formal writing, because these patterns are commoner in a report than in conversation.

| Noun + preposition | Example |
|---|---|
| *chance of* | *There's no **chance of getting** a table tonight.* |
| *cost of* | *The **cost of replacing** the roof came to nine thousand.* |
| *danger of* | *There's a real **danger of losing** the whole crop.* |
| *difficulty (in)* | *We had **difficulty finding** the entrance.* |
| *excuse for* | *That's no **excuse for lying** about it.* |
| *experience in* &middot; *experience of* | *She has ten years' **experience in managing** teams.* |
| *habit of* | *He's in the **habit of checking** twice.* |
| *idea of* | *I like the **idea of starting** earlier.* |
| *interest in* | *He's shown no **interest in learning** to drive.* |
| *method of* | *It's the cheapest **method of heating** a small house.* |
| *need for* | *There's no **need for shouting**.* |
| *point in* | *There's no **point in arguing** with him.* |
| *possibility of* | *We discussed the **possibility of merging** the two.* |
| *problem with* | *The **problem with driving** is the parking.* |
| *reason for* | *His **reason for resigning** was never made public.* |
| *risk of* | *You run the **risk of voiding** the warranty.* |
| *trouble with* | *The **trouble with waiting** is that prices go up.* |
| *way of* | *That's one **way of putting** it.* |

**More members**, since the pattern covers most abstract nouns: *hope of, fear of, thought of, prospect of, likelihood of, intention of, means of, question of, matter of, art of, joy of, benefit of, advantage of, disadvantage of, drawback of, purpose of, act of, result of, effect of; delay in, success in, skill in, pleasure in, confidence in; apology for, aptitude for, talent for, knack for, reputation for, responsibility for, opportunity for, technique for; issue with, experience with; the key to, an obstacle to, a barrier to, an objection to, an alternative to, an approach to, a commitment to, an introduction to.*

> There's no **hope of finishing** by Friday.  
> The **prospect of moving** again does not appeal to me.  
> She has a **knack for explaining** things simply.  
> I have no **intention of paying** twice.  
> The **key to making** it work is the temperature.

**The *in* that can disappear.** After *difficulty*, *trouble*, *a problem* and *a hard time*, the preposition is optional and Americans usually drop it. Both are correct; the bare version is commoner in speech.

> We had **difficulty (in) finding** the entrance.  
> He's having **trouble (in) getting** the door open.

**The nouns that take both.** A handful of these nouns accept a *to*-infinitive as well, and the two are not always synonyms. This is the one place in the section where a choice exists:

| Noun | + preposition + *-ing* | + *to*-infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| *chance* | *the **chance of winning*** — the probability | *a **chance to win*** — the opportunity |
| *reason* | *his **reason for going*** — the motive he had | *a **reason to go*** — a justification there is |
| *way* | *a **way of doing** it* — a method, described | *a **way to do** it* — a method, offered |
| *time* | *(spend) **time doing** it* | *the **time to do** it* — enough time |
| *need* | *no **need for shouting*** | *no **need to shout*** — commoner |

*There's no point* is the one that takes no infinitive at all: ✗ *there's no point to argue* is wrong however natural it sounds. Write *there's no point in arguing* or *there's no point arguing*.

The complete list is in **[dependent prepositions: noun patterns](../../parts-of-speech/06-prepositions/catalog/16-dependent-nouns.md)**, which also covers the trap that generates most of the errors here: the noun and the verb it comes from often take different prepositions, and sometimes the verb takes none at all — *discuss the budget* but *a discussion **about** the budget*.

## 6. Fixed expressions that require *-ing*

These are not built from parts, and the analysis behind them is not worth reconstructing: *it's no use* contains a noun, *can't help* contains a verb, *go swimming* contains nothing explicable at all. They are learned whole, and inside each one the *-ing* is not negotiable.

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *it's no use* + *-ing* | there is no point | ***It's no use arguing** with him.* |
| *it's no good* + *-ing* | same, more casual | ***It's no good pretending** you didn't hear.* |
| *there's no point (in)* + *-ing* | pointless | ***There's no point in calling** after six.* |
| *it's (not) worth* + *-ing* | justifies the cost | *It's **not worth fixing** at that price.* |
| *be worth* + *-ing* | deserves it | *The detour is **worth taking**.* |
| *can't help* + *-ing* | be unable to stop | *I **can't help laughing** when he does that.* |
| *can't stand* + *-ing* | hate | *She **can't stand being** kept waiting.* |
| *can't bear* + *-ing* | hate (stronger) | *He **can't bear watching** her struggle.* |
| *can't face* + *-ing* | lack the energy for | *I **can't face cooking** tonight.* |
| *can't resist* + *-ing* | be unable to refuse | *She **can't resist buying** shoes.* |
| *have difficulty (in)* + *-ing* | find it hard | *He **has difficulty hearing** in a crowd.* |
| *have trouble* + *-ing* | find it hard | *We **had trouble starting** the car.* |
| *have a hard time* + *-ing* | find it hard *(American)* | *I **had a hard time believing** him.* |
| *have fun* &middot; *have a good time* + *-ing* | enjoy | *They **had fun taking** the whole thing apart.* |
| *spend* + time/money + *-ing* | use it on | *I **spent three hours fixing** the printer.* |
| *waste* + time/money + *-ing* | use it badly | *Don't **waste an evening rewriting** it.* |
| *be busy* + *-ing* | be occupied with | *She's **busy grading** papers.* |
| *go* + *-ing* | do as an activity | *We **went hiking** on Saturday.* |
| *what about?* + *-ing* | suggestion | ***What about taking** the ferry?* |
| *how about?* + *-ing* | suggestion | ***How about leaving** at seven?* |
| *feel like* + *-ing* | want | *I **feel like staying** in.* |
| *there's no* + *-ing* | it is impossible to | ***There's no telling** how long it'll take.* |

> ✗ *It's no use **to argue**.* → *It's no use **arguing**.*  
> ✗ *There's no point **to wait**.* → *There's no point **(in) waiting**.*  
> ✗ *Is it worth **to buy** the annual pass?* → *Is it worth **buying** the annual pass?*  
> ✗ *I couldn't help **to laugh**.* → *I couldn't help **laughing**.* / *I couldn't help **but laugh**.*  
> ✗ *She spent the whole morning **to look** for it.* → *…**looking** for it.*  
> ✗ *How about **to go** out?* → *How about **going** out?*

### *Go* + *-ing*

English has one construction for leisure activities you go somewhere to do, and it is an *-ing* form with no preposition and no article:

> We're **going fishing** on Saturday.  
> She **goes running** before work.  
> They **went camping** in the Sawtooths.

The inventory is large and open, and nearly all of it is recreation or errands: *go swimming, shopping, fishing, hunting, hiking, camping, running, jogging, walking, climbing, skiing, snowboarding, skating, surfing, sailing, kayaking, bowling, golfing, riding, sightseeing, dancing, drinking, clubbing, birdwatching, house-hunting, job-hunting, window-shopping*.

Two errors go with it. **The first is inserting *to***: ✗ *go **to** shopping*, ✗ *go **for** swimming*. The second is the **preposition after it**: you do not *go swimming **to** the lake*, you *go swimming **in** the lake*; you do not *go shopping **to** the mall*, you *go shopping **at** the mall*. The place preposition describes where the activity happens, not where you went.

> ✗ *We went **to** fishing.* → *We **went fishing**.*  
> ✗ *She goes shopping **to** the outlet mall.* → *…shopping **at** the outlet mall.*

### *There's no* + *-ing*

A small, fixed family meaning "it is impossible to." The *-ing* form is the only one available, and the whole expression is a single unit.

> **There's no telling** what he'll say.  
> **There's no knowing** how the vote will go.  
> **There's no denying** it was a good deal.  
> **There's no getting around** the deadline.  
> **There's no pleasing** some people.  
> **There's no accounting for** taste.

✗ *There's no to tell* is not a near miss; it is not a structure. The alternative with an infinitive uses different words entirely: *It's impossible to tell.*

### *Mind*

*Mind* deserves a line of its own because it is the standard polite request, and both halves of it are *-ing*:

> **Would you mind opening** the window? — you do it  
> **Would you mind my opening** the window? — I do it; see [§9](#9-the-possessive-before-a-gerund)  
> **Do you mind waiting** a few minutes?

And the answer is a trap in both languages: *Would you mind…?* asks whether it bothers you, so **no** means yes, go ahead. Americans usually sidestep it with *Not at all* or *Go right ahead*.

### The reduced clause

*Before*, *after*, *since*, *while*, *when* and *on* are the interesting ones, because with *-ing* they are not really prepositions at all: they are **conjunctions introducing a clause with the subject and the tensed verb squeezed out of it.**

> **After he finished** the draft, he went home. — full clause  
> **After finishing** the draft, he went home. — reduced

The reduction buys compactness and costs one thing: **the subject.** Since it is no longer written, the reader recovers it from the main clause, and English enforces that rigidly.

**The same-subject requirement.** The understood subject of the *-ing* clause must be the subject of the main clause. If it is not, the sentence is a **dangling participle** — grammatically broken, and frequently funny.

> ✓ ***After finishing** the draft, **she** sent the report.* — she finished, she sent  
> ✗ ***After finishing** the draft, **the report** was sent.* — this says the report finished the draft  
> ✗ ***While driving** home, **the phone** rang.* — the phone was not driving  
> ✗ ***Before signing**, **the terms** should be read carefully.* — the terms are not signing anything

The repairs are always the same two:

> Put the real subject back into the main clause: ***After finishing** the draft, **she sent** the report.*  
> Or write the clause out in full: ***After the draft was finished**, the report was sent.*

This is one of the few errors in English that a native speaker's ear catches instantly and a learner's does not, because the learner is parsing the sentence and the native speaker is picturing it.

**Negation** goes in front of the *-ing*, not in front of the preposition:

> ***Not knowing** what else to do, I called the police.*  
> *He left **without saying** anything* — the standard way to express "and he did not say anything"

**Perfect gerunds** are available when the time gap matters, and are mostly formal: *After **having finished** the draft, she…* In most sentences the plain *-ing* already implies the earlier time, so *after finishing* is the normal choice and the perfect is reserved for cases where a contrast could be misread.

### *Rather than*, and the exception

*Rather than* is the one entry in the table that does not always take *-ing*. It takes whatever form **balances the thing it is being contrasted with.**

| Context | Form after *rather than* | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Contrasted with a **gerund** | *-ing* | ***Rather than waiting**, we walked.* |
| Contrasted with a ***to*-infinitive** | **bare** infinitive | *I decided **to walk rather than take** the bus.* |
| At the **start of the sentence** | bare infinitive or *-ing* | ***Rather than go** home, he stayed.* &middot; ***Rather than going** home, he stayed.* |
| Followed by a **noun** | a noun | *He ordered tea **rather than coffee**.* |

The rule underneath it is parallelism: whatever shape is on one side of *rather than*, put the same shape on the other. The same applies to *as well as*, which takes *-ing* after a finite verb (*He runs the shop as well as **doing** the books*) but a bare infinitive after an infinitive (*He agreed to run the shop as well as **do** the books*).

### *For* + *-ing* versus *to* + infinitive

Both express purpose and they are not interchangeable. This is a genuine choice, not a gerund-only rule, and it belongs here because the wrong choice is one of the four most common gerund errors a Russian speaker makes.

| | *for* + *-ing* | *to* + infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| What it describes | the **function** of a thing | the **purpose** of an action |
| Subject of the sentence | usually a thing | usually a person |
| Example | *A corkscrew is **for opening** bottles.* | *I went to the store **to buy** a corkscrew.* |

> This app is **for tracking** expenses. — what the app is for  
> I downloaded it **to track** my expenses. — why I acted  
> ✗ *I came here **for studying** English.* → *I came here **to study** English.*  
> ✗ *He stopped **for asking** directions.* → *He stopped **to ask** directions.*

*For* + *-ing* also appears after verbs of thanking, blaming, apologizing, praising, punishing and arresting, where it means **the reason**, not the purpose: *Thank you for **coming***, *He was fined for **parking** on the sidewalk*. That use is [§3](#3-phrasal-verbs-and-verb--preposition), not this one.

## 8. *need*, *want*, *require*, *deserve* + *-ing*: the passive pattern

A small pattern with a strange property: **the *-ing* form is active in shape and passive in meaning.**

> The car **needs washing**. = The car needs **to be washed**.  
> These shirts **need ironing**. = …need **to be ironed**.  
> The proposal **requires rewriting**. = …requires **to be rewritten**.  
> That fence **wants fixing**. = …needs **to be fixed**.  
> The whole idea **deserves looking** into. = …deserves **to be looked** into.

Nobody is washing in *needs washing*; the car is being washed. The pattern works only when **the subject of the sentence is the thing being acted on**, and it exists because English found it shorter than the passive infinitive.

| Verb | Frequency | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *need* | very common | *The lawn needs mowing.* The default member |
| *want* | common, slightly homespun | *That step wants fixing.* More British than American, but alive in American speech |
| *require* | formal | *The figures require checking.* Reports and specifications |
| *deserve* | common | *It deserves reading twice.* |
| *bear* (negative only) | fixed | *It doesn't bear thinking about.* Only in the negative |
| *could use* | informal American | *This room could use painting.* |

**The contrast that makes it useful.** *Need* has two constructions with two different subjects, and choosing the wrong one reverses who does the work:

| Sentence | Who does it |
|---|---|
| *I **need to wash** the car.* | I wash it |
| *The car **needs washing**.* | somebody washes it — unstated |
| *The car **needs to be washed**.* | same meaning, longer, and neutral in register |

All three are correct. The *-ing* version is the shortest, and it is what Americans say about chores, repairs and edits: *the gutters need cleaning*, *this paragraph needs cutting*, *the dog needs walking*.

**One regional form to recognize but not copy.** In western Pennsylvania, Ohio and parts of the Midland, people say *The car needs **washed*** — need + past participle, with the *to be* omitted entirely. It is a genuine regional feature of American English, not an error by the people who say it, and it will be read as an error everywhere else. Write *needs washing* or *needs to be washed*.

## 9. The possessive before a gerund

When a gerund has a subject of its own — somebody other than the subject of the main verb is doing it — that subject can appear in two forms:

> I don't mind **his leaving** early. — possessive, the traditional form  
> I don't mind **him leaving** early. — object form, what people say

Both are current. The difference is register, not correctness, and the rule of thumb that survives contact with real usage is:

| Situation | Preferred form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Formal or edited writing | **possessive** | *We object to **his being** named in the report.* |
| Ordinary speech and email | **object form** | *We're fine with **him being** there.* |
| The gerund phrase is the **subject** of the sentence | **possessive**, strongly | ***His leaving** surprised everyone.* |
| A proper name | either; *'s* is more formal | ***Dana's quitting*** *…* &middot; ***Dana quitting*** *…* |
| A long or plural noun phrase | **plain noun**, no *'s* | *…the chance of **the two departments merging*** |
| An inanimate subject | **plain noun**, no *'s* | *…the risk of **the roof leaking*** |

> What do you think of **my taking** the early flight? — formal  
> What do you think of **me taking** the early flight? — normal  
> ✗ *What do you think of **I taking**…* — the subject pronoun is never an option  
> **Their refusing** to sign killed the deal. — subject position; the object form here (*Them refusing*) reads as speech  
> We hadn't considered the **possibility of the bridge closing**. — never ✗ *the bridge's closing*

**The practical advice**, which is what a learner actually needs: **use the possessive with pronouns and names in anything you would call writing, and use the object form in speech.** The possessive in an email will read as careful; the object form in a legal brief will read as loose. Neither will read as foreign.

**One place the distinction is not stylistic.** After verbs of perception — *see*, *hear*, *watch*, *feel*, *notice*, *find* — the *-ing* word is a **participle**, not a gerund, and the object form is the only possibility:

> I **saw him leaving**. — I saw him; he was leaving  
> ✗ *I saw **his** leaving.* — not English

Compare *I appreciate **his** coming* (gerund; I appreciate the act) with *I saw **him** coming* (participle; I saw the person). The test: if you can replace the whole phrase with *it*, it is a gerund and the possessive is available. *I appreciate **it***. ✗ *I saw it* does not mean the same thing.

## 10. What is not on this list

Everything above is a place where *-ing* is the **only** option. This section marks the boundary, because a list of gerund-only triggers is dangerous if the reader walks away thinking the gerund is always safe. It is not. There are two other groups, and neither belongs to this page.

### Both forms, and the meaning changes

These eight verbs take a gerund **and** an infinitive, with a real difference in meaning. Getting them backwards produces a grammatical sentence that says something you did not mean, which is worse than an error — nobody corrects it.

| Verb | + *to*-infinitive | + *-ing* |
|---|---|---|
| *remember* | *I **remembered to lock** it.* — I didn't forget the task | *I **remember locking** it.* — I have the memory of doing it |
| *forget* | *I **forgot to call**.* — I never called | *I'll never **forget seeing** it.* — the memory stays |
| *stop* | *He **stopped to smoke**.* — he paused in order to | *He **stopped smoking**.* — he quit |
| *try* | *I **tried to open** it.* — I attempted, maybe failed | *I **tried opening** a window.* — as an experiment |
| *regret* | *We **regret to inform** you…* — bad news, delivered now | *I **regret telling** him.* — sorry about the past |
| *go on* | *She **went on to describe** the car.* — moved to the next thing | *She **went on describing** it.* — continued the same thing |
| *mean* | *I **meant to call** you.* — intended | *A rewrite **means starting** over.* — involves, entails |
| *need* | *I **need to wash** the car.* — I'll do it | *The car **needs washing**.* — it should be washed |

> He **stopped to talk** to a neighbor. — he was walking, and he stopped  
> He **stopped talking** to that neighbor. — they no longer speak  
> Those are the same four words in two orders, and there is no context in which one can stand in for the other.

### Both forms, and the meaning is the same

With these, the choice is free. Register leans very slightly toward the infinitive in formal prose and the gerund in speech, but both are standard and nobody will notice either.

| Verb | Both work |
|---|---|
| *begin* | *It **began to rain**.* &middot; *It **began raining**.* |
| *start* | *She **started to cry**.* &middot; *She **started crying**.* |
| *continue* | *They **continued to argue**.* &middot; *They **continued arguing**.* |
| *cease* | *It **ceased to matter**.* &middot; *It **ceased mattering**.* — formal either way |
| *bother* | *He didn't **bother to answer**.* &middot; *…**bother answering**.* |
| *attempt* | *She **attempted to explain**.* &middot; *…**attempted explaining**.* |
| *intend* | *We **intend to stay**.* &middot; *We **intend staying**.* — the infinitive is far commoner |
| *neglect* | *He **neglected to mention** it.* &middot; *…**neglected mentioning** it.* |
| *like* | *I **like to swim**.* &middot; *I **like swimming**.* |
| *love* | *She **loves to cook**.* &middot; *She **loves cooking**.* |
| *hate* | *I **hate to drive** in snow.* &middot; *I **hate driving** in snow.* |
| *prefer* | *I **prefer to walk**.* &middot; *I **prefer walking**.* |
| *can't bear* | *I **can't bear to watch**.* &middot; *I **can't bear watching**.* |

Three riders on that table:

**1. *Would like / would love / would hate / would prefer* take the infinitive only.** The moment *would* appears, the gerund is gone.

> I'**d like to go**. — ✗ *I'd like going.*  
> She'**d prefer to wait**. — ✗ *She'd prefer waiting.*  
> Compare: *I **like going** to the movies* (in general) with *I'**d like to go** to the movies* (tonight).

**2. Do not put two *-ing* forms in a row.** Even where the gerund is allowed, English avoids *It's starting raining*. Use the infinitive when the first verb is already *-ing*.

> It'**s starting to rain**. — not ✗ *starting raining*  
> They'**re beginning to understand**.

**3. After *begin* and *start*, a state verb takes the infinitive.** *I began **to understand***, *She started **to realize*** — ✗ *began understanding* is odd because you cannot be in the middle of understanding. The state verbs are listed in [the present simple](../04-tenses/01-present-simple.md).

### And the other list entirely

For completeness, the third group: verbs that take **only** a *to*-infinitive and never a gerund. They are not on this page and they are the reason the page has a boundary section at all.

*afford, agree, appear, arrange, ask, attempt, beg, care, choose, claim, consent, decide, demand, deserve, expect, fail, happen, hesitate, hope, learn, manage, offer, plan, prepare, pretend, promise, prove, refuse, seem, swear, tend, threaten, volunteer, vow, wait, want, wish*

> We **decided to wait**. — ✗ *decided waiting*  
> He **refused to sign**. — ✗ *refused signing*  
> She **managed to get** an extension. — ✗ *managed getting*  
> They **can't afford to move**. — ✗ *afford moving*

If the word in front of your slot is on **this** list, close this page.

## 11. For Russian speakers

**Russian has no gerund in the English sense, and the gap is filled by the infinitive almost everywhere.** «Я люблю читать», «Я закончил читать», «Вместо того чтобы идти» — three different English structures, one Russian form. The learner reaches for the infinitive because that is what the source language offers, and English accepts it in about half the cases, which is exactly the wrong amount: enough to keep the habit alive, not enough to be right.

Three facts about the mismatch are worth having in front of you:

**1. Russian's деепричастие is not the gerund.** It looks like a match — *читая*, *закончив*, *не сказав* — and for one section of this page it really is one: the reduced clauses in [§7](#7-prepositions-and-conjunctions-that-take--ing-directly) map onto it almost perfectly. *While waiting* is *ожидая*, *without saying* is *не сказав*, *after finishing* is *закончив*. That is the friendly part of the page, and Russian speakers handle it well — including the same-subject rule, because Russian enforces it too and a dangling деепричастие is an error there as well. The hostile parts are [§2](#2-the-verbs-that-take-only-a-gerund) and [§4](#4-adjective--preposition---ing), where Russian has nothing at all.

**2. Russian's closest thing to a gerund is a real noun.** *Чтение*, *плавание*, *ожидание* are nouns, and they take genitive objects: *чтение книг*. An English gerund is a **verb** wearing a noun's coat — it keeps its object in the ordinary place (*reading **books***), it takes adverbs rather than adjectives (*reading **quickly***, not ✗ *quick reading* for the same meaning), and it can be negated (*not reading*). That is why *-ing* cannot be translated as a noun and back again.

**3. There is no Russian preposition that takes an infinitive.** *Вместо* takes a noun (*вместо поездки*) and needs a whole conjunction to take a verb (*вместо того чтобы поехать*). English does the same job with *instead of* + *-ing*, and the learner, having translated *чтобы* as *to*, produces ✗ *instead of to go*. The fix is to notice that *instead of* already **is** the *чтобы*; nothing more is needed.

### The four errors, in order of frequency

| Russian | Correct English | The error |
|---|---|---|
| Я люблю читать / Мне нравится читать | *I **enjoy reading**.* | ✗ *I enjoy to read.* — *enjoy* is gerund-only |
| Жду с нетерпением ответа | *I **look forward to hearing** from you.* | ✗ *look forward to hear from you* — *to* is a preposition |
| Я привык работать по ночам | *I'**m used to working** nights.* | ✗ *I'm used to work nights* — that sentence means "I formerly worked nights" |
| Вместо того чтобы идти пешком | ***Instead of walking**…* | ✗ *instead of to walk* |

### The repair reflex

Three steps, and they take about two seconds once they are a habit. Run them every time your hand reaches for *to*.

```
  1.  Look at the word IMMEDIATELY BEFORE the slot — not the verb you are about to write.
      That word is making the decision, not you.

  2.  Could a plain noun go in the slot?
         "...at CHESS"   "...for THE RIDE"   "...of THE FEE"      →  yes  →  -ing
         "...decided THE FEE"   "...hope THE FEE"                 →  no   →  to + verb

  3.  If the word before the slot is "to", run step 2 on the "to" itself.
         look forward to THE WEEKEND ✓  →  preposition  →  look forward to SEEING
         want to THE WEEKEND ✗          →  marker       →  want to SEE
```

And one habit worth building separately: **whenever you learn a new verb, learn what it takes after it.** *Enjoy + -ing*, *decide + to*, *suggest + that*. It is the same discipline as learning a Russian verb's case government — *помогать кому*, *ждать чего* — and it is the only thing that makes this page unnecessary.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✗ *I **enjoy to read** before bed.* | *I **enjoy reading** before bed.* | *Enjoy* is gerund-only. |
| ✗ *We **look forward to hear** from you.* | *We **look forward to hearing** from you.* | The *to* is a preposition — *look forward to the weekend*. |
| ✗ *I'm **used to work** nights.* | *I'm **used to working** nights.* | *Be used to* = accustomed; *used to work* is a past habit. |
| ✗ ***Instead of to go**, we stayed in.* | ***Instead of going**, we stayed in.* | *Instead of* is a preposition. |
| ✗ *He **suggested me to leave**.* | *He **suggested leaving**.* / *He **suggested that I leave**.* | *Suggest* never takes object + infinitive. |
| ✗ *I **recommend you to book** early.* | *I **recommend booking** early.* / *…**that you book** early.* | Same pattern as *suggest*. |
| ✗ *She **avoided to answer**.* | *She **avoided answering**.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *They **finished to paint** the hall.* | *They **finished painting** the hall.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *He **denied to take** the money.* | *He **denied taking** the money.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *We're **considering to move**.* | *We're **considering moving**.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *I **don't mind to wait**.* | *I **don't mind waiting**.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *She **practices to speak** every day.* | *She **practices speaking** every day.* | Gerund-only verb. |
| ✗ *It's **no use to argue**.* | *It's **no use arguing**.* | Fixed expression. |
| ✗ *There's **no point to wait**.* | *There's **no point (in) waiting**.* | Fixed expression; *point* takes *in*, never an infinitive. |
| ✗ *Is it **worth to buy** the pass?* | *Is it **worth buying** the pass?* | *Worth* takes *-ing* with no preposition. |
| ✗ *I'm **interested in to learn** Spanish.* | *I'm **interested in learning** Spanish.* | Adjective + preposition. |
| ✗ *He's **good at to fix** things.* | *He's **good at fixing** things.* | Adjective + preposition. |
| ✗ *Thanks **for to help** me move.* | *Thanks **for helping** me move.* | *For* is a preposition. |
| ✗ *I came here **for studying** English.* | *I came here **to study** English.* | Purpose of an action takes the infinitive; *for* + *-ing* gives a thing's function. |
| ✗ *She **spent two hours to clean** the kitchen.* | *She **spent two hours cleaning** the kitchen.* | *Spend* + time + *-ing*. |
| ✗ *He's **busy to pack**.* | *He's **busy packing**.* | *Busy* takes a bare *-ing*. |
| ✗ *The rain **prevented us to finish**.* | *The rain **prevented us from finishing**.* | *Prevent / stop / keep* + object + *from* + *-ing*. |
| ✗ *We **went to shopping** at the mall.* | *We **went shopping** at the mall.* | *Go* + *-ing*, with no *to*. |
| ✗ *I **couldn't help but laughing**.* | *I **couldn't help laughing**.* / *…**but laugh**.* | The two constructions do not mix. |
| ✗ ***After finishing** the draft, **the report was sent**.* | ***After finishing** the draft, **she sent** the report.* | Dangling gerund — the understood subject must match. |
| ✗ *The car **needs to wash**.* | *The car **needs washing**.* / *…**to be washed**.* | The subject is the thing being washed. |
| ✗ *It's **starting raining**.* | *It's **starting to rain**.* | Avoid two *-ing* forms in a row. |
| ✗ *I'**d like going** to the movies.* | *I'**d like to go** to the movies.* | *Would like* takes the infinitive only. |
| ✗ *He **stopped to smoke** in 2011.* | *He **stopped smoking** in 2011.* | *Stop to* = paused in order to; *stop* + *-ing* = quit. |
| ✗ *Can you **explain me** why?* | *Can you **explain to me** why?* | *Explain*, *describe*, *mention* and *report* take *to* + person. |

17. ✗ *We look forward to hear from you.*
18. ✗ *He suggested me to take the train.*
19. ✗ *I'm interested in to learn Spanish.*
20. ✗ *She spent two hours to clean the kitchen.*
21. ✗ *Thank you for to help me move.*
22. ✗ *After finishing the draft, the report was sent.*
23. ✗ *Is it worth to buy the annual pass?*
24. ✗ *We went to shopping at the outlet mall.*
25. ✗ *The rain prevented us to finish the game.*

**C. Choose the form, and be ready to say what the other one would have meant.**

26. I remembered (to lock / locking) the door — which is why I was surprised to find it open.
27. He stopped (to smoke / smoking) in 2011, after thirty years.
28. Try (to restart / restarting) the router — that usually fixes it.
29. We regret (to inform / informing) you that the position has been filled.
30. She went on (to describe / describing) the accident for another ten minutes.

### Answers

| # | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *looking forward to **meeting*** | *To* is a preposition here — *look forward to the weekend*. |
| 2 | *avoided **answering*** | *Avoid* is gerund-only. |
| 3 | *decided **to wait*** | *Decide* is infinitive-only; not on this page. |
| 4 | *used to **getting*** | *Be used to* = accustomed, so *to* is a preposition. |
| 5 | *used to **live*** | The past-habit *used to*, where *to* is the infinitive marker. |
| 6 | *no point in **arguing*** | Fixed expression; *in* is a preposition. |
| 7 | *mind **opening*** | *Mind* is gerund-only. |
| 8 | *need **cleaning*** | Passive pattern — the gutters are the thing being cleaned. *Need to be cleaned* is equally correct. |
| 9 | *insisted on **paying*** | Verb + preposition. |
| 10 | *can't help **laughing*** | Fixed expression. *Can't help but laugh* is the other standard version. |
| 11 | *instead of **flying*** | *Instead of* is a preposition. |
| 12 | *suggested **leaving*** | *Suggest* is gerund-only, and never takes object + infinitive. |
| 13 | *worth **calling*** | *Worth* takes a bare *-ing*. |
| 14 | *busy **packing*** | *Busy* takes a bare *-ing*. |
| 15 | *After **finishing*** | A reduced clause; the subject *she* is recovered from the main clause. |
| 16 | *I **enjoy reading** before bed.* | *Enjoy* is gerund-only — the single most common error on this page. |
| 17 | *We **look forward to hearing** from you.* | Preposition *to*; the noun test settles it. |
| 18 | *He **suggested taking** the train.* / *He **suggested that I take** the train.* | *Suggest* never takes object + infinitive. |
| 19 | *I'm **interested in learning** Spanish.* | Adjective + preposition. |
| 20 | *She **spent two hours cleaning** the kitchen.* | *Spend* + time + *-ing*. |
| 21 | *Thank you **for helping** me move.* | *For* is a preposition — here meaning the reason. |
| 22 | ***After finishing** the draft, **she sent** the report.* | Dangling gerund: the report did not finish the draft. Or write it out: *After the draft was finished…* |
| 23 | *Is it **worth buying** the annual pass?* | *Worth* takes *-ing*, with no preposition and no infinitive. |
| 24 | *We **went shopping** at the outlet mall.* | *Go* + *-ing* takes no *to* — and the place preposition is *at*, not *to*. |
| 25 | *The rain **prevented us from finishing** the game.* | *Prevent / stop / keep* + object + *from* + *-ing*. |
| 26 | ***locking*** | *Remember* + *-ing* = the memory of having done it. *Remembered to lock* would mean the task was not forgotten. |
| 27 | ***smoking*** | *Stop* + *-ing* = quit. *Stopped to smoke* would mean he paused in order to have one. |
| 28 | ***restarting*** | *Try* + *-ing* = an experiment worth making. *Try to restart* would mean it may not succeed. |
| 29 | ***to inform*** | *Regret* + infinitive is the formal announcement of bad news being delivered now. *Regret informing* would mean they are sorry they told you. |
| 30 | ***describing*** | *Go on* + *-ing* = continued the same thing. *Went on to describe* would mean she moved to a new topic. |
