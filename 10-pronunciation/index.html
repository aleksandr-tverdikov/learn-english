00 How to use this page

This page describes General American (GA) — the accent used in national broadcasting and treated as the reference for American English teaching. It is not the only American accent; regional varieties differ, and the notes point out where.

The symbols are IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet). Slashes mark phonemes — meaningful sound units: /kæt/. Square brackets mark actual pronunciation detail: [ˈwɑɾɚ] for water. A raised ˈ goes before the stressed syllable: /əˈbaʊt/.
Audio uses your browser's built-in text-to-speech, so nothing is downloaded. Pick a US English voice above for the most accurate model. Synthetic speech is a good guide but not a perfect one — trust a native speaker over the slider.

01 Consonants

Twenty-four consonant phonemes. Most pair up as voiceless/voiced with the same mouth position — the only difference is whether the vocal cords vibrate. Put a finger on your throat and say sss then zzz: same tongue, different buzz.

02 Simple vowels

Vowels are where accents live, and where learners lose the most intelligibility. English spelling hides them badly — the letter a alone writes six different vowels. Learn each vowel by its keyword, not by its letter.

Length marks. Some dictionaries write /iː/ /uː/ /ɔː/ /ɑː/ with a colon for length. American dictionaries usually drop it, because in GA the quality of the vowel does the work, not its duration. This page writes them without the colon.

03 Diphthongs

A diphthong is one vowel that glides into another inside a single syllable. Your tongue must actually move — holding a steady vowel is the single most common giveaway of a non-native accent in go and day.

04 R-colored vowels

This is the most distinctly American part of the sound system. In GA the r is pronounced everywhere it is written, and it fuses with the vowel before it rather than following as a separate sound. British RP drops all of these r's.

How to make it. Either curl the tongue tip up and back (retroflex) or bunch the middle of the tongue up toward the roof of the mouth (bunched). Both produce an identical sound and both are used by native speakers — use whichever you can hold comfortably. Crucially, the lips round slightly and the tongue tip never touches anything.

Eleven features that separate General American from British RP and from careful "textbook" English. Getting these right does more for an American accent than perfecting individual vowels.

06 Stress

English is a stress-timed language. Stressed syllables are longer, louder, higher in pitch, and have full vowels; everything else compresses toward schwa. Put the stress on the wrong syllable and a word can become unrecognizable even when every sound is correct.

Noun / verb pairs

Same spelling, stress moves. Nouns take first-syllable stress, verbs take second.

Compound nouns vs. adjective + noun

A compound noun stresses the first element; a descriptive phrase stresses the second. This is a meaning difference, not a style choice.

07 Reduction, weak forms, and linking

Native speakers do not pronounce every word fully. Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words) keep their full vowels; function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) collapse to schwa. Learning to hear weak forms matters even more than producing them.

Casual contractions

Standard in speech at every level of formality, and normal in informal writing. Avoid writing them in academic or business prose.

08 The -ed and -s endings

Two spellings, three pronunciations each, and the choice is fully automatic — it depends only on the sound immediately before the ending. Learn the rule once and every regular verb and plural is solved.

Plural, possessive, and third-person -s

Watch out. A handful of adjectives keep the old /ɪd/ where the verb does not: a learned professor /ˈlɝnɪd/ but I learned it /lɝnd/. Also aged, blessed, beloved, wicked, naked, rugged, crooked.
