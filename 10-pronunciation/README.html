Pronunciation#

The complete sound system of General American English — 24 consonants, 15 vowels and diphthongs, and 7 r-colored vowels.

What "General American" means#

GA is the accent used in national broadcasting and taken as the reference for teaching American English. It is not the only American accent — the notes flag where regional varieties differ, particularly on the cot–caught merger and the Mary–marry–merry merger.

Symbols are IPA. Slashes mark phonemes: /kæt/. Square brackets mark phonetic detail: [ˈwɑɾɚ]. A raised ˈ precedes the stressed syllable: /əˈbaʊt/.

How to make the r
either curl the tongue tip up and back, or bunch the middle of the tongue toward the roof of the mouth. Both are used by native speakers and sound identical — use whichever you can hold. The lips round slightly and the tongue tip never touches anything.

What makes it sound American#

  1. Rhoticity — every written r is pronounced: car, card, farther, worker.
  2. T-flapping — /t/ between vowels before an unstressed syllable becomes a fast tap [ɾ] that sounds like d: water, better, city, thirty. Also across words: get it, a lot of. Because /d/ flaps too, latter and ladder merge.
  3. Cot–caught merger — most Americans no longer distinguish /ɑ/ from /ɔ/, so cot/caught, Don/dawn, stock/stalk are homophones. The distinction survives in parts of the Northeast, the South, and around the Great Lakes.
  4. Yod-dropping — no /j/ after t, d, n, s, l: new /nu/, tune, duty, student. Kept elsewhere: music, few, cute, human.
  5. BATH takes /æ/ — bath, ask, dance, chance, can't, after, class, half, where RP takes /ɑː/.
  6. Dark L everywhere — the back of the tongue lifts, giving a hollow quality: feel, milk, call, help.
  7. Glottal stop before syllabic n — button is [ˈbʌʔn̩], mountain, kitten, important.
  8. T-deletion after n — casually, winter sounds like winner: internet, twenty, center. Informal.
  9. Mary–marry–merry merger — all three are /ˈmɛri/ for most Americans; kept distinct around New York and Philadelphia.
  10. Different word stress — address, garage, ballet, adult, cigarette, magazine, laboratory.
  11. No syllable dropping in -ary, -ery, -ory — secretary, necessary, territory, military, cemetery keep the full syllable.

The -ed and -s endings#

Both are fully automatic — the choice depends only on the sound immediately before the ending.

-edAfterExamples
/t/a voiceless sound (p, k, f, s, ʃ, tʃ, θ)stopped, walked, laughed, washed
/d/a voiced sound, including all vowelsplayed, called, lived, cleaned
/ɪd//t/ or /d/ only — adds a syllablewanted, needed, started, decided
-sAfterExamples
/s/a voiceless sound (p, t, k, f, θ)cats, books, stops, laughs
/z/a voiced sound, including all vowelsdogs, plays, runs, trees
/ɪz/a sibilant (s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ) — adds a syllablebuses, boxes, watches, judges

A few adjectives keep the old /ɪd/ where the verb does not: a learned professor /ˈlɝnɪd/ but I learned it /lɝnd/. Also aged, blessed, beloved, wicked, naked, rugged, crooked.

Stress#

English is stress-timed: stressed syllables are longer, louder, and higher in pitch, and everything else compresses toward schwa.

  • Noun/verb pairs — nouns stress the first syllable, verbs the second: a RECord / to reCORD, a PRESent / to preSENT, an OBject / to obJECT.
  • Compound noun vs. phrase — a GREENhouse (for plants) vs. a green HOUSE (painted green). This is a meaning difference, not a style choice.
  • Suffixes that pull stress onto the preceding syllable — -tion, -sion, -ity, -ic, -graphy, -logy: inforMAtion, aBILity, speCIFic, phoTOGraphy.
  • Suffixes that take the stress themselves — -ee, -eer, -ese, -ette: employEE, enginEER, JapanESE.
  • Neutral suffixes — -able, -ous, -al, -ly, -ment, -ness, -ful leave the root's stress alone.

Reduction and connected speech#

Content words keep full vowels; function words collapse to schwa. Hearing weak forms matters more than producing them.

Linking — final consonants attach to following vowels (an apple, turn it off); glides bridge vowel-to-vowel gaps (go on, I am); doubled consonants are held once (bus stop, big game); sounds assimilate (did you → /ˈdɪdʒə/); and consonants elide in clusters (next day, friendship).

Casual contractions are standard in speech at every level of formality: gonna, wanna, gotta, hafta, oughta, kinda, lemme, gimme, whatcha, didja, a lotta.
