American English Reference#

A reference library covering every word class in English — one folder per class — plus a complete pronunciation guide.

Traditional grammar counts eight parts of speech: nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections. Modern descriptive grammars split determiners out of the adjective class, because the, my, some, and three do not describe a noun — they anchor it, and they follow their own ordering rules. That gives nine classes, and this library has nine folders.

The nine word-class folders live under parts-of-speech/; the pronunciation guide sits alongside them, since how English sounds is not a word class.

Each class folder is a self-contained README with the same ten sections: what the class does, its types, forms and inflection, position and word order, rules and patterns, common mistakes, American English notes, tricky cases, a quick reference, and practice. The five closed classes — pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, interjections, determiners — additionally have a catalog/ holding a complete dictionary of that class. Verbs and nouns are open classes, but each has a catalog covering the finite part that has to be memorized: the irregular verbs, and the irregular plurals and uncountables.

The Noun Dictionary#

01-nouns/catalog/ — 9,004 nouns in 92 groups, built in two tiers, because nouns are an open class and the two halves of the problem are different.

The grammar tier (groups 1–23, 694 entries) covers the nouns that misbehave — the part a learner has to memorize because no rule predicts it. Each gets the full treatment: numbered senses, contrast notes, a dozen examples. Three systems, grouped by what goes wrong:

  • Irregular plurals (groups 1–12), organized by how the form changes rather than alphabetically — the seven vowel-change plurals (foot/feet), the -en survivors (child/children), the zero plurals (sheep, deer, series), -f → -ves with its traps (thief/thieves but chief/chiefs), the -o rule, and the borrowed Latin, Greek, Italian, Hebrew, and French plurals
  • Countability (groups 13–16) — the uncountables that break learners (✗ an information, ✗ two advices), the nouns that are both with different meanings (a hair vs hair), the plural-only nouns (scissors, jeans), and the -s nouns that take a singular verb (the news is, physics is)
  • Agreement and counting (groups 17–20) — collective nouns and the American/British split (the team is vs the team are), compound plurals (mothers-in-law), the partitives that let you count the uncountable (a piece of advice, a pinch of salt), and nationality nouns (two Japanese, but two Spaniards)
  • Naming and marking (groups 21–23) — the collective nouns for animals (a pride of lions, a murder of crows), the gender pairs and which of them are dying (actor has swallowed actress; firefighter has replaced fireman), and proper nouns and brand names, where the capital letter is the whole grammar (aspirin lost its trademark; Kleenex has not)

The core-vocabulary tier (groups 24–108, 8,310 entries) covers the nouns you simply need to know: frequent, regular, countable words organized by semantic field — the body, health, food, the house, clothing, tools, materials, landscape, weather, animals, money, work, time, measurement, law, government, science, technology, transport, travel, war, religion, art, education, and the rest. A regular noun like doctor has no grammatical story, so these entries are deliberately lean: pronunciation, Russian, plural, countability, a one-line gloss, three examples. A Contrast line appears only where the word hides a real trap — plumber has a silent b, rapids is always plural, Americans play sports where the British play sport.

A word never appears in both tiers: if it misbehaves, the full entry wins and the lean one is dropped.

Every entry carries General American pronunciation for both forms, since the plural is often where the sound changes: woman /ˈwʊmən/ → women /ˈwɪmɪn/, analysis → analyses /-siːz/, corpus → corpora. Traps are called out explicitly — ✗ octopi, ✗ rooves, ✗ criterias, ✗ a phenomena, ✗ mongeese.

Irregular & Phrasal Verbs#

03-verbs/catalog/ — 366 irregular verbs, 696 phrasal verbs built on them, and 229 verb + preposition patterns, built from a Russian-authored spreadsheet and grouped by how the three forms change rather than alphabetically: identical forms, the i—a—u shift (sing/sang/sung), past = participle with one vowel change, -d becoming -t, participle = base + -n, and so on. Learning the pattern beats memorizing 366 rows.

  • Forms — all three with General American pronunciation. The source spreadsheet's IPA was British RP and missing the participle for 230 verbs, so it was retranscribed
  • Русский — the translation from the original spreadsheet, kept verbatim
  • Separable — on every phrasal verb: whether the object can split it (turn it off, never ✗ turn off it) or not (look after it, never ✗ look it after)
  • Examples — five or more per verb, and between them they use the base, the past, and the participle with have/has/had, because that is the form learners get wrong

Traps are called out where they exist: ✗ I begun, ✗ I have went, ✗ I seen it; lie/lay/lain vs lay/laid/laid; hang/hung (objects) vs hang/hanged (people); read spelled the same but /rɛd/ in the past; and the American/British splits — gotten, dreamed, learned, proved.

The verb + preposition patterns (depend on, abide by, account for) moved here from the preposition catalog — they are verbs, and this is where you look them up.

The Pronoun Dictionary#

02-pronouns/catalog/ — 512 pronouns in 10 categories: personal, possessive, reflexive and reciprocal, demonstrative, relative, interrogative, indefinite, quantifying, fused and generic, and the archaic and dialectal forms. The class where case errors are most visible, in both directions — ✗ Me and him went and the hypercorrection ✗ between you and I.

Three fields specific to this class:

  • Case — subject, object, possessive, or reflexive, with the deletion test for coordinated phrases (He gave it to Bob and me — try it without Bob)
  • Refers to — a person, a thing, or a whole preceding clause. This is what separates who from which
  • Agreement — the verb form it forces: everyone is, neither is, both are

It is also honest about usage rather than teaching zombie rules: It's me is standard, whom survives mainly after a preposition, and singular they is standard American English including in edited prose.

The Determiner Dictionary#

09-determiners/catalog/ — 415 determiners in 9 categories: articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, numerals, and the pre- and postdeterminers. The tightest closed class in English, and the one where a single wrong choice is instantly ungrammatical — much books and many information are wrong not because they are unclear but because determiner and noun type do not match.

Three fields specific to this class:

  • Goes with — exactly which noun types it may and may not precede. The line that stops much books
  • Position — central, predeterminer, or postdeterminer. English allows only one central determiner per noun phrase, which is why the my book is impossible
  • With of — optional in all (of) the students, required in each of the students and before any pronoun, impossible for every

How word class is decided#

A word does not own a class. Class is a job, not a label — it is decided by what the word is doing in a particular sentence: what it modifies, what it can be replaced by, what inflections it takes, and where it can sit. Most dictionaries list several classes for the same word, and the sentence picks one.

  • book — I finished the book (noun) / Please book a table (verb, taking tense: booked) / a book club (noun acting as a modifier, an attributive noun)
  • fast — a fast car (adjective, modifying a noun) / He drives fast (adverb, modifying a verb) / They fast until sundown (verb) / break your fast (noun)
  • that — That book is mine (determiner) / That is mine (demonstrative pronoun) / the car that broke down (relative pronoun) / She said that she would come (complementizer, a subordinating conjunction)

The practical test: substitute, don't translate. If you can swap in the or my, the slot is a determiner slot. If it can replace the whole chunk, that chunk is a noun phrase. If very can go in front, you probably have a gradable adjective or adverb.

Suggested reading order#

The numbered folder order is the traditional one and is fine for browsing. For reading straight through, this order builds each class on the one before it:

  1. Nouns — the anchor class. Almost every other class is defined by its relationship to nouns.
  2. Determiners — read immediately after nouns, because most singular countable nouns require one. Nouns and determiners together give you the noun phrase.
  3. Pronouns — pronouns replace whole noun phrases, so they make sense only once you can recognize one.
  4. Verbs — the largest chapter. With noun phrases available as subjects and objects, verb types (transitive, ditransitive, linking) become easy to see.
  5. Adjectives — the modifiers inside the noun phrase, plus the ones that follow a linking verb.
  6. Adverbs — best read right after adjectives, since the two are constantly confused and the contrast is the fastest way to learn both.
  7. Prepositions — prepositions take a noun phrase as their object, so this needs chapters 1–3 in place.
  8. Conjunctions — joins finished clauses, so it comes after you can build one.
  9. Interjections — the detached class, grammatically independent of everything above. Read last, or any time.

American vs. British English#

The nine classes themselves are identical in both varieties. No word class exists in one and not the other, and the category boundaries are the same. The real differences are narrower:

  • Spelling — color / colour, center / centre, defense / defence, traveled / travelled.
  • Vocabulary — apartment / flat, truck / lorry, elevator / lift.
  • A few preferred forms — American gotten as a past participle, learned over learnt, and singular agreement with collective nouns (The team is playing well, where British English also allows are).
  • Some preposition and article choices — on the weekend vs. at the weekend, in the hospital vs. in hospital, write me vs. write to me.
  • Pronunciation — the largest difference of all, and the one that has nothing to do with grammar. See What makes it sound American.

Every folder carries a dedicated American English notes section covering these points for that class. This library documents American usage as the default and flags the British alternative where it matters.
